AA Car Check

Rates and terms checked 21 September 2026 · HPI Checks · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

A full AA car check costs £14.99 for a single registration, or £29.99 for a Multicheck covering five vehicles — £6.00 each. Both prices were taken on 21 September 2026 from theaacarcheck.com and from the AA's own vehicle-check page, which quote the same figures. The report is delivered on screen the moment you pay, with a copy emailed to you.

For that money you get the searches a private buyer actually worries about: outstanding finance, a stolen marker on the Police National Computer, an insurance total loss, a mileage discrepancy, plate and colour changes, the number of previous keepers, a DVLA scrapped marker and a V5C and VIN match, plus what the site calls "more than 72 other pieces of information". Behind it sits a register of over 7 million current finance agreements. The AA attaches a guarantee of up to £30,000, running for two years from the date of the check.

The badge is the AA's; the report is not. Every page of the service carries the line "Powered by HPI", and the small print is blunter than that: the contract for the check itself is with HPI Limited, company number 4068979, of 8A Platform, New Station Street, Leeds LS1 4JB. The website it sits on belongs to Automobile Association Developments Limited (company 1878835, Fanum House, Basing View, Basingstoke RG21 4EA), and the trade marks belong to AA Brand Management Limited. The customer line, 0113 222 2012, is a Leeds number. None of that costs you anything — HPI compiles the register the rest of the trade licenses, and as the next section shows, the AA badge is the cheaper way to buy it in bulk — but it decides who you are arguing with if the report turns out to be wrong.

£14.99 once, £6.00 five times — and HPI charges more for less

One registration is £14.99. Five are £29.99, which works out at £6.00 apiece, and the AA prints that unit price on the buying page rather than leaving you to divide. Anybody checking two or three cars on a weekend of viewings is past the break-even before the second report: two singles cost £29.98, a penny under the bundle that covers five.

Set that against the company actually writing the reports. HPI sells the identical full check under its own brand at £19.99, and its MultiCheck bundle is £29.99 for three — £10.00 each. The AA's £29.99 buys five. Same data, same register, same Leeds call centre, and the licensee's bundle is £4.00 a check cheaper than the compiler's. HPI also sells a cut-down Basic Check at £9.99 that leaves out the finance search; the AA does not offer a stripped version at all, which is the more honest range of the two, because the finance answer is the one people are paying for.

There is no members' discount. The AA charges its 3.3 million paid personal members the same £14.99 it charges anyone else, which is worth knowing before you go looking for the member price that exists on its inspections and breakdown products.

Nine registers, one report

The sample report names what gets searched, and it is more than the marketing bullet points suggest. The finance answer comes from agreements lenders have registered with HPI — over 7 million live at any one time. Stolen markers come from the Police National Computer. Total-loss and theft claims come from insurers' own records. Mileage readings come from the National Mileage Register, described in the report as holding over 200 million recorded mileages. On top of those sit a manufacturer recall register, a security watch register for vehicles flagged as high-risk, a plate transfer register, DVLA registration data and manufacturer specification data.

That last group is why a report runs to dozens of fields that have nothing to do with risk. The sample prints torque of 420.7 lb-ft, 425.1 BHP, 0-60 mph in 5.0s, a 165 mph maximum, combined fuel economy of 20.2 mpg and an estimated fuel cost of £2,729 over 12,000 miles. It is padding, but it is accurate padding, and it lets you check the V5C describes the car in front of you.

The searches that genuinely cannot be had for nothing are four: finance, stolen markers, insurance write-offs, and mileage readings taken from outside the MOT record. Everything else on the report — tax status, MOT expiry, first registration date, engine size, fuel type, CO2 — the DVLA publishes free.

What the £30,000 promise is actually attached to

The guarantee has two ceilings, not one. The terms set "the maximum limit of indemnity under the HPI Guarantee in respect of a failure to acquire good title" at £30,000, and they attach a condition to it: you must have supplied the VIN, the registration mark, and the serial number and issue date from the V5C. Run the check on the registration alone and you have not met it. The second ceiling, £15,000, applies to a different loss — a fall in the car's market value because it had been written off or declared an insurance total loss and that was not recorded on the register when you searched.

Both run for two years from the date of the check, not from the date you bought the car. The exclusions then take out a long list: vehicles carrying a stolen or security watch alert, cars with a recorded mileage discrepancy, anything bought more than 30% below retail market value, "Q" prefix and suffix registrations, cars bought without a valid MOT where one was required, vehicles from salvage auctions, cloned vehicles, vehicles previously registered outside mainland Britain, and any purchase by a motor trader or anyone buying to sell on at a profit.

Read as a group, those exclusions describe the bargain-hunting, paperwork-light purchase where something is most likely to be wrong. A buyer paying close to book price for an MOT'd car with a clean V5C is well covered. A buyer tempted by something 35% under the odds is not covered at all, and that is the buyer reaching for a £14.99 check.

The sample report on sale in 2026 was run in January 2017

The specimen report the AA publishes to show you what you are buying carries a check date of 11/01/2017 and a tax expiry of 01 February 2017. Its subject is a 2007 Chrysler 300C SRT-8 with an estimated market value of £7,590, and its mileage table stops in 2013.

Two details give the age away as more than cosmetic. The write-off flagged on it is "Category C", dated 11/02/2015. Category C no longer exists: the Association of British Insurers replaced categories A, B, C and D with A (scrap), B (break), S (structurally damaged repairable) and N (non-structurally damaged repairable) on 1 October 2017, nine months after that report was run. A buyer who studies the specimen to learn what a bad result looks like is learning a vocabulary the industry retired almost a decade ago.

The second is the price. The sample's payment line reads "HPI Check: 19.99", discounted to zero — HPI's price, on the AA's website, £5.00 above what the AA charges. It is a small thing and it is not a lie, but a nine-year-old specimen with an obsolete damage category and a competitor's price on it is the clearest evidence on the site of how thin the AA layer over this product really is.

Seven-year-old statistics, and what the current ones say

The numbers on the AA's sales page are sourced, dated and old. The footnote credits "HPI Ltd 2019 data" for all of them: one in three cars checked carries some hidden history, one in three is still on finance, one in four has had a plate change, 1,815 cars a day show a mileage discrepancy, 2,205 a day turn out to be insurance write-offs, and 109 a day are identified as currently stolen. Crediting the source and the year is better practice than most of the trade manages. Leaving 2019 figures on a 2026 page is still asking a reader to act on evidence collected before the pandemic changed both used-car prices and vehicle crime.

Annualised, the daily figures claim roughly 662,000 mileage discrepancies, 805,000 write-offs and 39,800 stolen identifications a year. Against that, the Office for National Statistics recorded 312,250 vehicle offences in England and Wales in the year ending March 2026, published on 23 July 2026 and down 11% on the year before, inside 1.6 million recorded thefts of all kinds. The two counts measure different things — a register hit is not a police report — but a falling crime figure sitting beside a static seven-year-old marketing statistic is the sort of gap a buyer should price in.

Where the write-off answer comes from, and what changed in November 2025

No insurer tells HPI directly that it has written a car off. The industry files those claims into a shared register that used to be called the Motor Insurance Anti-Fraud and Theft Register, and from 24 November 2025 it has been accessed through the Motor Insurers' Bureau's Vehicle Salvage and Theft Data service instead — a renaming rather than a new database, but the label on every older guide is now out of date. The MIB describes its contents as records of claims relating to written-off and stolen vehicles, defined by the ABI's Salvage Code of Practice.

Two things follow for anyone reading a clean report. The categories are the ABI's, and the code setting them is a voluntary industry framework, not law — so the letter attached to a damaged car depends on an engineer applying a code his employer has agreed to follow. And the entry only exists if an insurer was involved and filed it. A car repaired privately after a serious shunt, or bought back by its owner before a claim was settled, leaves nothing for any check to find. A blank field means nothing has been reported. It does not mean nothing happened.

Most of this is free on the AA's own used-car site

AA Cars, the used-car marketplace carrying the same badge, runs a history check at no charge on the vehicles listed there. Its free check covers whether the car is recorded as currently stolen, whether an insurance write-off is recorded, a current third-party trace, plate changes, colour changes, mileage data, whether the car is an import, and the number of keepers. The page says so plainly, and adds that the check "won't tell you if any outstanding finance is present on the vehicle".

Compare that with the £14.99 list and the paid extra shrinks to one answer — finance — plus the guarantee, the recall and security-watch searches, and the specification padding. Finance is the answer worth paying for on a private sale, because it is the one that can cost you the car. But a buyer on the AA's own forecourt listings is being offered free what the AA's other site charges £14.99 for, minus the single field, and neither page mentions the other.

The free government services fill in more of the gap. GOV.UK's MOT history service gives every test result since 2005, including the mileage recorded at each test, failure items and advisories, which is a rough clocking check in itself; its vehicle enquiry service gives tax status and expiry, SORN, MOT expiry, date of first registration, last V5C issue date, year of manufacture, engine size, fuel type and CO2.

No regulator, and a dispute link pointing at a platform that shut in 2025

A vehicle data check is not a regulated financial service. There is no FCA permission behind it, no Financial Ombudsman Service to escalate to, and no ratings agency — Defaqto, Fairer Finance or anyone else — that scores car checks. The AA is a regulated firm for its insurance business; none of that regulation touches this £14.99 purchase.

What the terms offer instead has expired. They say: "By law, we are required to provide a link from our website to the EU Online Dispute Resolution Platform. We are not obliged to use ADR should you have a complaint with us." That platform ceased to operate on 20 July 2025, and the obligation it refers to stopped applying to UK traders years before that. The sentence that follows it — that they are not obliged to use alternative dispute resolution — remains entirely true. The cancellation clause is dated in the same way, ending your right to cancel "under The Consumer Protection Distance Selling Regulations 2000", a set of rules revoked on 13 June 2014.

The protection that does apply is statutory and worth knowing, because nothing on the site mentions it. A paid report is digital content under the Consumer Rights Act 2015: section 34 requires satisfactory quality, section 35 fitness for a purpose you made known, section 36 that it matches its description. Sections 42 to 44 give you repair or replacement first and a price reduction after that, section 46 covers damage caused to your own device, and section 47 stops a trader contracting out of any of it. On a £14.99 report a price reduction is £14.99 — which is precisely why the £30,000 guarantee, with all its exclusions, is the part of this product that matters.

Where it wins

  • Only one paid tier, and it includes the outstanding finance search rather than holding it back for an upsell
  • Guarantee of up to £30,000 on title for two years, with the conditions and exclusions published in full rather than summarised
  • Nine named registers, including the Police National Computer, a manufacturer recall search and a security watch register
  • Report delivered instantly and emailed, with a phone line answered Monday to Friday 8am to 6pm
  • The sales-page statistics are credited to a named source with a year attached, which most of the trade does not bother with

  • That same specimen prints a price of £19.99, which is HPI's price rather than the AA's £14.99
  • Every statistic on the sales page is credited to 2019 data and presented without qualification in 2026
  • The £30,000 ceiling only applies if you supplied the VIN, the registration mark and the V5C serial number and issue date; a registration-only check does not reach it
  • The guarantee excludes cars bought more than 30% below retail value, cars without a valid MOT, Q-registrations, cloned vehicles, salvage auction purchases, vehicles previously registered outside mainland Britain and anyone buying to sell at a profit
  • The terms still direct complaints to the EU Online Dispute Resolution Platform, which ceased operating on 20 July 2025, and still cite the Consumer Protection (Distance Selling) Regulations 2000, revoked on 13 June 2014
  • The AA's own used-car site gives away the stolen, write-off, plate change, colour change, mileage, import and keeper searches for nothing, and neither page acknowledges the other

Common questions

How much is an AA car check?

£14.99 for one full check. The Multicheck is £29.99 and covers five vehicles, which is £6.00 each. There is no cheaper cut-down tier, and paid AA members pay the same price as everyone else.

Is the AA check the same as an HPI check?

Yes. The service is marked "Powered by HPI", and the terms name HPI Limited of Leeds as the company you contract with. The AA sells it for £14.99 against HPI's own £19.99, and five checks for £29.99 against HPI's three for the same money.

What does the £30,000 guarantee actually cover?

A failure to acquire good title — broadly, undisclosed finance — up to £30,000, and only where you supplied the VIN, registration mark and the V5C serial number and issue date. A missed insurance total loss is treated as a fall in value and capped at £15,000. Both last two years from the date of the check, and neither applies to trade buyers, cars bought more than 30% below retail value, Q-plated or cloned vehicles, salvage auction purchases or cars without a valid MOT where one was required.

Can I get any of this without paying?

A good deal of it. AA Cars runs a free history check covering stolen markers, recorded write-offs, plate and colour changes, mileage data, imports and keeper numbers, but not finance. GOV.UK gives every MOT result since 2005 with the mileage at each test, plus tax, SORN, first registration and V5C issue dates. The paid check adds the finance search, the guarantee, the recall and security watch searches and the specification data.

What happens if the report turns out to be wrong?

You claim under the guarantee, which is dealt with by HPI in Leeds rather than by the AA. If that fails there is no ombudsman: a car check is not a regulated financial service. Your fallback is the Consumer Rights Act 2015, which treats the report as digital content and gives you repair, replacement and then a price reduction — on a £14.99 report, a price reduction is £14.99 — or a chargeback through your card issuer.

Our verdict

As a way of buying an HPI report, the AA badge is the better deal: £14.99 against £19.99 for a single check, and £6.00 a check in the five-pack against £10.00 in HPI's own three-pack. The data is identical because it is the same company supplying it, and the range is simpler, with no stripped-down tier that quietly drops the finance search. What you are paying for, once the free government services and the AA's own free listing check are taken off, is really the finance answer and the guarantee — and the guarantee is narrower than £30,000 suggests, conditional on details a casual buyer will not have to hand. The presentation deserves better than it gets: a 2017 sample report with a retired damage category, 2019 statistics and a complaints route pointing at a platform that closed in 2025 are all things the AA could fix in an afternoon.
