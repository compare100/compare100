Airport Lounges Airport Lounge

Rates and terms checked 21 September 2026 · Airport Lounge · Compare100 editorial team

Airport Lounges sells lounge entry one visit at a time, and a pre-booked UK visit costs between £29.99 and £64 an adult at the prices the companies running those rooms were publishing on 21 September 2026. There is no membership, no annual fee and no card to hold: you pick an airport, pick a date, pay once and walk in.

That suits the traveller who flies twice a year. A lounge pass bought outright is the only route into these rooms for anyone without a premium bank account or a business-class boarding pass, and it is priced the same whether you fly once or fifty times. UK airports handled 302 million passengers in 2025, a record the Civil Aviation Authority confirmed on 24 February 2026, and a paid seat away from the gate is one of the few things at an airport you can still fix in advance for a known price.

What you are buying, though, belongs to somebody else. Airport Lounges is a reseller: the rooms are run by Swissport, by Manchester Airports Group's CAVU business and by Plaza Premium, and it is their rules on how long you may stay, how late you may arrive and whether you get your money back that apply to your booking. Check the operator's own price before you pay, because that is the number the reseller is marking up or matching.

What a UK lounge visit costs in September 2026

Swissport publishes a price for every Aspire-family lounge it runs in the UK, and the spread is wider than most people expect. The cheapest are £31 at Humberside and Inverness and £32 at Aberdeen's Northern Lights and Glasgow's Upperdeck. In the south, Club Aspire at Gatwick South is £34, the No1 Lounge at Gatwick North is £38, Club Aspire at Heathrow Terminal 5 is £40, My Lounge at Heathrow Terminal 3 is £44 and the No1 Lounge in the same terminal is £50. Manchester Terminal 3 is £37; the C Gates lounge in Terminal 2 is £46. At the top, Luxe by Aspire at Liverpool and Newcastle is £57 and Suite by Aspire at Newcastle is £64.

CAVU's Escape Lounges, which you will find at Manchester, Stansted, East Midlands and Edinburgh among others, start at £29.99 per person pre-booked. Children under three go in free, and a single booking may carry no more than three children or infants.

Two levers move the number. Booking ahead rather than paying at the door is worth up to 20% by No1 Lounges' own reckoning, and a pre-booked visit there carries delay cover, so a late flight does not cost you a second entry. Arriving early costs extra: the three hours before departure are included and additional hours are sold as an add-on. Against that, Which? researchers paid an average of £42 across the lounges they visited, with individual entries ranging from £16.99 to £68 — so a reseller quoting much above the operator's own published figure is not saving anyone anything.

The rooms belong to somebody else

Edinburgh Airport is the clearest illustration. Three lounges sit airside — an Aspire Lounge, the Turkish Airlines Lounge run by Plaza Premium Group, and an Escape Lounge — and all three are operated by third-party companies rather than by the airport. Book any of them through any seller and the contract behind your ticket is with that operator.

Swissport GB Ltd is the name in the footer of the Aspire estate, which now runs to more than 100 lounges worldwide across the UK, Europe, Australia, the Middle East, Africa, North America and Asia. The No1, Clubrooms, My Lounge and Club Aspire brands all sit inside it, which is why one price list covers all four. The Escape Lounges are run in the UK by CAVU Experiences (EMEA) Limited, 6th Floor Olympic House, Manchester Airport, M90 1QX — the commercial arm of Manchester Airports Group — with sister companies in Chicago and Carlton South, Victoria handling the American and Australian rooms.

This matters for one practical reason. If the room is full, the coffee machine is broken or staff turn you away at the desk, the seller who took your card is not the party that can fix it, and the operator's terms are the ones a complaint is judged against. Keep the operator's name and the booking reference together, and raise a problem with whichever of the two the terms name — on a resold booking that is usually the seller first, the operator second.

Three hours, one hour late, and no refund

The booking rules are tighter than a restaurant reservation and looser than a flight, and almost nobody reads them before paying. Outside the United States, Escape Lounge bookings must be made at least 12 hours before your arrival time, so there is no walking up to the website from the departures hall. Cancellation needs at least 24 hours' notice; inside that window the wording is blunt — bookings cancelled within 24 hours of the arrival date “will not be refunded”. Once the booking has started, no amendment can be made at all.

Arrive late and the seat may be gone. Escape reserves the right to free a seat if you have not turned up within one hour of the booked time. Edinburgh's lounge terms say a booking that goes unused simply expires and is not refundable, and that bookings are not transferable to another person or another lounge — you cannot hand a pass you cannot use to the friend you are travelling with. Amendments there can be made online up to 24 hours before departure.

Stay lengths vary by brand rather than by seller: three hours at a standard Escape Lounge, two at a 1903 Lounge, two and a half to three at the Executive and Essence rooms. Only an adult of 18 or over may make a booking. There is a smart-casual dress code, footwear at all times, clothing with offensive slogans refused, and an expectation that guests “drink sensibly” — the free bar is not unlimited in practice, and staff can refuse entry or ask you to leave.

Which? sent researchers into 28 lounges

There is no Defaqto or Fairer Finance for airport lounges, so the nearest thing to an independent rating is the survey Which? published on 22 November 2025. Its researchers visited 28 UK lounges, mostly between July and September 2025, scoring each on food, comfort, space and views.

Only about a third managed three stars or better. The best of them were Clubrooms at Gatwick South at 3.5 stars for £44 to £50 and Clubrooms at Birmingham, also 3.5, at £48. The worst was Club Aspire at Heathrow Terminal 5, which scored one star at £42, while Suite by Aspire at Newcastle — the most expensive room in the survey at £68 — managed 2.5 and the Escape Lounge at Manchester Terminal 1 scored two. Prices have run well ahead of inflation: the average of £42 compares with £25 in 2017, a rise of 68%.

Which? travel editor Rory Boland put the value case plainly: “Instead of paying over £40 for a lukewarm buffet breakfast on a dirty plate, you can get a lot more for your money by visiting a bar or restaurant.” That is worth weighing before booking, and it cuts both ways — the cheaper regional rooms at £31 and £32 have to beat a sit-down breakfast and a drink by a much smaller margin than a £64 suite does.

Where the money protection stops

A lounge pass sits in an awkward gap in consumer law, and a reseller sits in the middle of it.

Start with the card. Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act 1974 makes a credit card company equally liable with the trader for misrepresentation or breach of contract — but section 75(3)(b) excludes any single item with a cash price of £100 or less, or more than £30,000. At £29.99 to £64, one lounge pass falls under the floor, and so does a pair of them. Paying by credit card buys you a chargeback claim against the card scheme and nothing stronger; debit card holders have chargeback alone.

Then the cooling-off period, which does not exist here. Regulation 28(1)(h) of the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 removes the usual 14-day right to cancel an online purchase where the contract is for “catering or services related to leisure activities” on a specific date. A lounge booked for a named flight is exactly that, so the operator's own 24-hour rule is the only cancellation right you have.

Nor is any of this a package. Buy a lounge pass on its own and there is no ATOL cover, no Package Travel Regulations protection and no financial regulator standing behind the seller, because selling entry to a room is not a regulated activity. If the seller fails between your payment and your flight, recovering the money is a chargeback question rather than a claim against a protection scheme. That is a reason to book close to travel rather than months out.

The checks we could not complete

Two things about Airport Lounges itself could not be verified, and both should be resolved before a reader hands over card details.

The first is corporate identity. A Companies House search on 21 September 2026 for a company registered under the name Airport Lounges returns no results at all. That does not mean the business is not real — plenty of sites trade under a name that differs from the registered one — but it does mean the trading name alone will not tell you which company takes your money, where it is registered or what its accounts look like. The site's own terms should name the legal entity and a company number; look for both before booking, and treat their absence as a reason to pay by credit card.

The second is the site itself. Both www.airportlounges.com and www.airport-lounges.co.uk block automated retrieval, so the prices, the lounge list, the cancellation terms and the complaints route on those pages could not be read or quoted here. Every figure on this page therefore comes from the companies that run the lounges, from the Civil Aviation Authority, from Which? or from the legislation itself. Compare the seller's quote against the operator's published price for the same room on the same date — Swissport and CAVU both publish theirs — and check the cancellation deadline in writing before you pay, because a resold booking can carry the seller's terms on top of the operator's.

Common questions

How much does a UK airport lounge cost to book?

Between £29.99 and £64 an adult on the operators' own September 2026 price lists. Escape Lounges start at £29.99, Aspire charges £31 at Humberside and Inverness, £38 at Gatwick North and £50 for the No1 Lounge at Heathrow Terminal 3, and Suite by Aspire at Newcastle is £64. Which? found an average of £42 across 28 lounges in its November 2025 survey.

Can I cancel a lounge booking and get my money back?

Usually only with at least 24 hours' notice. Escape Lounges will not refund a booking cancelled within 24 hours of the arrival time, and no amendment is possible once the booking has started. Edinburgh's lounge terms say an unused booking expires unrefunded and cannot be transferred to another person or lounge. The 14-day online cancellation right does not apply, because regulation 28(1)(h) of the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013 excludes leisure services booked for a specific date.

How long can I stay in the lounge?

Three hours before your scheduled departure at a standard Escape Lounge and across the Aspire and No1 estate, with extra hours sold as an add-on if you want to arrive earlier. A 1903 Lounge allows two hours and the Executive and Essence rooms two and a half to three. Turn up more than an hour after your booked time and the seat can be released.

Is a lounge pass protected if the seller goes bust?

Not by ATOL, and not by Section 75 either. A lounge pass bought on its own is not a package holiday, so the Package Travel Regulations and ATOL do not apply, and at under £100 a pass it falls below the Section 75 threshold in the Consumer Credit Act 1974. The practical remedy is a card chargeback, which is why booking close to the travel date rather than months ahead is the safer habit.

Can I take children into an airport lounge?

Yes at most, with limits. Escape Lounges admit children aged three to 15 at a charge, let infants under three in free, and cap a single booking at three children or infants. The booking itself must be made by someone aged 18 or over. The 1903 Lounges are adults-only unless a child is accompanying an adult whose access came with an airline ticket.

Our verdict

Airport Lounges is a booking front for rooms run by Swissport, CAVU and Plaza Premium, and it is competing on price and convenience rather than on anything it owns. That is a perfectly reasonable thing to buy — a single visit at £31 in Aberdeen or £34 at Gatwick South is decent value against airport food prices, and there is no membership to carry. Do two things first. Check the same lounge on the operator's own site for the same date, because the operator publishes its price and the reseller's margin sits on top of it. And read the cancellation deadline: outside 24 hours you are usually fine, inside it you have bought a non-refundable ticket to a room you may not like, with no Section 75 claim to fall back on.
