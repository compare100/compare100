Children's clothes on Amazon start at £1. Amazon Haul, the budget storefront that opened in the UK on 6 May 2025, prices everything at £20 or less and most of it under £10, and delivery is free once the basket reaches £15. Those figures come from Amazon's own UK newsroom and were checked on 25 September 2026.

The main store is a different shop with different economics. Prime costs £8.99 a month or £95 a year, £4.49 and £47.49 for students, and in 2025 Amazon delivered more than 1.6 billion items to UK addresses on the same or the next day. Around 100,000 UK small and medium-sized businesses sell through it; roughly 60% of Amazon's sales come from small businesses, and in 2024 they sold more than a billion products and booked over £3.8 billion in export sales.

And the two storefronts run on different clocks: Haul orders are quoted at up to two weeks to arrive and carry a 15-day window to ask for a return, which is a tighter pair of numbers than the main store's next-day delivery.

What a pound buys, and what it costs to get it to the door

Amazon now runs two children's clothing shops under one roof and they price in opposite directions. Amazon Haul, launched in the UK on 6 May 2025 after a late-2024 debut in the United States, caps everything at £20, puts most of the range under £10 and keeps a section of items at £1. Delivery is free on orders of £15 or more and £2.99 below that, and Amazon quotes the majority of Haul orders as arriving in two weeks or less.

The main store buys speed instead. Prime is £8.99 a month or £95 a year — £4.49 and £47.49 on the student rate for 18 to 24-year-olds and those in higher education — and it carries unlimited one-day delivery, same-day delivery on more than a million items across 80-plus UK towns and cities, and a year of Deliveroo Plus Silver. Amazon says more than 1.6 billion items reached UK customers the same or the next day during 2025.

The arithmetic for a parent is not complicated. A £95 annual Prime fee is six Haul baskets brought up to the £15 free-delivery threshold with change to spare, or it is 31 separate £2.99 delivery charges avoided. If you buy school polo shirts twice a year, Haul plus patience is cheaper; if you are replacing a coat the week the weather turns, the two-week quote makes the decision for you.

One number to check on the day rather than take from here. Amazon's two newsroom articles about Haul give different multi-buy thresholds — one says 5% off orders over £30 and 10% over £50, the other says 5% over £50 and 10% over £75. Both are Amazon's own pages. Read the basket total at checkout and believe that.

Who actually sells the dress

Around 100,000 UK small and medium-sized businesses sell on Amazon. Roughly 60% of Amazon's sales come from small businesses, they sold more than a billion products in 2024, and their export sales through the platform passed £3.8 billion in the same year. Amazon employs about 75,000 people in the UK, has invested more than £75 billion here between 2014 and 2024, announced a further £40 billion of planned UK investment in June 2025, and runs more than 30 fulfilment centres, sort centres and delivery stations.

For a shopper, the consequence sits in one line of grey text on the listing. Where the seller is an independent business, the sale contract is with that business. Amazon's role is the shop window, the payment rail and, where the item is held in its warehouses, the packing and posting. That matters when a jacket arrives with a cord where a cord should not be: the Consumer Rights Act claim and the 14-day distance cancellation right under regulation 30 of the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 both run against the seller named on the order, and regulation 30 starts that 14-day clock on the day the last of the goods physically reaches you, not the day you ordered.

The position of Amazon's own retail arm inside its own marketplace has been examined by the competition regulator. In case 51184, decided on 3 November 2023, the Competition and Markets Authority accepted binding commitments from Amazon.com, Inc., Amazon UK Services Ltd, Amazon Europe Core SARL, Amazon Services Europe SARL and Amazon EU SARL. The CMA's published reasoning contains the figures nobody else prints: more than 75% of UK Marketplace purchases go through the Featured Offer — the box with the buy button in it — and Amazon Retail was selected as the Featured Offer in more than 80% of cases where both Amazon and a third-party seller were eligible. Prime subscribers accounted for over 80% of total sales value in 2021. Under the commitments, which run until 20 June 2028, Amazon must apply objectively verifiable and non-discriminatory criteria when choosing the Featured Offer and may not use Prime eligibility or Prime labelling as one of them.

The economics behind a £6 T-shirt

Amazon publishes what it charges the businesses selling on it, and on clothing the numbers are unusually low. A seller pays either £25 a month excluding VAT on the Professional plan or 75p per item sold on the Individual plan, then a referral fee on each sale. For Clothing and Accessories that fee is tiered: 5% on items priced at or below £15, 10% between £15 and £20, and 15% above £20, with a minimum of 25p per item. Amazon cut clothing referral fees in its European stores on 5 January 2026.

Children's clothing sits almost entirely in the bottom band. A £6 babygrow carries a 30p referral fee; a £12 pack of school socks carries 60p. Most categories on Amazon run at a flat 8% to 15%, so a seller of infant clothes keeps a larger share of a small price than a seller of almost anything else on the site — which is a large part of why the cheap end of this category is as deep as it is.

Sellers using Fulfilment by Amazon or Seller Fulfilled Prime on items over £40 pay 15% on the first £40 and 7% on the rest. Those sellers are also enrolled automatically in prepaid returns: they must supply a prepaid return label or a returnless refund on every request, and respond to any request that falls outside Amazon's own returns policy within 24 hours.

Two rulebooks that apply to children's clothes and nothing else

Most clothing in Britain is sold under the general duty in the General Product Safety Regulations 2005 that a product be safe in normal or reasonably foreseeable use, and nothing more specific. Children's clothing is one of the few exceptions, and the first of the two extra rulebooks is about fire.

The Nightwear (Safety) Regulations 1985 came into operation on 1 March 1987 and cover anything designed for and normally worn by a person under 13 years old. Children's nightwear must meet the flammability performance of BS 5722, tested after washing to BS 5651 — with three exceptions: pyjamas, cotton terry-towelling bath robes, and infants' garments for children up to three months with a chest measurement of 53cm or less. The regulations also cut off at size: a nightdress with a chest over 91cm or a length over 122cm, or a robe with a chest over 97cm or a sleeve over 69cm, falls outside the children's definition.

The labelling is the part a parent can actually use. A garment that does not meet BS 5722 must carry KEEP AWAY FROM FIRE in red. One that does may carry LOW FLAMMABILITY TO BS 5722 in black. Where the fabric has been treated with a flame-retardant chemical, the label must add DO NOT WASH AT MORE THAN 50 °C. CHECK SUITABILITY OF WASHING AGENT, because a hot wash strips the treatment out. The label belongs inside the neck or next to the size label, on durable material with enough colour contrast to be legible. BS EN 14878 is the recommended standard for the exempted pyjamas, robes and infants' garments. Enforcement sits with local Trading Standards under these regulations and the 2005 regulations together.

Open the parcel, look inside the neck, and read what is printed there. That is a thirty-second check and it is the only one in this article that requires nothing but the garment.

Cords, hoods and the 7.5 centimetre rule

The second rulebook is BS EN 14682:2014, on cords and drawstrings, and it exists because children strangle on them. It splits childhood into two bands: young children from birth to 7 years, taken as heights up to 134cm; and older children and young persons from 7 to 14, up to 182cm for boys and 176cm for girls. It then divides the garment into zones.

In the head, neck and upper chest zone, drawstrings and functional cords are prohibited outright on young children's clothing, and decorative cords may not appear on hoods or at the back of the neck, with no knots or three-dimensional embellishments. For older children the same zone allows a drawstring with no free ends, a protruding loop no more than 15cm in circumference measured at the smallest opening, functional cords limited to 7.5cm, no elastic in a functional cord, and toggles fixed to the garment.

Lower down, the limits loosen and stay precise. On trousers, shorts and skirts, drawstring free ends may run to 20cm each; on shirts, coats and dresses, 14cm each with the garment open and flat. A tied belt or sash is capped at 36cm from the tying point. Below the hip, no cord may hang below the lower edge of the garment, and fringes count as a series of decorative cords. At the back, drawstrings and functional cords are banned and decorative cords are held to 7.5cm. On long sleeves, free ends stop at 7.5cm and nothing below the elbow may extend past the cuff; on short sleeves the limit is 7.5cm for young children and 14cm for older ones. A drawstring must be bar-tacked at its middle so it cannot be pulled out in one piece.

None of that is a legal requirement in the way the nightwear labelling is. A standard of this kind creates a presumption: a product that meets the relevant standard is presumed safe. It is still the best single test a parent can apply to a hooded top bought online, and the numbers are short enough to remember — nothing at the neck under seven, nothing hanging past a hem, 7.5cm anywhere at the back.

What the recall register shows, and what it does not

The Office for Product Safety and Standards publishes every UK product recall, safety report and safety alert in one free database on GOV.UK. It held 3,730 reports when this page was written, and it is filtered by product category, so a parent can look up a brand before buying and a recall after.

Search it for nightwear and four recalls come back across five years: Lipsy girls' jersey nightwear on 9 July 2021, Shein children's sleepwear sets on 8 October 2021 marked high risk, an Asda older girls' leopard print pyjama set on 11 November 2022, and a La Redoute Alanis printed bathrobe on 15 September 2026. All four were recalls from end users, which is the strongest corrective action the register records. None of the four is an Amazon own-brand product.

Read that carefully in both directions. Four recalls in the whole register is a small number for a category with a mandatory flammability standard, and it says something reassuring about how rarely the rule is broken by businesses large enough to be traced. It is also a register of what was caught. A listing from a seller with no UK presence, taken down before anyone tested the garment, never reaches it. The categories most relevant to a parent — clothing, textiles and fashion items, and childcare articles and children's equipment — are both there to be browsed, and the most recent entry on the day of checking was a baby side sleeper wedge flagged as a serious risk on 21 September 2026.

Sending it back when it does not fit

Children's clothes are bought against a moving target, so the returns terms matter more here than in almost any other category. Amazon offers free return shipping on eligible items in Clothing, Shoes and Handbags, and charges sellers a returns processing fee on customer returns in those categories — a fee that does not apply to other fashion lines such as jewellery or luggage. Sellers who fulfil their own orders are automatically enrolled in prepaid returns labels and must provide a label or a returnless refund on every request, with 24 hours to respond to anything outside Amazon's standard policy.

Underneath all of that sits the statutory right, and it is narrower than most people assume. Regulation 30 of the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 gives 14 days to cancel a distance sale, counted from the day the goods come into your physical possession; where an order is split into several deliveries, the clock starts when the last of them arrives. A retailer may give you longer and many do. None may give you less.

Haul is the exception worth planning around. Its return window is 15 days from delivery — one day past the statutory floor — and the item must be new, unused and unaltered. Set that against a delivery quoted at up to two weeks and the practical position is stark: a Haul coat ordered on the first of the month may not arrive until the fourteenth, leaving a fortnight in which a growing child must try it on, fail to fit it, and have it dropped at a Post Office, Asda or Morrisons collection point. Refunds are typically processed within seven days after that.

The rules for marketplaces are being rewritten

Until recently, British product safety law was built around manufacturers, importers and distributors — three roles that a website connecting a Chinese workshop to a Bolton doorstep does not obviously occupy. The Product Regulation and Metrology Act 2025 changes that. Section 2(2)(e) expressly allows product regulations to cover the marketing of products through an online marketplace, and section 2(3) lists the people on whom requirements may be imposed: manufacturers, anyone who markets a product in the UK, importers, anyone controlling access to or the contents of an online marketplace, anyone acting as an intermediary, authorised representatives, and anyone carrying out monitoring, assessment, verification or certification.

The Act is a framework: it grants the powers, and the regulations made under it will decide what marketplaces have to do. Until they arrive, the General Product Safety Regulations 2005 and the sector rules above are what apply, and the practical burden of checking sits with the buyer.

OPSS has effectively said as much. Its online marketplaces safety campaign ran in two phases, November to December 2025 and February to March 2026, and its stakeholder toolkit sets out five checks to make before buying: look up the seller's name, location and how long they have been trading; read the detailed reviews rather than the star average, looking for comments about safety; check the description for instructions, warnings and material information; look for a UKCA or CE mark; and search GOV.UK for product recalls. The campaign's one published statistic is that nearly one in four people had bought a counterfeit electrical product online.

How Amazon scores against the shops it competes with

Which? surveys shoppers on baby and children's retailers and publishes the results, which is the nearest thing this category has to an independent rating. The most recent survey drew on 2,317 members of the public with fieldwork in July and August 2025, and set a category average customer score of 80%.

Amazon scored 81% — above the average and below most of its direct rivals in children's clothing. Next took 83%, Marks & Spencer and John Lewis 82% each, and JoJo Maman Bébé 76%. The best score in the survey went to UK Baby Centre on 86%, with Baby City and iCandy World on 85%; The Range came last on 74%. Seven retailers were named Which? Recommended Providers — Joie Baby, Maxi Cosi, Next, Smyths Toys, The Nursery Store, Bugaboo and John Lewis. Amazon was not among them.

A single percentage point above a category average is a fair summary of the Amazon children's clothing experience: it works, it is cheap, it arrives quickly, and the people who scored it did not love it the way they loved a specialist. Most retailers in the survey took four or five stars for customer service and delivery, with value for money typically at four.

What Which? found when it went looking for unsafe listings

Two pieces of Which? research sit behind the safety sections above, and both are specific about Amazon.

On 21 November 2025 Which? published research that took all 702 OPSS safety reports issued between 1 August 2024 and 11 August 2025, extracted 1,036 product images from them and reverse-image-searched 17 online marketplaces. It found close to 800 products matching items that regulators had already declared unsafe, more than 200 of them with immediately verifiable safety risks; 31% of the OPSS reports had a matching product still on sale; 98% of the matches related to reports graded serious or high risk; and 95% of the products had been available for at least 28 days after the warning. Among them were 71 baby sleeping bags with suffocation hazards. Amazon initially declined to remove sleeping bags reported to it and subsequently took down eight of the twelve products flagged.

On 8 July 2026 Which? published a follow-up covering 150 unsafe products across Alibaba, AliExpress, Amazon, eBay, Etsy, OnBuy, TikTok Shop and Wish. Nearly a quarter of the 150 were found on Amazon, including six dangerous sleeping bags, some of them fulfilled by Amazon itself. One example was a swaddle blanket for babies aged nought to six months with a hooded design and no arm holes, sold for nursery use — the same product Which? had reported to Amazon in January. Sue Davies, the organisation's head of consumer protection policy, said the lives of babies were at risk because the platforms would not stop dangerous products reaching their customers. Amazon removed the flagged listings while it investigated and pointed to its controls and its requirement for third-party laboratory testing; eBay, Etsy, TikTok Shop, AliExpress, Alibaba and OnBuy also removed items, and Wish did not respond.

Neither study is a study of children's clothing, and neither is a reason to avoid the shop. Both are a reason to treat the safety label as the thing you buy on, rather than the listing copy.

Common questions

Who am I actually buying from on Amazon?

The trading entity behind Amazon.co.uk is Amazon EU S.à r.l., UK Branch, registered in the UK on 2 February 2015 under FC032354 and incorporated in Luxembourg; the main UK operating company is Amazon UK Services Ltd, company 03223028, incorporated on 10 July 1996 and registered at 1 Principal Place, Worship Street, London EC2A 2FA. Where a listing names an independent seller, however, your sale contract is with that business, not with Amazon.

How long do I have to send children's clothes back?

On the main store, eligible Clothing, Shoes and Handbags items carry free return shipping and sellers must provide a prepaid label. On Amazon Haul the window is 15 days from delivery and the item must be new, unused and unaltered. Underneath both sits regulation 30 of the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013, which gives 14 days from the day the goods reach you — a floor no retailer may go below.

What should I check on a child's pyjamas?

Look inside the neck. Under the Nightwear (Safety) Regulations 1985 anything designed for a child under 13 must carry either KEEP AWAY FROM FIRE in red or LOW FLAMMABILITY TO BS 5722 in black, and a flame-retardant-treated garment must add DO NOT WASH AT MORE THAN 50 °C. Pyjamas, cotton terry-towelling robes and infants' garments up to three months with a chest of 53cm or less are exempt from the BS 5722 performance requirement but not from the labelling regime.

Are hooded tops with drawstrings allowed for young children?

BS EN 14682:2014 prohibits drawstrings and functional cords in the head, neck and upper chest zone of clothing for children from birth to seven years, and bars decorative cords on hoods or at the back of the neck. For children of seven to fourteen the same zone allows a drawstring with no free ends and a protruding loop of no more than 15cm, with functional cords capped at 7.5cm.

Is Amazon Prime worth it for buying children's clothes?

That depends on how often you order. Prime is £95 a year or £8.99 a month, which is the equivalent of 31 separate £2.99 Haul delivery charges, or a little over six Haul baskets brought up to the £15 free-delivery threshold. If your orders are planned and infrequent, Haul plus the two-week wait is cheaper; if you buy in small quantities at short notice, the annual fee pays for itself quickly.

Where do I report something unsafe?

The Office for Product Safety and Standards publishes UK product recalls, safety reports and safety alerts at gov.uk — 3,730 reports as at 25 September 2026, filtered by product category, including clothing, textiles and fashion items. Enforcement against a seller is carried out by local Trading Standards, which consumers reach through Citizens Advice rather than directly.

Our verdict

Amazon is the cheapest broad children's clothing range in Britain and the fastest, and the numbers behind that are published rather than claimed: £1 entry prices on Haul, £95 a year for unlimited one-day delivery, referral fees of 5% on anything under £15 that keep small sellers in the category. It scores 81% with Which? shoppers against a category average of 80%, which is about right — competent rather than cherished. The reasons to be careful are structural rather than commercial. Most of what you buy comes from an independent seller, so that is who your legal rights run against; Haul's 15-day return window sits awkwardly beside its two-week delivery quote; and Which? has twice found unsafe baby goods listed on the site, once after flagging them. Buy on the label rather than the listing: children's nightwear must carry its BS 5722 wording inside the neck, and nothing should hang from the hood of anything made for a child under seven.
