Amazon Furniture

Rates and terms checked 26 September 2026 · Furniture · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Amazon takes 15% of a furniture sale up to £175 and 10% of everything above that line, which makes furniture the cheapest large category on its published rate card — kitchenware and mattresses both pay a flat 15%, and tools 13%. Those rates were read from Amazon's own seller pricing page on 26 September 2026, and they explain a great deal about why the aisle looks the way it does.

What Amazon has built behind that rate card is a genuine bulky-goods operation rather than a parcel service pretending to handle wardrobes. Anything over 23kg, or with a packed side longer than 175cm, is routed through a separate network: Scheduled Delivery lets you pick a date and a slot, carriage into the room of your choice is part of it, and eligible items can be bought with unpacking, assembly and removal of the old one added at checkout. Sellers using Amazon's own warehouses can put items of up to 140kg and 300cm through the same pipes.

Most furniture on the site is sold by third-party businesses rather than by Amazon, and that is worth knowing before you click rather than after. It is not a warning: purchases from those sellers carry the A-to-z Guarantee, which you can claim on for up to 90 days after the last delivery date, and which also reaches property damage and personal injury caused by a product bought on the site. It does change who turns up if a sofa has to go back, and that is covered further down.

The rate card, and what it tells you about the aisle

Amazon publishes what it charges the businesses selling on it, and for furniture the schedule is tiered rather than flat: 15% of the first £175 of the sale price, 10% of the portion above it, with a 25p minimum on any item. Sit that beside the neighbouring categories and the shape of the shop becomes clear. Home Products pay 8% at or below £20 and 15% above it. Kitchen pays 15%. Mattresses pay 15%. Tools and Home Improvement, along with door, window and shower accessories, pay 13%. Amazon reduced a number of these rates across its European stores on 5 January 2026.

On top of the commission a seller pays either 75p per item sold on the Individual plan or £25 a month excluding VAT on the Professional plan. If they also store the goods in Amazon's warehouses under the heavy and bulky programme, handling is charged per item: £15.43 up to 20kg, £18.48 from 20 to 30kg, £19.16 from 30 to 40kg, then a sharp step to £42.98 at 40 to 50kg and £44.25 at 50 to 60kg, with anything heavier at 39p per kilogram. The eligibility band for that programme in the UK runs from 31.5kg to 140kg, with a length of 175cm to 300cm or a girth of at least 360cm.

The arithmetic matters to a buyer for one reason. A three-piece suite at £900 hands Amazon £26.25 on the first £175 and £72.50 on the rest — £98.75, or about 11% — before a penny of handling. A £90 flat-pack chest of drawers hands over £13.50, a full 15%. The tapering fee is a deliberate invitation to list expensive furniture, and it is the reason the upper end of the range on Amazon is deeper than most people expect it to be.

Getting a wardrobe through your front door

Amazon treats an item as heavy or bulky once it passes 23kg or has any side longer than 175cm when packed, and from that point the delivery behaves differently from a parcel. On Scheduled Delivery you choose the date and time window at the checkout and placement in the room you want is included. On Standard Delivery you get an estimated date, the driver will normally ring around 30 minutes before arriving, and — this is the detail people miss — an item under 31.5kg priced below £250 is only carried as far as the entrance to your home. Getting it upstairs is either a Scheduled Delivery or a paid add-on.

Where a service is offered it is bought at the point of purchase and not afterwards: unpacking with removal of all the packaging, installation and assembly, wall mounting where that applies, and recycling of the item being replaced. Amazon does not take payment for any of it until the work is finished, though the card is authorised at the time of the order. An adult has to be there, appointments can be changed up to 24 hours beforehand, and a signature may be required on the delivery itself. Availability is regional, and some postcodes simply cannot be served.

Sellers who fulfil their own heavy and bulky orders either have the carrier telephone to agree a date, or publish a calendar of slots for the buyer to choose from. That is a materially different experience from Amazon's own network, and nothing on a listing page makes the distinction obvious before you order.

The label sewn inside the sofa

Upholstered furniture sold in the UK has to satisfy the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988, and there is no version of the rules that applies less strictly because the seller is on a marketplace. Four things have to be true: filling materials must meet the ignition requirements in Schedule 1 for foam or Schedule 2 for non-foam fillings, the upholstery composite must pass the cigarette test in Schedule 4, the cover must pass the match test in Schedule 5 or sit over a compliant interliner, and a permanent label must be attached to every item apart from mattresses and bed bases.

That label is the one thing on this entire subject a buyer can check with nothing but the delivered item. It is headed CARELESSNESS CAUSES FIRE and it carries the supplier's name and postcode, a batch or identification number, the date of manufacture or import, a description of the filling and the covering, and whether a Schedule 3 interliner is present. It has to be durable and securely fixed to an external surface, usually beneath a seat cushion or behind a loose cover. Trading Standards guidance treats a stitched label as a better sign than a stapled one, because stapling suggests it was added after the fact. Mattresses are labelled instead against BS 7177.

Second-hand upholstery is caught by the same regime, with one exemption: furniture made before 1 January 1950, and upholstery materials for it, falls outside the Regulations. Anything newer that arrives without a legible permanent label should not have been sold, and that applies to a private resale as much as to a shop.

What changed on 30 October 2025

The 1988 Regulations were amended by SI 2025/531, made on 28 April 2025, laid before Parliament on 30 April and brought into force on 30 October 2025 after a six-month deferral to meet World Trade Organization obligations. The changes followed a public consultation held between August and October 2023 and announced in January 2025.

Three things moved. The display label — the swing ticket that used to hang off new furniture in a showroom — was abolished outright, with regulation 10 omitted and its schedules deleted, on the reasoning that the permanent label already carries the compliance information. Stock still bearing an accurate display label may go on being sold. A long list of baby and child products came out of the definition of furniture altogether, including prams, pushchairs, buggies and strollers, cots, cribs, cradles, carry cots, Moses baskets, travel cots and bedside sleepers, cot bumpers, play pens, play mats, changing mats, baby nests, rockers and bouncers, highchairs, booster seats, baby walkers and children's car seats, along with children's mattresses under 170cm long and 75cm wide. Those products are now governed by general product safety law instead. And the window for prosecuting a breach was extended from six months to twelve months from the date the offence was committed, across all four UK nations, with no retrospective effect on anything done before 30 October 2025.

For anyone buying online the practical consequence of the first change is small but real: the ticket that a shopper in a showroom could once read at a glance no longer exists, so the permanent label is now the whole of the evidence, and it cannot be seen until the thing is in the house.

Why the fire rules are written the way they are

Home Office figures for the year ending March 2026 put the case better than any argument about regulation could. Fire and rescue services in England attended 26,180 dwelling fires in that year, up 2.8% on the 25,465 of the year before. There were 184 fire-related fatalities in dwellings, 72% of all fire deaths, and that figure fell 14% from 214.

Inside those numbers, textiles, upholstery and furnishings were the item first ignited in 27% of primary dwelling fires and were mainly responsible for the spread of the fire in 22% of them. They were associated with 48% of the deaths. Cooking appliances start far more fires — 42% — and kill far fewer people. A sofa is not usually what starts a house fire; it is very often what turns one into a fatal one. That asymmetry is the entire reason a piece of upholstered furniture carries a label and a toaster does not.

Sending it back, and the clause that stops you

Two separate rights run in parallel. The statutory one gives 14 days from receipt to cancel a distance purchase for any reason at all. Amazon's own policy is more generous and gives 30 days from receipt for unused, undamaged goods, which is the number most people are working from without realising the two are different.

For a heavy or bulky marketplace item — 31.5kg or more, or a longest packed side over 175cm — the process is its own thing. The return has to be started within 30 days of delivery, after which collection cannot be guaranteed and the matter becomes a negotiation with the seller. Once started, the seller has 48 hours to supply carrier details and a collection date through the messaging service, and collection is due within 15 calendar days in the UK or 22 for an international seller. An adult has to be present, and the item should be taken apart and packed, ideally in what it arrived in. If it turned up damaged or faulty, a free replacement or a full refund with home collection can be asked for instead.

Where the buyer simply changed their mind, return postage is charged from £1.99 in the UK and £8.09 internationally unless the item was both sold and dispatched by Amazon and flagged as eligible for free return. On Global Store orders the site automatically refunds return postage up to £20 for a US seller and £15 for an EU one.

The clause that catches furniture buyers is regulation 28(1) of the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, which removes the cancellation right entirely for goods made to the consumer's specifications or clearly personalised. A sofa ordered in a chosen fabric, a wardrobe cut to a stated width, a bed built to order: none of those has to be taken back because the buyer has changed their mind, and Amazon's own policy records customised products as not returnable, refundable only where they arrive damaged or defective. The fault-based rights survive; the free trial of the purchase does not.

Two recalls with an order number attached

The Office for Product Safety and Standards runs a free, searchable register of product safety alerts, reports and recalls, and it is the most useful thing a furniture buyer can look at before ordering. A search on the word furniture returned 83 reports on 26 September 2026, and a search on sofa returned 16.

Two of them name a product sold on Amazon. On 27 November 2025 a Yaheetech recliner armchair, model 610887 in grey, batches 202507 and 202508, was recalled from end users at serious risk of fire for failing to meet the requirements of the 1988 Regulations; the register records the Amazon ASIN, B0BDKX966B, as the sales channel, and names Monumart Ltd as the distributor handling returns. On 15 May 2026 a Yaheetech velvet convertible sofa bed, model 615825 in modern grey, batch Y02058B-202511, sold between January and April 2026 and made in China, was recalled on the finding that the foam filling could catch fire and continue to flame. Both were notified by local authority Trading Standards, and both offered a full refund including postage.

Read that in both directions. The system worked: a national regulator published the model number, the batch and the marketplace identifier, and a refund route exists. It also means a fire-risk armchair reached British living rooms through the site, and a buyer who does not think to search a government database before ordering will never know.

Who hears the complaint

Furniture retailing in the UK has its own ombudsman. The Furniture & Home Improvement Ombudsman closed 6,832 cases in 2025, handled 19,520 telephone enquiries and 10,028 emails, settled 99% of cases by conciliation at an average of 37 days, and sent the remaining 1% to adjudication with an expert inspection. Of those adjudicated, 45% were upheld in full for the consumer and 23% in part, with 32% not upheld. It awarded £1.2 million in settlements. Upholstery was the largest single complaint category at 37%, ahead of home improvement at 36%, with beds and mattresses and furniture and cabinetry at 5% each.

Membership is voluntary and paid for on a scale set by turnover. B&Q, DFS, Furniture Village, Marks & Spencer, Wickes and Wren Kitchens are all on the scheme's published member list. Amazon is not, and neither is any Amazon entity — checked against that list on 26 September 2026. A buyer with a dispute about a sofa bought on Amazon therefore has the A-to-z Guarantee, the seller, their card provider and the small claims track, but not an ombudsman with a code of practice behind it.

The A-to-z Guarantee is a reasonable substitute in most cases and a poor one in a few. It runs for 90 days from the last delivery date, requires three days to have elapsed since the latest estimated delivery before an undelivered-item claim, and gives the seller 48 hours to respond. It does not cover refused deliveries, chargebacks already raised, Amazon Global Store purchases, or Prime-delivered marketplace items, which are handled under a different route.

The company taking the commission

Amazon.com reported net sales of $200.6 billion for the quarter to 30 June 2026, up 20% year on year, with operating income of $27.5 billion, a 43% increase. AWS contributed $42.2 billion of sales and grew 37%. Net income of $62.6 billion, or $5.75 a diluted share, was inflated by $53.4 billion of non-operating pre-tax other income arising principally from the company's investment in Anthropic, so the operating line is the one to read.

The segment that contains the UK is International, and it is the thin one: $42.2 billion of sales produced $1.7 billion of operating income, a margin of 4.1%, against $9.1 billion on $116.2 billion in North America. Retail outside the United States is not where this company makes its money, which is worth holding in mind when reading any claim about how much Amazon can afford to spend on handling a damaged wardrobe.

Amazon has also stepped back from selling furniture under its own labels, having cut most of its private-label lines in 2023 as costs and antitrust scrutiny mounted. Its second-hand operation continues: Amazon Resale sells returned and open-box stock graded Like New, Very Good, Good or Acceptable, and Amazon Renewed sells refurbished goods graded Premium, Excellent, Good or Acceptable, with savings quoted at up to 50% off recommended retail prices. Buying used upholstery still puts the fire label question squarely back on the buyer.

Where it wins

  • A tiered furniture commission — 15% to £175, then 10% — that is cheaper than the flat 15% on kitchenware and mattresses, and encourages sellers to list higher-value pieces
  • A real heavy and bulky network rather than a parcel service: Scheduled Delivery with a chosen date and slot, and room-of-choice placement included
  • Unpacking, assembly, wall mounting and recycling of the item being replaced can be bought at the checkout, and are not charged until the work is done
  • A voluntary 30-day return window that more than doubles the 14 days the law requires
  • The A-to-z Guarantee covers third-party purchases for 90 days and extends to property damage and personal injury
  • Sellers can put items of up to 140kg and 300cm through Amazon's own warehouses, so large furniture is not confined to seller-fulfilled listings
  • Free, checkable recall data exists for the exact products sold here, published by the OPSS with model numbers and ASINs

Where it falls short

  • Amazon is not a member of the Furniture & Home Improvement Ombudsman, unlike DFS, B&Q, Furniture Village, Marks & Spencer, Wickes and Wren Kitchens, so there is no ombudsman route for a sofa dispute
  • On Standard Delivery an item under 31.5kg priced below £250 is only carried to the entrance of your home; getting it to the right room costs extra or requires Scheduled Delivery
  • Returning a heavy or bulky marketplace item depends on the seller, not on Amazon: 48 hours for them to respond and up to 15 calendar days before anyone collects, with an adult required to be present
  • Regulation 28(1) of the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013 removes the cancellation right for made-to-specification and personalised goods, which covers a large share of sofas and fitted furniture ordered online
  • Two Yaheetech items — a recliner armchair in November 2025 and a velvet sofa bed in May 2026 — were recalled at serious fire risk for breaching the 1988 Regulations, one with its Amazon ASIN recorded on the register
  • The permanent fire label cannot be inspected until the furniture is in the house, and since the display label was abolished on 30 October 2025 there is nothing else to read
  • Services, availability and even whether a delivery is possible vary by postcode, and cannot be added after the order is placed

Common questions

Does furniture sold on Amazon have to meet UK fire safety law?

Yes. Upholstered furniture supplied in the UK must comply with the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988 regardless of who sells it or where they are based. Fillings must meet Schedule 1 for foam or Schedule 2 for non-foam, the composite must pass the Schedule 4 cigarette test, the cover must pass the Schedule 5 match test or sit over a compliant interliner, and a permanent label headed CARELESSNESS CAUSES FIRE must be attached to everything except mattresses and bed bases, which are labelled against BS 7177 instead.

How long do I have to return a sofa bought on Amazon?

The statutory right is 14 days from receipt; Amazon's own policy extends that to 30 days for unused, undamaged goods. For a heavy or bulky marketplace item — 31.5kg or over, or a longest packed side over 175cm — the return must be started within 30 days of delivery. The seller then has 48 hours to give you carrier details, and collection is due within 15 calendar days in the UK or 22 days for an international seller. An adult must be at home for it.

Can I cancel a made-to-order sofa?

Not on a change of mind. Regulation 28(1) of the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 excludes goods made to the consumer's specifications or clearly personalised from the cancellation right, and Amazon records customised products as not returnable. If the item arrives damaged or defective your rights are unaffected and a refund is available within 30 days.

Will the driver take the furniture upstairs?

Only on a Scheduled Delivery, which includes placement in the room of your choice, or where you have paid for a service at the checkout. On Standard Delivery an item under 31.5kg priced below £250 is delivered to the entrance of your home. Unpacking, assembly and removal of the old item are separate paid options on eligible products and cannot be added after the order is placed.

Is there an ombudsman if something goes wrong?

Not for Amazon. The Furniture & Home Improvement Ombudsman closed 6,832 cases in 2025 and awarded £1.2 million, but it only takes disputes about member businesses, and Amazon is absent from its member list — checked on 26 September 2026 — while B&Q, DFS, Furniture Village, Marks & Spencer, Wickes and Wren Kitchens are all on it. Your routes are the seller, the A-to-z Guarantee within 90 days of the last delivery date, your card provider and the small claims track.

How do I check whether a piece of furniture has been recalled?

Search the OPSS product safety alerts, reports and recalls register on GOV.UK by keyword. On 26 September 2026 a search on furniture returned 83 reports and a search on sofa returned 16, and individual entries carry the model number, the batch codes, the country of manufacture and, in at least one case, the Amazon ASIN. It is free, it takes a minute, and no marketplace listing will tell you.

Our verdict

Amazon is a serious place to buy furniture rather than an accident of scale: the tiered 15%/10% commission pulls expensive pieces onto the site, and the heavy and bulky machinery behind it — dated slots, room-of-choice carriage, assembly, recycling, items to 140kg — is closer to a furniture retailer's operation than to a parcel network. The 30-day return window beats the statutory 14 days, and the A-to-z Guarantee gives 90 days of cover on third-party purchases. The gaps are structural rather than accidental. There is no ombudsman behind a dispute, because Amazon has not joined the one its competitors use. A large item returned to a marketplace seller moves at that seller's pace. Made-to-specification furniture is outside the cancellation right altogether under regulation 28(1). And on a category where textiles and upholstery are the item first ignited in 27% of dwelling fires but are associated with 48% of the deaths, the only compliance evidence a buyer can see is a label they cannot read until the thing is already in the room. Buy from it with the OPSS register open in another tab, and check the label under the cushion the day it arrives.
