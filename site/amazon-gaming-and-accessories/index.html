Amazon Gaming and Accessories

Rates and terms checked 26 September 2026 · Gaming and Accessories · Compare100 editorial team

Amazon's cheapest way into gaming costs £8.99. One month of Prime carries Luna Standard, Amazon's own cloud gaming service, with more than 50 titles included and nothing at all to be delivered; the Luna Controller, if you want the hardware, is £59.99. Both prices come from Amazon's UK newsroom and were checked on 26 September 2026.

The catalogue sitting behind that is the deepest in British gaming, and the reason is printed on Amazon's own seller price list rather than in its advertising. It takes 15% of the sale price on video games and gaming accessories and 8% on video game consoles — and, unusually, applies no minimum fee to either, where nearly every other category on that list floors at 25p. A seller shipping a £3 thumbstick cover keeps more of the money here than almost anywhere else in Amazon's catalogue, and that is why the cheap end of the accessories shelf runs as deep as it does.

One thing worth knowing before you click: Amazon's product pages refuse automated access, so no figure on this page has been taken from a listing or from another comparison site. Everything below comes from Amazon's seller, newsroom, payments and customer-help hosts, from Ukie's own market valuation, or from a regulator — the Office for Product Safety and Standards, the Games Rating Authority, the Advertising Standards Authority and the Chartered Trading Standards Institute.

Where the 15% goes, and what it does to the shelf

Amazon publishes what it charges the people selling on it, and for this category the numbers are worth reading before the prices are. Video games and gaming accessories carry a 15% referral fee. Video game consoles carry 8%. Both are recorded with the minimum referral fee shown as not applicable — there is no 25p floor, which there is on Consumer Electronics at 7%, on Electronic Accessories at 15% up to £100 and 8% above it, on Toys and Games at 15% and on Computers at 7%. On top of that a seller pays either 75p an item on the Individual plan or £25 a month, excluding VAT, on the Professional plan.

Removing the floor matters more than the headline percentage. On a £1.50 cable clip, 15% is 23p; with a 25p minimum it would be 25p. The difference is two pence, and two pence is the whole margin at that price. It is the clearest single explanation for why a search for a controller grip or a console stand on Amazon returns pages of sub-£5 listings that no high-street shop carries.

Set that against the market Ukie measured. Its UK Games Market Valuation for 2025, published in April 2026, puts the consumer market at £8.76 billion, up 7.4% on £8.158 billion. Game software took £6.03 billion of it, with digital console at £2.49 billion, mobile at £2.07 billion, digital PC at £1.15 billion and boxed games down 1.0% at £319 million. Hardware took £2.17 billion: consoles £811 million and up 12%, gaming PCs £790 million and up 11%, VR £151 million and down 26%.

The line that should shape how you shop is console game accessories: £420 million, down 9%, in a year when the consoles themselves grew 12%. More people bought machines and spent less on the things that plug into them. A falling accessories market with a rising installed base is the condition in which cheap, unbranded, high-volume listings multiply — which is exactly the shape of Amazon's own fee schedule for the category.

The number on the box that Trading Standards can enforce

Most retail categories have no rating system at all. Video games are one of the very few consumer products in Britain where an age label on the packaging is a legal instrument, and it has been since 30 July 2012, when the Video Recordings (Labelling) Regulations 2012 (SI 2012 No. 1767, made 4 July 2012) came into force. Sections 40 and 41 of the Digital Economy Act 2010 removed the general exemption video games had enjoyed and allowed the Video Standards Council — now operating as the Games Rating Authority — to be designated the classification authority for games, with the British Board of Film Classification keeping R18 material and small games carried on discs containing film.

The underlying statute is the Video Recordings Act 1984, revived by the Video Recordings Act 2010 after the original was found never to have been properly notified. It bites on physical media sold, hired, exchanged or lent in England, Scotland and Wales. PEGI 12, 16 and 18 are the enforceable ratings; PEGI 3 and 7 are advisory. Exemptions survive for educational works and for games about sport, religion or music, provided they do not depict to any significant extent violence, sexual content, genital imagery, excretory functions or criminal technique.

The penalties are not nominal. Selling an age-classified game to someone below the rated age carries a fine of up to £5,000 or imprisonment of up to six months, or both. Supplying a game that should have been classified and was not carries an unlimited fine or up to two years. A due diligence defence exists for a trader who took all reasonable precautions, and who can show the breach was down to another person's act or default — which on a marketplace is a sentence worth rereading.

The labelling rules give you a test you can run with nothing but the parcel in your hands. The classification symbol must be white and at least 5 millimetres high, on a red background where the rating is 18 and an orange background where it is lower, and it must appear clearly on one of the largest faces of the case and again on another face. Content descriptor icons may sit beside it, white on black or black on white. Everything must be clearly legible, indelible and not hidden or obscured. Turn the case over. If the symbol is missing, undersized, or printed on the wrong colour, the game in your hands was not labelled the way the law requires, whoever sold it.

For scale: PEGI has rated 39,930 games in total to the end of 2023, and 1,666 in 2023 alone — 435 at PEGI 3 (26.1%), 268 at 7 (16.1%), 488 at 12 (29.3%), 268 at 16 (16.1%) and 207 at 18 (12.4%). Roughly three in five new games carry a rating the law will enforce. One limit worth stating plainly: the Video Recordings Act reaches physical copies, so a download of the same title is outside it entirely.

A wireless controller is electrical equipment before it is a gaming product

The accessories half of this category is governed by safety law rather than content law, and the rules are the Electrical Equipment (Safety) Regulations 2016 and the Plugs and Sockets etc. (Safety) Regulations 1994. A mains-powered console charger, a plug-in headset base or a docking station has to meet them regardless of which seller's name is on the listing. Anything that transmits — a wireless controller, a Bluetooth headset — also falls under the Radio Equipment Regulations 2017 (SI 2017 No. 1206), which carry across the requirements of Directive 2014/53/EU.

The GOV.UK product safety register is free, searchable and shows how those rules are actually used. Searching it for the single word console returned 6 reports on 26 September 2026, and two of them are gaming power supplies rather than consoles. A Games Console Charger, model USG-002, made in China and sold through an online marketplace under listing ID 1054467868, was recalled from end users on 14 August 2023: creepage and clearance distances were insufficient and the product failed the electrical strength test, so accessible metal parts could become live. The Office for Product Safety and Standards notified it and the marketplace removed the listing. An AC Adapter for Xbox One, model PE-2121-03M1, barcode 6900214000121, also made in China, was reported on 12 February 2024 as a serious fire risk: insufficiently earthed, poorly constructed, no fuse, and a clover-leaf plug whose live pins the user can touch while plugging it in. That import was rejected at the border and destroyed, on a notification from local authority Trading Standards.

A note on searching that register yourself, because the search is an OR and small numbers mean more than large ones. The keyword gaming returns 90 reports, almost all of them wooden fishing games and toy sets, and tells you nothing. The keyword console returns 6 and is worth reading line by line. Among the wider gaming results is one piece of furniture: a Trust GXT 705R Ryon gaming chair, recalled on 2 July 2021.

Read six notices in both directions. It is reassuring that so few gaming products have been formally recalled, and it says nothing about listings taken down before anyone tested them. The recurring hazard is not the console or the controller — it is the third-party power supply sold to sit beside it, which is the cheapest item in the transaction and the only one carrying mains voltage.

There is also a duty on the seller that almost nobody claims. Under the WEEE rules, a retailer of electrical and electronic equipment must give customers a way to dispose of the old equivalent item when it sells them a new one, and that applies “regardless of how you sell the products, whether direct or by internet, mail order or telephone”. It can be met in store, by a free alternative take-back service, or by joining the Distributor Take Back Scheme, and the seller must publish on its website which service it offers, how to reuse and recycle, and what the crossed-out wheelie bin symbol means. If you are replacing a controller or a headset, that take-back route exists and is free to use.

Break the seal and the refund goes with it

The general shape of Amazon's returns policy is generous and well known: a statutory 14 days from receipt under regulation 30 of the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, extended voluntarily to 30 days on most things. This category is where the exceptions bite hardest, and Amazon states them in its own words on its customer help pages.

“We won't accept the return of any software returned to us if it's been opened.” Sealed audio or video recordings, and sealed software, are non-returnable “if unsealed by you after delivery”. Digital content that is not supplied on a tangible medium — apps, digital software, eBooks, MP3 — is outside the cancellation right altogether. Amazon.co.uk gift cards are listed as non-returnable items. And the policy reserves the right to refuse a return or charge its fees and expenses where a product is not received in new, unopened condition.

None of that is Amazon being difficult. Regulation 28 of the same 2013 Regulations sets out the exceptions to the 14-day right, and sealed audio recordings, video recordings and computer software unsealed after delivery are on that list, alongside goods made to the customer's specification. The law hands the retailer this one, and every game shop in the country uses it.

The arithmetic is worth doing before you order. A full-price console game at £69.99 that turns out to be the wrong platform, the wrong region or simply the wrong game is worth £69.99 while the cellophane is intact and nothing once it is not. On a marketplace where the seller is often not Amazon, that is a harder position than it looks, because the person you would be negotiating with is a third party with a 48-hour response window rather than a shop with a returns desk. Buy the disc you meant to buy, and open it last.

A rating tells you about the content, not the cost

An age rating answers one question — what is in this game — and is silent on the one that increasingly decides what a game costs. A game rated at any PEGI level may also contain in-game purchases, and where it does, the Games Rating Authority's own explanation is that this “will be indicated by an icon on the box”. The rating itself does not rise because the game sells things. A PEGI 3 title with an open wallet behind it is still a PEGI 3 title.

What exists instead is a voluntary regime. On 18 July 2023 the video games industry agreed 11 principles on paid loot boxes, drawn up by a Technical Working Group of international platforms, publishers and developers that met over ten months from September 2022 to June 2023, alongside a £1 million, three-year public awareness campaign. The principles commit signatories to “make available technological controls to effectively restrict anyone under the age of 18 from acquiring a Loot Box, without the consent or knowledge of a parent, carer or guardian”, to “give clear probability disclosures” so players can see the odds of receiving a given item, to improve access to spending controls, and to take a lenient line on refunds where a child has spent money without a parent knowing. They are commitments, not law.

Advertising is the one part of this with teeth, and the enforcement is recent. The Advertising Standards Authority's guidance on advertising in-game purchases was published in September 2021 and reviewed in May 2024. On 24 June 2026 it upheld a complaint against Microsoft over two digital posters for Xbox Game Pass seen on 13 December 2025, which showed identifiable gameplay from Tony Hawk's Pro Skater 3 + 4 and Call of Duty: Black Ops 7 without saying those games contained in-game purchasing. The ruling breached CAP Code rules 3.1 and 3.3, and its reasoning was that the omission “was material to a consumer's transactional decision”. The complaint came from a single academic researcher in game regulation.

That ruling is about a publisher's advertising, not a retailer's, and it is here because it is the clearest available statement of what the label on a box is not doing. If a game in a child's basket carries the in-game purchases icon, the useful next step is the platform's family spending controls, not the age rating.

The age check Amazon publishes — and the one it does not

Selling an 18-rated game online raises a question the Chartered Trading Standards Institute answers bluntly through its Business Companion guidance: a seller “must have effective systems in place for preventing sales to customers who are underage”, and the things that do not count as such a system are named. Relying on the purchaser confirming they are over the minimum age does not count. Asking the purchaser simply to provide a date of birth does not count. Tick boxes, disclaimers and payment-method checks do not count on their own. What is expected is a risk analysis of the business to find the weak points, measures against them, and monitoring to keep them working. The guidance also states that age-restricted products “must not be delivered to self-service lockers, as it would affect the due diligence defence”. The old reference point, PAS 1296, has been withdrawn in favour of BS ISO/IEC 27566-1:2025 on age-assurance systems.

Amazon does publish an age-restricted items policy, and what it contains is specific. The items it names are “alcoholic beverages, such as beer and wine, e-cigarettes and bladed items”, with a threshold of 18 or over. For bladed items the check is a UK passport, an international passport or a UK driving licence together with a selfie, a process it says usually takes 30 to 45 seconds. At the door, “the delivery driver is required to request and record a year of birth and record that the customer is visibly over 25 years old”, with photo ID required if they are not, and age-restricted items cannot be left with a neighbour.

Video games are not mentioned on that page, and neither is PEGI. That is a gap in what is published rather than proof of a gap in practice, and it should be read that way: Amazon may well apply a check at checkout that it has not described on the help page this review could reach, and its own product pages are closed to automated access. What can be said is that the page a customer is pointed to lists three categories of goods and not the fourth that Parliament made criminal to sell to a child. If it matters to you — if you are buying a PEGI 18 game on an account a teenager can reach, or having one delivered to a locker — that is worth a question to customer services before the order, and the answer would be worth having in writing.

Renting instead of owning: what Luna actually includes

Amazon's own gaming product is Luna, and it is the cheapest way into the category because the entry tier is bundled. Luna Standard is included with a Prime membership and carries more than 50 titles, among them Fortnite, Hogwarts Legacy, Kingdom Come: Deliverance II and TopSpin 2K25. Above it, Luna Premium is £8.99 a month, a Ubisoft+ channel with more than 45 Ubisoft games is £14.99 a month, and a Jackbox Games channel with more than 50 party titles is £3.99 a month. GameNight is a separate collection of more than 25 local multiplayer games, and the only part of Luna that does not need a controller or a mouse and keyboard.

It runs on supported Windows PCs, Macs, Fire TV, Fire tablets, Chromebooks, iPhone, iPad, Android devices and selected Samsung and LG smart televisions, and Amazon recommends a sustained 10Mbps for the best experience. The Luna Controller costs £59.99, which makes it the most expensive single item in the cheapest route into Amazon's gaming range — the subscription is free with Prime and the hardware is not.

Two things follow. A cloud library is a rental: nothing in Luna Standard belongs to the player, the list changes, and a title that goes leaves with it. And a 10Mbps sustained connection is a real requirement rather than a suggestion, which for a household on a congested evening connection is the difference between the service working and the money being wasted. Against a £69.99 disc that keeps its value until it is opened, a bundled subscription at no extra cost is an easy first step and a poor place to build a collection.

Common questions

Is a PEGI rating legally binding in the UK?

PEGI 12, 16 and 18 are. They became enforceable on 30 July 2012, when the Video Recordings (Labelling) Regulations 2012 came into force under powers in sections 40 and 41 of the Digital Economy Act 2010, on top of the Video Recordings Act 1984 as revived in 2010. Selling a game to someone under the rated age carries a fine of up to £5,000 or six months' imprisonment, or both, and supplying a game that should have been classified and was not carries an unlimited fine or up to two years. PEGI 3 and 7 are advisory. The Act covers physical copies, so a download of the same game sits outside it.

Can I return a game or a piece of software to Amazon once I have opened it?

No. Amazon's own policy says “We won't accept the return of any software returned to us if it's been opened”, and sealed audio recordings, video recordings and sealed software are non-returnable once unsealed after delivery. Digital content not supplied on a disc, and Amazon.co.uk gift cards, are non-returnable as well. This follows regulation 28 of the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013, which lists those exceptions to the 14-day cancellation right that regulation 30 otherwise gives you. Unopened, the ordinary window applies — 14 days by statute and 30 days by Amazon's own policy on most goods.

How can I tell whether a game has been labelled properly?

Look at the case. The classification symbol must be white and at least 5 millimetres high, set on a red background where the rating is 18 and an orange background for every lower rating, and it has to appear clearly on one of the largest faces of the case and again on another face. Content descriptor icons may sit alongside it, white on black or black on white. All of it must be legible, indelible and not hidden or obscured. A missing, undersized or wrongly coloured symbol means the copy in front of you was not labelled the way the 2012 Regulations require.

What does Luna cost, and do I own the games?

Luna Standard is included with Prime and carries more than 50 titles. Luna Premium is £8.99 a month, a Ubisoft+ channel with more than 45 games is £14.99 a month and a Jackbox Games channel with more than 50 titles is £3.99 a month. The Luna Controller is £59.99, and most of the service needs a controller or a mouse and keyboard — the exception is the GameNight collection of more than 25 local multiplayer games. Amazon recommends a sustained 10Mbps. Nothing is owned: the library is Amazon's to change, and access depends on the subscription continuing.

Who checks that a gaming accessory is safe?

Mains-powered accessories fall under the Electrical Equipment (Safety) Regulations 2016 and the Plugs and Sockets etc. (Safety) Regulations 1994, and anything that transmits also falls under the Radio Equipment Regulations 2017. Enforcement is by the Office for Product Safety and Standards and local authority Trading Standards, and their notices are published free on GOV.UK. Searching that register for the word console returned 6 reports on 26 September 2026, two of them third-party gaming power supplies — one recalled from end users on 14 August 2023 because accessible metal parts could become live, the other stopped at the border on 12 February 2024 with no fuse and touchable live pins.

Does Amazon verify age when you buy an 18-rated game?

Its published age-restricted items page names alcoholic drinks, e-cigarettes and bladed items, and does not mention video games or PEGI. For bladed items the check is a passport or UK driving licence plus a selfie, taking 30 to 45 seconds, and at delivery the driver records a year of birth and that the customer is visibly over 25, with photo ID required otherwise and no leaving the parcel with a neighbour. Nothing reachable states whether an equivalent check applies to a PEGI 18 game, and Trading Standards guidance is explicit that self-declaration and a date-of-birth box are not enough on their own. It is worth asking customer services before ordering.

Our verdict

As a place to buy gaming hardware and accessories, Amazon's advantages are structural rather than promotional: a 15% referral fee with no minimum on games and accessories, 8% on consoles, and no price floor at all, in a category where console accessories sales fell 9% to £420 million in 2025 while console sales rose 12%. That combination is why the shelf is deep and why so much of it is cheap, and it is also why the mains-powered end of it deserves a look at the plug: both of the gaming power supplies on the GOV.UK safety register were third-party accessories, not manufacturer parts. For games themselves the decisive facts are legal rather than commercial. PEGI 12, 16 and 18 are enforceable ratings, the symbol has to be white, at least 5 millimetres high and on two of the largest faces of the case, and opening the seal ends the right to a refund. Turn the case over before you open it, and check the rating is printed the way the 2012 Regulations require. The one thing this review could not settle is how Amazon verifies age on a PEGI 18 game — its published age-restriction page names alcohol, e-cigarettes and bladed items and stops there — so if that matters in your household, ask before you order rather than after.
