Amazon Health and Beauty: Fees, Returns and Who Is Answerable for What Is in the Bottle

Rates and terms checked 27 September 2026 · Health and Beauty · Compare100 editorial team

Amazon gives you 30 days to change your mind on a beauty order, against the 14 days the law actually requires, and repeat deliveries on a Subscribe & Save order carry free standard delivery to the UK and Ireland whether or not you pay for Prime. Both figures come from Amazon's own customer help pages, read on 27 September 2026. The next site-wide discount window is already dated: Prime Big Deal Days runs from 12:01am BST on 6 October 2026 to 11:59pm on 7 October, 48 hours, with beauty named as one of the six categories in it.

The range behind that is enormous and it is cheap to stock, which is the reason it is enormous. Amazon takes 8% of a beauty, health or personal care sale priced at or below £10 and 15% above it, with a 25p floor per item. That is the second-lowest band the company publishes for any consumer category, so a £5.99 shampoo is worth a seller's attention in a way it would not be at a flat 15%.

One mechanic decides more beauty arguments than any other and it is worth knowing before you order rather than after. Amazon's returns policy excludes "products which are not suitable for return due to health protection or hygienic reasons if unsealed by you after delivery". A sealed serum can go back inside 30 days. The same serum, opened, cannot go back at all, and that is the law rather than a shop rule.

What a beauty seller pays, and why the shelf is so deep

Amazon publishes its referral fees openly, and the beauty band is unusually kind to cheap products. Beauty, Health and Personal Care attracts 8% on any item priced at or below £10 and 15% on anything above, with a 25p minimum referral fee per item sold. Sitting on top of that is either the Professional selling plan at £25 a month excluding VAT, or the Individual plan at 75p per item sold, also excluding VAT.

Work that through and the shape of the category explains itself. On a £7.99 hand cream the referral fee is 64p. On the same cream priced at £10.99 it is £1.65 — a jump of a pound in Amazon's favour for three pounds of extra price. The step at £10 is the reason so much of the beauty listing sits at £9.99 and below, and the reason multipacks appear at prices that cross the threshold in one go rather than creeping over it.

Subscribe & Save is the other lever, and it is worth being precise about what Amazon does and does not commit to. Every repeat delivery carries free standard delivery to the UK and Ireland, and a non-Prime customer may still pay on the first order if it sits below the free delivery threshold. The discount itself is not a published rate: Amazon says only that "the current discount for each item is posted on the product detail page when you sign up" and that it may rise or fall with daily pricing. Vouchers cannot be applied to an existing subscription, some items carry quantity limits, and Prime-Exclusive items need a Prime membership before the subscription will take at all. You can cancel at any time.

Prime itself is £8.99 a month or £95 a year, with a 30-day free trial, and £4.49 a month or £47.49 a year for students and 18 to 24 year olds on a six-month trial. For a beauty basket that is a delivery decision rather than a discount one, because the Subscribe & Save delivery benefit does not depend on it.

The name on the label that decides who is answerable

Every cosmetic sold in Great Britain has to have one identifiable company standing behind it, called the responsible person, and that company's name and address is required on the container or the packaging. It is the single most useful thing to look for on a delivered bottle, because it tells you which firm is legally on the hook for what is inside and which enforcement authority can knock on its door.

Behind that name sits a document most shoppers never hear of. Under Article 10 of the retained cosmetics regulation, a cosmetic product safety report must exist before the product goes on sale, and it has to be signed off by an assessor holding "a diploma or other evidence of formal qualifications awarded on completion of a university course of theoretical and practical study in pharmacy, toxicology, medicine or a similar discipline". Article 11 then requires the product information file — the safety report, the quantitative composition, the manufacturing method, the proof of any claimed effect and the animal testing record — to be kept for ten years after the last batch was placed on the market, in English, readily accessible at the address the responsible person notified to government. Article 13 requires that notification through the Submit Cosmetic Product Notifications service before a single unit is sold.

Two provisions matter especially on a marketplace. A distributor stops being merely a distributor and becomes the responsible person itself the moment it sells a product under its own name or trademark, or modifies one already on the market — so an own-label listing changes who is answerable, even where the factory is the same. And Article 23 puts the duty to report a serious undesirable effect on "the responsible person and distributors", without delay, naming the product and any corrective measure taken. The reporting address is a government mailbox, seriousundesirableeffects@businessandtrade.gov.uk, and enforcement itself sits with local authority Trading Standards, with the Office for Product Safety and Standards as the competent authority alongside the Secretary of State.

There is a deadline worth diarising if you buy imported brands. EU responsible person details may be used on GB labelling until 31 December 2027, after which UK responsible person details have to replace them. Until then a label naming a company in Warsaw or Milan is compliant; after that date it will not be, and the brands least likely to notice are the ones with no UK office to notice on their behalf.

What the safety register actually shows about beauty

There is no Defaqto for shampoo and no ombudsman for mascara, so the nearest thing to an independent scoreboard in this category is the government's own product safety register. Searched on 27 September 2026 it returns 182 entries for the keyword "cosmetic". A narrower search on a single banned ingredient, hydroquinone, returns 51 — and every one of the ten on the first page is classified as a serious risk.

Reading those titles in a row tells you where the danger concentrates. RODIS Creme Skin Light, Fair & White So White Skin Perfector Serum, Purec Egyptian Magic Whitening Face & Body Lotion, SWP+ Skin Whitening Body Lotion, Caro White Lightening Beauty Lotion, Cocopulp Skin Lightening Cream, Jaina Beauty Lightening Face Cream, Golden Pearl Beauty Cream, Angel Cosmetics Clairmen Lightening Program for Men. Skin-lightening preparations dominate the serious-risk end of the cosmetic register almost to the exclusion of everything else.

One entry shows what "serious" means in laboratory numbers. Parley Cosmetics Goldie Advanced Beauty Cream, reference 2508-0034a, recalled on 12 August 2025, was found to contain mercury at 16,680 mg/kg — 1.7% of the product — together with the corticosteroid clobetasol propionate at 1,470 mg/kg, or 0.15%. Mercury is prohibited in cosmetics outright. That consignment was rejected at the border and destroyed, which is the best possible outcome and also the one that depends entirely on the shipment being intercepted rather than posted.

The register also carries a very recent entry with Amazon's name on it. On 16 September 2026, eleven days before this page was written, Baylis & Harding PLC recalled the one-litre eco refill of its oud, cedar and amber hand wash, reference 2609-0060, batch codes 6210A and 6211A printed on the base of the pouch. Testing found Pluralibacter gergoviae, which "may lead to infection or adverse health effects, particularly in vulnerable users". The breach cited is of the cosmetics regulation itself. The product was made in the United Kingdom, the risk level is recorded as low, it was notified by local authority Trading Standards, and the retailers listed are Ocado, Sainsbury's, Waitrose and Amazon. Customers are told to stop using it immediately and return it to the place of purchase for redress.

That is worth reading carefully rather than as an accusation. A recall naming four large retailers is a recall of a British manufacturer's product that reached all four; it is evidence that the system found a contamination problem and published it, and Amazon appears there for the same reason three supermarkets do. What it does establish is that a brand being familiar, British and sold by household-name grocers is not on its own a safety argument, and that the batch code on the base of the bottle is the thing that decides whether yours is affected.

Peroxide, and the one percentage on a whitening kit that matters

Tooth whitening is the corner of health and beauty where the law draws a hard line at a number, and it is the corner where marketplace listings most often sit on the wrong side of it. A product sold to the public for home use may contain no more than 0.1% hydrogen peroxide. Between that and 6% the product may only be supplied through a dentist. Above 6% it has no lawful consumer route at all.

The scale of the gap between that rule and what is actually listed online was measured by Which?, whose findings the British Dental Association publicised. Of 36 whitening products bought from online marketplaces, 21 contained more hydrogen peroxide than the legal limit allows and 11 contained more than 6%. Two syringes bought through AliExpress came in at around 30% — described as roughly 300 times the amount that can lawfully be sold to a consumer. Paul Woodhouse of the BDA board put it as plainly as it can be put: "Hydrogen peroxide is a seriously strong chemical and not to be messed with."

Two caveats belong with those numbers. That testing was published in May 2021, so it is five years old and the sample was drawn across several marketplaces rather than from Amazon alone. No equivalent study of comparable size has been published since that a search will surface, which is itself worth saying. The 0.1% and 6% thresholds, on the other hand, have not moved, and they are printable on a box — which makes the concentration on the packaging a test any reader can apply in the time it takes to read a listing.

The same logic runs through the rest of the category. The retained regulation works by lists: Annex II names the substances a cosmetic may not contain at all, Annex III those allowed only within stated restrictions, Annex IV colourants, Annex V preservatives and Annex VI UV filters. A product outside those lists is not a matter of opinion or of house style. It is unlawful, and the ingredient panel is where it shows.

Sending it back, and the seal that ends the argument

Amazon's stated position is better than the legal minimum and then stops abruptly. The voluntary window is 30 days from receipt, where the statutory cancellation right is 14. Return postage, where free returns do not apply, starts at £1.99 within the UK and may be deducted from the refund — unless the item was defective, damaged, wrong or delivered late, in which case it should not be.

The exclusions are where beauty differs from everything else Amazon sells. The policy excludes "products which are not suitable for return due to health protection or hygienic reasons if unsealed by you after delivery, or which are, after delivery, inseparably mixed with other items", and separately excludes "goods which may deteriorate or expire rapidly". Together those two lines cover most of a beauty basket the moment it is opened. This mirrors the hygiene exception in consumer contracts law rather than inventing anything, and the practical consequence is worth stating flatly: the cellophane and the little sticker on the lid are not packaging, they are the boundary of your right to change your mind.

There is a narrower rule that catches fragrance and aerosols. On Luxury Stores listings, Amazon states that "beauty products made of hazardous materials, including flammable liquids or gases, are not returnable to Amazon". An eau de parfum is a flammable liquid. A shopper spending three figures on a scent should read that line before ordering rather than after deciding it does not suit them.

What Amazon does not publish anywhere reachable is a minimum shelf life on delivery. A cosmetic with a durability of less than 30 months has to carry a best-before date, and one above that carries the open-jar period-after-opening symbol instead; a distributor is separately forbidden from supplying a product whose minimum durability date has passed. None of that tells you how much of the life will be left when the parcel arrives, and no undertaking on that point could be found on the help pages, the product pages or the seller programme material.

Where a beauty complaint goes when the seller stops replying

Financial products have the Financial Ombudsman Service. Furniture has an ombudsman of its own. Beauty retail has an approved dispute scheme, and the interesting part is who has joined it.

RetailADR, run by Consumer Dispute Resolution Limited, is approved by government under the Alternative Dispute Resolution for Consumer Disputes (Competent Authorities and Information) Regulations 2015 and by the Chartered Trading Standards Institute. It is free to consumers, and between 1 January and 1 September 2026 it received 1,014 claims, accepted 905, published 793 determinations and turned cases round in an average of 21 days. Its published member list includes ALDI, ASOS, IKEA, Harrods, Poundland, Oliver Bonas, Richer Sounds, Topshop, Virgin Active and around forty others.

Checked on 27 September 2026, no Amazon entity appears on that list. Neither does Superdrug, Lookfantastic, Cult Beauty or The Body Shop; the only Boots name on it is Boots Kitchen Appliances, which is a different business from the chemist. Membership is voluntary, so this is not a rule anyone has broken — but it does mean that a beauty dispute Amazon declines to resolve has no independent adjudicator behind it, and that the A-to-z process and the card chargeback are the realistic routes rather than a first step towards one.

Two other doors are worth knowing. Product safety concerns — an unlabelled cream, a suspected banned ingredient, a reaction — go to Trading Standards and to OPSS, which is the body that publishes the register quoted above and which receives serious undesirable effect reports directly. And if the order includes a medicine rather than a cosmetic, the MHRA keeps a register of websites authorised to sell medicines online, searchable by name, company number or domain. The registration duty described in the government guidance is framed around selling medicines to the public in Northern Ireland, and an application can take up to 90 working days to process; how far that register covers a Great Britain shopper's purchase is a point this page could not settle from the published guidance and one to confirm with the MHRA directly.

The compliance paperwork Amazon asks its sellers for

Amazon runs a product compliance service for sellers and names "cosmetics and medicinal products" among the regulated areas it covers, alongside toys, electricals, chemicals, medical devices and food. The concrete mechanism it describes is narrower than the list implies: it collects responsible person details for non-EU brands and ASINs fulfilled through Fulfilment by Amazon, via a seller dashboard, on the basis that "since July 16 2021, it has been illegal to sell CE-marked products in the EU without a Responsible Person in the EU".

Note what that is and is not. It is an EU-facing requirement built around CE marking, gathered for products Amazon itself warehouses. Cosmetics are not CE-marked, the GB responsible person duty is a separate regime with its own register, and the pages carry Amazon's own disclaimer that they do "not constitute any legal or other compliance advice from Amazon". Whether a cosmetics seller is required to hold a signed safety report and a product information file before listing is therefore settled by the regulation, not by the marketplace — and no requirement to produce either before a beauty listing goes live could be found in Amazon's published seller material.

For a shopper that has one practical consequence. The document trail that makes a cosmetic lawful sits with the responsible person, and the responsible person is identified on the label rather than on the listing page. If the bottle that arrives carries no UK name and address at all, the problem is not the shop's returns policy. It is that nobody in this country has taken the legal responsibility for what is inside it, and Trading Standards is the right recipient for that rather than customer services.

Common questions

Can I return make-up or skincare to Amazon after opening it?

No. Amazon's returns policy excludes products that are not suitable for return for health protection or hygiene reasons once you have unsealed them after delivery, and separately excludes goods that may deteriorate or expire rapidly. Sealed items can go back inside the 30-day voluntary window, and the statutory cancellation right is 14 days. Return postage starts at £1.99 within the UK where free returns do not apply, though it should not be charged if the item was defective, damaged, wrong or delivered late.

How do I tell whether a cosmetic sold on Amazon is legal to sell here?

Look on the bottle or the box for the name and address of the UK responsible person. That company has to exist, has to have commissioned a cosmetic product safety report signed by a qualified assessor, and has to keep a product information file in English for ten years after the last batch was sold. EU responsible person details may still be used on GB labelling until 31 December 2027. A cosmetic arriving with no UK name and address at all is a matter for Trading Standards rather than for customer services.

How much hydrogen peroxide is a home teeth whitening kit allowed to contain?

0.1%. Between 0.1% and 6% a whitening product may only be supplied through a dentist, and above 6% there is no lawful consumer route at all. When Which? tested 36 whitening products bought from online marketplaces, 21 exceeded the legal limit and 11 contained more than 6%, with two AliExpress syringes at around 30%. That testing was published in May 2021 and covered several marketplaces rather than Amazon alone, but the thresholds have not changed and the concentration is printed on the packaging.

What does Amazon charge a seller on a beauty product?

8% of the total price on items at or below £10 and 15% above that, with a minimum referral fee of 25p per item. Sellers also pay either £25 a month on the Professional plan or 75p per item on the Individual plan, both excluding VAT. The step at £10 is sharp: the fee on a £7.99 item is 64p and on a £10.99 item £1.65.

Who can I complain to if Amazon will not resolve a beauty order?

There is no ombudsman for beauty retail, and no Amazon entity appears on the member list of RetailADR, the CTSI-approved retail dispute scheme, checked on 27 September 2026. That leaves Amazon's own A-to-z process and a card chargeback for the money side. For anything about the product itself — a missing label, a suspected banned ingredient, a reaction — the routes are local authority Trading Standards and the Office for Product Safety and Standards, which publishes the product safety register and receives serious undesirable effect reports.

Our verdict

Amazon's beauty proposition is a wide, cheap shelf with a returns policy that beats the legal minimum by a fortnight and then hits a wall at the hygiene seal. The 8% referral fee below £10 is why the range runs so deep at low prices, and Subscribe & Save's free repeat delivery is a real benefit even without Prime — though the discount attached to it is never committed to as a number. The part worth taking seriously is provenance rather than price. A cosmetic is lawful because one identifiable company has signed a safety report and keeps a product information file for ten years, and that company is named on the label, not on the listing. With 182 cosmetic entries on the OPSS register, 51 of them turning on a single banned ingredient, and one September 2026 recall that names Amazon alongside three supermarkets, the ten seconds it takes to look for a UK responsible person and a batch code on the bottle is the most useful thing a shopper can do here.
