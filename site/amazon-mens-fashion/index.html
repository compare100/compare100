Amazon Men's Fashion: Free Returns, Fibre Labels and Who You Are Buying From

Rates and terms checked 27 September 2026 · Men's Fashion · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Amazon pays the postage when men's clothes go back. Clothing, footwear, handbags, jewellery and watches flagged Free Return at checkout come with a prepaid label and 30 days from delivery, with no minimum order — checked on 27 September 2026 against Amazon's own returns pages. Anything else it sells starts at £1.99 for a UK return, taken out of the refund.

That is worth more in menswear than in most categories, because sizing is the thing nobody gets right first time. A jacket that turns up a size out costs nothing to send back here, against the £1.99 Amazon charges on a return that was not flagged as free. The condition is strict rather than hidden: new and unworn, with the original packaging, the tags and any certificate of authenticity still attached, and the free label has to have been offered on that listing when you bought it — on a site where a large share of the stock belongs to third-party sellers rather than to Amazon, it is not offered on everything.

The other thing worth knowing before you click is where the reliable information about a garment actually sits. It is not in the listing copy, which the seller writes. It is on the label stitched into the side seam, and what has to be printed there is set by statute — fibre content by percentage, in English, on something durable enough to survive a wash. The same is true of the pictograms inside a shoe. This page is about reading those, and about the points where Amazon's own rules pick the job up and the points where they put it down.

The label in the side seam is the one document that has to be true

Listing copy on a marketplace is written by whoever owns the stock. The composition label is written to a statute. The Textile Products (Labelling and Fibre Composition) Regulations 2012 (SI 2012/1102) bring the assimilated EU Regulation 1007/2011 into UK law, and they catch anything made of textile fibres plus anything that is at least 80% textile fibre by weight. A shirt, a suit, a knitted tie and the fabric part of an umbrella are all in. The duty falls on the manufacturer or the importer into the UK, which on Amazon usually means the seller rather than Amazon.

What the label has to say is more prescriptive than most shoppers realise. Where two or more fibres make up 85% of the finished garment, each is named with its percentage — cotton 80%, polyester 15%, nylon 5%. The word pure is reserved for a single-fibre garment, and a fibre name cannot be borrowed to flatter a texture, so silk acetate on an acetate shirt is unlawful rather than merely optimistic. Decorative matter accounting for 7% or less is left out of the calculation, which is why an embroidered motif does not appear in the numbers. Where a garment has components of different composition — a jacket and its lining being the obvious case — each component is declared separately, so a single figure on a lined coat is a sign the label is wrong.

Two more lines are worth hunting for. Only the fibre names in Annex I of the assimilated Regulation may be used, which is why you see elastane and not a brand name. And where any part of the item comes from an animal but is not a textile — a leather trim, a horn button, a fur collar — the wording contains non-textile parts of animal origin has to appear. That phrase is the one a shopper buying a wool coat with a trimmed hood should look for, because mislabelling real fur as fake is an offence in itself, and selling, importing or exporting cat or dog fur is prohibited outright under separate 2008 regulations.

The label has to be durable, easily legible, visible and accessible, and it has to be in English. Around forty categories are exempt in Annex VI — buttons, artificial flowers, flags, travel goods, toys, make-up cases, disposable articles and protective sports requisites other than gloves — and multipacks of identical items may be labelled once on the packaging rather than individually. Enforcement is a Trading Standards matter: regulation 11 puts the duty on local weights and measures authorities, regulation 5 creates the offence of making a non-compliant product available on the UK market, and regulation 6 sets the punishment at a fine up to the statutory maximum on summary conviction or an unlimited fine on indictment. There is no prison sentence attached. Home workers and self-employed tailors making customised garments sit outside the offence, which is a sensible carve-out and also a gap on a marketplace.

Shoes are labelled in three parts, and 80% is the number that decides

Footwear has its own rulebook and it is older: the Footwear (Indication of Composition) Labelling Regulations 1995 (SI 1995/2489), covering England, Scotland and Wales. Every pair sold new has to declare the material of three separate parts — the upper, the lining and sock, and the outer sole — and the test is coverage rather than weight. The label names whichever material accounts for 80% of the surface area of that part. Where nothing reaches 80%, the two main materials have to be given instead, which is the honest answer on most modern trainers.

There are four material categories, each with its own pictogram: leather, coated leather, textile and the catch-all other materials. The distinction between the first two is the one that costs money. A shoe carrying the coated-leather symbol is not the same product as one carrying the leather symbol, and the pictogram says so where the listing photograph will not. The label may be printed, stuck, embossed or attached, it only has to appear on one shoe of the pair, and English or the pictograms are both acceptable. In a shop there must be a notice explaining what the symbols mean; for a distance sale the consumer has to be clearly informed of the same thing, which is a requirement an online listing meets far less often than a shop window does.

Four things fall outside the regulations altogether: second-hand footwear, protective footwear, anything containing asbestos, and children's play footwear for under-14s. Manufacturers and importers carry the labelling duty, and a retailer is expected to check compliance before selling and keep a record of having checked. Trading Standards enforces, and a misleading indication can also be attacked under the unfair trading provisions now sitting in the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024.

Leather has a chemical ceiling, and two pairs of gloves went past it

The least-known rule in menswear is a limit on a chemical. Chromium salts are what turn hide into leather, and the tanning process can leave behind chromium VI, a skin sensitiser. Entry 47 of Annex XVII to REACH caps it at under 3 mg/kg — three parts per million — in any leather article that comes into contact with the skin. Shoes, belts, gloves, watch straps and jacket trim are all inside that description. The industry test is EN ISO 17075, and part 2 of it, using high performance liquid chromatography, is the method recommended for dyed leather because the dye can otherwise mask the result.

It is not a theoretical limit. A search of the OPSS product safety register for chromium returned nine entries on 27 September 2026, and two of them are gloves. McCalifornia women's brown leather gloves were recalled on 5 February 2021 at serious risk. Ashwood fine leather gloves, style D8906, red with a faux fur lining and sold through TK Maxx, were recalled on 9 April 2021, also at serious risk, for chromium VI in the leather above the legal maximum; the entry was published on 25 March 2022, names the REACH Enforcement Regulations 2008 as the breach, records the country of origin as unknown and shows the recall as voluntary, notified by local authority Trading Standards.

Two points follow for anyone buying leather online. The country of origin being unrecorded on a formal recall tells you how thin the paper trail behind a leather good can be. And both of those recalls came from named businesses with a UK address to write to, which is exactly why they could be recalled at all.

What 248 entries on the recall register do and do not tell you

The Office for Product Safety and Standards runs a free, searchable register of alerts, reports and recalls at gov.uk, and almost nobody looking at a clothing listing thinks to use it. Searched on 27 September 2026, it returned 248 entries for clothing, 135 for textile and only 5 for footwear. Those totals cover several years and every retailer in the country, which is the first thing to hold on to: a clothing recall in Britain is rare.

The detail is more useful than the totals. Under textile the register carries an Ayan Textile hoodcord jacket recorded at serious risk on 12 February 2024, an Asda ladies' handbag range at serious risk on 2 September 2026, Primark purses and children's shorts, Mint Velvet slingback heels through Next in December 2024, and the La Redoute Alanis printed bathrobe on 15 September 2026. Under clothing, an AOOWU mermaid princess dress was reported at serious risk on 15 September 2026 with Amazon recorded as the seller.

Read that last one in both directions, because it deserves it. A serious-risk entry naming the shop you are about to buy from is not a reassuring sentence. It is also evidence that the system found the product, published it and can be searched by anyone in about thirty seconds — and Asda, Primark, Next and Morrisons are all on the same register for the same reason. What the register cannot show is the listing that was quietly pulled before anyone tested it, which on a marketplace of third-party sellers is the population that matters. Two searches by brand name before you order is a habit worth having.

Since March 2024 a 'responsible' range has to show its working

Menswear marketing leans hard on words like sustainable, conscious and responsible, and since 2024 those words have carried a regulatory cost. The Competition and Markets Authority opened an investigation into environmental claims by ASOS, Boohoo and George at Asda on 29 July 2022 and closed it on 27 March 2024 by taking formal undertakings from all three. The undertakings are specific: green claims must be accurate with the key information clear and prominent, fabric claims such as organic or recycled must state the actual percentage, any named environmental range — ASOS's Responsible edit, Boohoo's Ready for the Future, Asda's George for Good — must publish the criteria a product has to meet to be in it, search filters must genuinely match what the garments are made of, natural imagery must not be used to overstate a benefit, target-based claims need a verifiable strategy behind them, and accreditation logos must make the scope of the certification clear. Each of the three reports to the CMA on compliance.

The CMA followed up on 18 September 2024 with guidance addressed to the whole fashion retail sector. It deliberately sets very few numeric thresholds — it does not say what percentage of recycled content earns the word recycled — and instead requires the figure itself to be shown, gives at least 50% recycled content as an example of a filter label that works, and warns that broad terms are far more likely to be inaccurate than specific ones. Amazon was not one of the three firms investigated, and it is fair to say so. The guidance applies to the sector rather than to those companies, the unfair trading rules behind it now sit in the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, and the practical test for a shopper is the same either way: if the filter says recycled and the composition label does not carry a percentage, the claim has nothing under it.

Free returns are paid for twice, and only one of the prices is published

The free return is the best thing on this page and it is worth being precise about how it works. Amazon's voluntary policy gives 30 days from receipt to send most things back unused and undamaged, which sits on top of the 14-day statutory cancellation right rather than replacing it. Where the listing was marked eligible for a free return at the time of purchase, Amazon supplies a printable prepaid label and carries the cost on items it sold or dispatched; on a seller-fulfilled order the seller provides the label and pays. Where the listing was not marked eligible, the cost of the label comes off the refund, starting at £1.99 within the UK and £8.09 internationally — unless the item was defective, damaged, wrong or delivered late, in which case nothing is deducted. Hand-delivered returns are refused, and returning an eligible and an ineligible item in one parcel can still cost you postage on the ineligible one.

Somebody is paying for all of that, and on Amazon it is the seller. Amazon charges a returns processing fee on customer-returned goods in precisely the categories where it offers free return shipping — Clothing, Shoes and Handbags — and explicitly not on jewellery, watches or luggage, which get the free return without the fee attached. Amazon does not publish the amount of that fee anywhere a shopper can read it, which makes the true cost of a generous returns policy invisible in the price of the shirt.

The seller side has one more asymmetry a menswear shopper can use. Amazon's published referral fees put footwear on a flat 15% with a 25p minimum and no low-price band at all, while clothing and accessories are tiered so that cheap items are charged least, with the schedule last changed on 15 December 2025. Run the arithmetic on two £60 purchases fulfilled by Amazon and they are not taxed alike: a £60 jacket is charged 15% on the first £40 and 7% on the rest, coming to £7.40, while a £60 pair of shoes is charged 15% flat, or £9.00. That £1.60 gap is one reason the cheap end of Amazon's clothing is so deep and its footwear is comparatively thin. Sellers also pay £25 a month excluding VAT on the Professional plan or 75p per item as an Individual, and £0.62 to £1.51 per cubic foot a month to store stock, which is what a bulky winter coat costs to sit unsold through a warm November.

Who stitched it, and what Amazon says it checks

Garment supply chains are the reason the Modern Slavery Act 2015 exists, so the section 54 statement is a fair document to hold Amazon to. Its statement for the year to 31 December 2025, approved and signed in 2026 by UK country manager John Boumphrey, is made on behalf of 13 UK subsidiaries. On training it is detailed: 68,000 Amazon employees reached by e-learning during 2025, 2,500 suppliers trained through the same platforms, 24 in-person workshops across 13 countries engaging 253 suppliers, and 38 third-party courses made available.

On remediation it puts real money on the page, which is rarer. It records $340,632 returned to workers during the 2025 phase of a recruitment-fee repayment programme, 146 further workers reimbursed in a second phase, and 847 workers repaid a combined total of more than $2.2 million. Its audit findings are given as rates rather than counts: incomplete employment information under 1%, ineffectively communicated employment information 1.77%, mandatory overtime under 1%, worker-paid recruitment fees under 1% and document retention failures under 1%.

The limitation is worth stating plainly rather than dressing up. Every one of those figures is group-wide, covering a business that sells servers and groceries as well as shirts, and nothing in the statement is broken out for apparel or textiles specifically. A reader trying to find out about the factory that made a particular £12 polo shirt will not get there from this document. It is more than most marketplaces publish and it is still not an answer about clothes.

No used menswear, and one storefront that skips men entirely

Two absences are worth knowing about before you go looking. The first is second-hand. Amazon's own guidance to clothing sellers states that used or refurbished clothing cannot be sold on the site, and that only authentic apparel may be listed, with permission obtained from the retailer, wholesaler, manufacturer or designer first. That rule is why the counterfeit problem in clothing is smaller here than on some rivals, and it is also why the resale market that has taken over menswear elsewhere has no presence at all on Amazon.

The second is more specific. Amazon does run a pre-owned designer storefront in Europe: on 6 February 2024 it added Hardly Ever Worn It, a family-run resale business founded in 2012 and shipping to more than 40 countries, to Luxury Stores for customers in the UK, Germany, Spain and Italy, with a pre-screening process for authenticity. Its range is women's ready-to-wear, shoes, accessories, jewellery and watches. There is no equivalent for men.

Amazon's seller guidance is prescriptive in one way that does help menswear shoppers: it requires professionally shot, high-resolution photographs of the actual garment from multiple angles, on a model or a mannequin rather than on a hanger, with close-ups of details such as buttons and cuffs, and it requires a size chart giving the dimensions of the item in several international standards. Where a listing has none of those, it is not meeting the standard Amazon sets for it, and that is a reasonable reason to move on to the next one.

Where it wins

  • Return postage is paid on clothing, footwear, handbags, jewellery and watches marked Free Return, with no minimum order and 30 days to decide
  • 30-day return window is more than twice the 14-day statutory cancellation right, and nothing is deducted when an item arrives faulty, wrong or late
  • Third-party sellers are barred from listing used or refurbished clothing, and need permission from the brand or wholesaler to list at all
  • Seller rules require photographs of the actual garment on a model or mannequin from several angles, plus a size chart in multiple international standards
  • Tiered referral fees on clothing charge the least on the cheapest items, which is why the sub-£15 end of the range is unusually deep
  • Amazon's section 54 modern slavery statement puts figures on remediation rather than intentions, including more than $2.2 million of recruitment fees repaid to 847 workers

Where it falls short

  • Amazon charges sellers a returns processing fee in exactly the Clothing, Shoes and Handbags categories where it gives shoppers free returns, and publishes the amount nowhere a buyer can see it
  • Free returns only apply where the listing was flagged eligible at the moment of purchase; on everything else the label costs from £1.99 and comes out of the refund
  • Mixing an eligible and an ineligible item in one return parcel can still cost postage on the ineligible one, and hand-delivered returns are refused outright
  • There is no route to second-hand menswear: sellers cannot list used clothing, and the pre-owned designer storefront added in February 2024 covers womenswear only
  • The modern slavery statement's audit, training and remediation figures are all group-wide, with nothing broken out for apparel or textiles
  • Which? publishes rated shop surveys for baby products, technology and home goods but none for clothing, so there is no independent customer score for menswear retailers to measure Amazon against
  • A serious-risk product safety report published on 15 September 2026 records Amazon as the seller of the item concerned
  • Whether Prime Try Before You Buy still operates for UK customers could not be established from Amazon's own UK pages and needs confirming

Common questions

Does Amazon charge for returning clothes that do not fit?

Not on items marked Free Return at the time of purchase, which covers clothing, footwear, handbags, jewellery and watches. Amazon supplies a prepaid label and pays the postage on goods it sold or dispatched, and there is no minimum order. On a listing that was not marked eligible, the return label costs from £1.99 within the UK and from £8.09 from overseas, and the amount is deducted from the refund unless the item was faulty, damaged, wrong or late.

How long do I actually have to send something back?

Two periods run at once. The statutory cancellation right gives 14 days from the day the item is received, for any reason. Amazon's own voluntary policy gives 30 days from receipt on most products provided they are unused and undamaged, and that is the one most shoppers use. Amazon Haul orders are different again at 15 days. Fashion returns must go back new and unworn with all original packaging, tags and certificates of authenticity; anything resized or altered will be refused.

What has to be on the label of a shirt or a jacket sold in the UK?

Fibre content by percentage, in English, on a label that is durable, easily legible, visible and accessible. Where two or more fibres make up 85% of the garment, each is named with its share. The word pure may only be used for a single-fibre item. Decorative matter of 7% or less is excluded from the figures, and a garment with components of different composition, such as a lined jacket, must declare each component. Any part of animal origin that is not a textile triggers the wording contains non-textile parts of animal origin.

How do I tell real leather shoes from coated leather on a listing?

By the label rather than the photograph. Under the Footwear (Indication of Composition) Labelling Regulations 1995 every new pair must indicate the material of the upper, the lining and sock and the outer sole, using whichever material covers 80% of that part's surface area, or the two main materials where none reaches 80%. There are four categories, each with a pictogram: leather, coated leather, textile and other materials. The label need only appear on one shoe of the pair, and for a distance sale the seller is supposed to explain what the symbols mean.

Can I buy second-hand menswear on Amazon?

No. Amazon's guidance to clothing sellers states that used and refurbished clothing may not be sold on the site, and that only authentic apparel may be listed with the brand's or wholesaler's permission. Amazon does operate a pre-owned designer storefront in Europe — Hardly Ever Worn It joined Luxury Stores on 6 February 2024 for the UK, Germany, Spain and Italy — but its range is women's ready-to-wear, shoes, accessories, jewellery and watches, with no men's equivalent.

Is there a way to check whether a clothing brand has been recalled?

Yes, and it is free. The Office for Product Safety and Standards publishes a searchable register of alerts, reports and recalls on gov.uk. Searching it on 27 September 2026 returned 248 entries for clothing, 135 for textile, 9 for chromium and 5 for footwear. Those numbers span several years and the whole UK market, so a clothing recall is genuinely uncommon; what the register cannot show is a marketplace listing withdrawn before anyone tested it.

Our verdict

For men's clothing Amazon's strongest feature is the one it charges nothing for: a prepaid return label on clothes, shoes and accessories, 30 days to use it and no minimum order, which takes the risk out of the sizing problem that makes buying menswear online awkward in the first place. The rules around it are strict rather than concealed — tags on, packaging kept, eligibility flagged at checkout — and the £1.99 charge on everything else is published plainly. Where the site is weakest is in what it tells you about the garment itself, because on a marketplace the listing copy belongs to the seller. The information that is reliable comes from the label rather than the page: fibre percentages under the 2012 Regulations, the three-part 80% pictogram on a shoe, the wording about non-textile parts of animal origin, and the 3 mg/kg chromium VI ceiling on leather that touches skin. Check those, run a brand name through the OPSS register before a larger order, and treat any environmental claim without a percentage next to it as decoration.
