Amazon Televisions Review 2026: Fire TV Prices, Power and Parts

Rates and terms checked 28 September 2026 · Televisions · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Amazon's own-brand televisions start at £249.99 for a 32-inch Fire TV 2-Series and run to £849.00 for the 55-inch Omni Mini-LED, and every one of them carries a one-year limited warranty from Amazon EU S.à r.l. in Luxembourg. Those prices come from Amazon's UK newsroom buying guide, published 30 September 2025 and last modified 27 December 2025; the warranty term is from Amazon's own device warranty page, both checked on 28 September 2026.

Amazon is unusual among the shops on this list because it is also a manufacturer. It builds the 2-Series, the 4-Series, the Omni QLED and the Omni Mini-LED under its own name, licenses the Fire TV software to Hisense, Panasonic, TCL and Xiaomi, and sells every other brand alongside them. Amazon says customers have bought 300 million devices with Fire TV built in.

What makes the range worth a serious look is not the price. It is that Amazon publishes a safety and compliance sheet for each set, and those sheets carry numbers most retailers never put in front of a shopper: the watts the panel draws, the energy class, the standby draw, and how long the company will keep making spare parts. We have read them, and the figures below are all Amazon's own.

The price, the guarantee and what Amazon is promising when it gives you one

Four Fire TV lines are sold in the UK under Amazon's own name. The 2-Series is HD at 32 and 40 inches from £249.99; the 4-Series is 4K at 43, 50 and 55 inches from £429.99; the Omni QLED runs 43 to 65 inches from £549.00; and the Omni Mini-LED covers 55, 65 and 75 inches from £849.00. The Fire TV Stick 4K Select, which turns any set into a Fire TV, is £49.99.

Every one of those televisions comes with what Amazon calls a One-Year Limited Warranty. It runs "one year from the date of original retail purchase", covers "defects in materials and workmanship under ordinary consumer use", and is given by Amazon EU S.à r.l., 38 avenue John F. Kennedy, L-1855 Luxembourg. Remotes, game controllers, IR extenders and the Fire TV Blaster bought separately get a 90-Day Limited Warranty instead.

A guarantee of that kind is not a favour. Section 30 of the Consumer Rights Act 2015 says a guarantee "takes effect, at the time the goods are delivered, as a contractual obligation owed by the guarantor" — so the year is enforceable in its own right, on top of the statutory rights that run for six years in England, Wales and Northern Ireland and five in Scotland. The same section requires the guarantee to be written in English where the goods are offered in the UK, to set out the claims procedure in plain language, to state that statutory rights are unaffected, and to give the guarantor's name and address, the duration and the territorial scope. Amazon's wording does all of that, and says so explicitly: "If you are a consumer, this One-Year Limited Warranty is provided in addition to, and without prejudice to, your consumer rights."

One year is what Amazon adds. On a television made by somebody else and sold through the marketplace, Amazon adds nothing to the guarantee at all — the cover is whatever the manufacturer or the seller offers, and it varies set by set.

The watts Amazon prints, and the bill they turn into

The single most useful document on an Amazon television is the safety and compliance sheet, and it is not on the product page. For the 65-inch Fire TV Omni, model QL65F601U, the sheet gives on-mode power demand of 104 W in Standard Dynamic Range and 164 W in High Dynamic Range, standby at 0.44 W and networked standby at 0.53 W, at a resolution of 3,840 by 2,160.

Those figures do something a star rating cannot: they price the set for the years after you buy it. Four hours of viewing a day is 1,460 hours a year. At 104 W that is 151.8 kWh, and at the Ofgem price cap direct debit electricity rate of 26.11p per kWh for 1 July to 30 September 2026 it comes to £39.65 a year. From 1 October the capped rate rises to 26.32p, which takes the same viewing to £39.96. Watch in HDR instead and the same four hours a day draws 239.4 kWh, or £62.52 — £22.87 a year for the brighter picture. Leaving it in networked standby all year, which is what it does when you are not watching, costs about £1.21.

Across the seven years Amazon commits to supplying spare parts, that SDR figure is £277.55 of electricity behind a television that cost a few hundred pounds. It is the sort of sum a shopper can only do if the retailer publishes the wattage, and Amazon does.

The 2025 sheets extend the ladder. The Fire TV 4-Series is given as 100.0 W at 43 inches (4K43N402U), 135.0 W at 50 and 150.0 W at 55, all at 220–240 V, 50 Hz. The Omni QLED is 140.0 W at 50 inches, 180.0 W at 55, 200.0 W at 65 and 250.0 W at 75. Those newer numbers are not split between SDR and HDR, so they are not directly comparable with the 104 W on the older Omni sheet, and we have not treated them as if they were.

Class G is normal, and the label explains why

The Omni 65 sheet rates the set G for energy efficiency in both SDR and HDR. That reads alarmingly and it should not. The GB energy label for electronic displays was rescaled to a plain A to G range that applied from 1 March 2021, with the printed-label duty biting from 1 November 2020, and the scale was deliberately set so that almost nothing qualified for the top letters at the start. Under the retained regulation on the energy labelling of electronic displays, class A needs an Energy Efficiency Index below 0.3 and class G covers anything at 0.9 or above. Large 4K panels sit at the bottom of that scale almost by construction.

The label itself is a mandated disclosure with a fixed content list, which is what makes it useful. A GB label must carry the model number, the supplier's name or trademark, the consumption data, the class, the UK flag, English text and, where applicable, a QR code. The duties are split: the supplier has to provide the label free and promptly, include the product information sheet and keep the technical documentation for 15 years for inspection; the dealer has to display the label clearly and, for distance selling, put the label and the information sheet in front of the buyer before they buy. For internet sales the electronic label must appear "in proximity to the price of the product", with a nested display permitted so long as it opens on the first click.

There is one quiet divergence worth knowing. In Great Britain the QR code on the label points to "the required model information on a publicly accessible website" — the supplier's own. In Northern Ireland it still points to EPREL, the EU's central database. So a Northern Irish buyer can look a television up in one place and cross-check the maker's claims against a public register; a buyer in England, Scotland or Wales is sent to whatever page the supplier decided to put up.

Seven years of parts, fifteen working days to get one

Energy labelling is only half of the rulebook. The ecodesign regulation for electronic displays sets minimum efficiency and, more usefully for a buyer, minimum repairability. From 1 March 2021 the maximum Energy Efficiency Index was 0.9 for HD and 1.1 for UHD-4K; from 1 March 2023 it tightened to 0.75 and 0.9. Standby is capped at 0.5 W, off mode at 0.3 W and networked standby at 2.0 W.

Set the Omni 65's own figures against those caps and the result is mixed in an interesting way. Networked standby at 0.53 W is comfortably under the 2.0 W ceiling. Standby at 0.44 W is inside the 0.5 W limit with very little room left.

The repair provisions are the part almost nobody reads. Manufacturers must make the internal power supply, connectors, capacitors, batteries, and the DVD/Blu-ray and HD/SSD modules where fitted available to professional repairers for seven years after the last unit of the model is placed on the market, and the external power supply and remote control to end users for the same period. Orders must be delivered within "15 working days after having received the order". Repairers must be given an exploded view, a list of the test equipment needed, wiring diagrams and fault codes within one working day of asking. Plastic parts over 50 g must be marked with the polymer type, joins must not prevent removal of the components listed in the WEEE rules using ordinary tools, and "the use of halogenated flame retardants is not allowed in the enclosure and stand of electronic displays."

Amazon's own compliance sheet for the Omni 65 states spare parts availability of 7 Years and product support of 7 Years, which matches the regulation rather than beating it.

Three documents, three different answers on how long the set is supported

The same compliance sheet says software and firmware updates are guaranteed for 8 Years. Amazon's customer-facing security update page says something different: "We'll support your device with software security updates until at least four years from when you bought it new", or the published date if later. The published date for every current Fire TV television — Omni QLED at 43, 50, 55 and 65 inches, 4-Series at 43, 50 and 55, 2-Series at 32 and 40 — is 31 December 2030, as it is for every current Fire TV Stick. Only the first-generation Fire TV Stick 4K sits earlier, on 31 December 2029.

Both statements are Amazon's and they do not say the same thing. Buy an Omni QLED on the day we checked, 28 September 2026, and 31 December 2030 is four years and three months away, so the four-year floor and the published date land in almost the same place. Buy one in 2029 and the date, not the four years, is what you get — unless Amazon extends it. The eight-year figure on the compliance sheet is the most generous of the three and the one no customer-facing page repeats. We have printed all three rather than choosing between them.

The update page also carries its own caveat, and it is a fair one: "Please note the update period is not a guarantee as to how long your device will last." For context, Samsung and LG have both told Which? they support their televisions for five years from launch, which is measured from a different starting point again.

Who actually answers for the set, and which regulator is watching

Three Amazon entities appear across the documents for one television. The compliance declaration is made by Amazon.com Services LLC, a United States company, and the sheets we read give no address for it. The guarantee is given by Amazon EU S.à r.l. in Luxembourg. The shop is Amazon's UK store. None of the three is a UK address a buyer could write to, and the compliance sheets point readers to a support page rather than naming a responsible person in Great Britain.

Enforcement is split in a way that matters on a marketplace. The Office for Product Safety and Standards is the market surveillance authority for suppliers under the energy information rules; local authorities enforce the same rules against dealers. When Amazon sells a television it has built, it is the supplier and OPSS is the body concerned. When a third-party seller on the same site lists a Hisense or a Toshiba, that seller is the dealer, and the duty to show the energy label beside the price falls on them, policed by their local trading standards service rather than by Amazon or by OPSS.

The Fire TV compliance sheets state the devices "are in compliance with Directive 2014/53/EU and relevant UK statutory requirements" and meet the public exposure thresholds for electromagnetic fields in Council Recommendation 1999/519/EC. On recycling they say only that you should "dispose of, or recycle, your device in accordance with your local laws and regulations".

The badge on the bezel is not always the company that built it

A television is one of the few purchases where the brand on the front tells you very little about who made the thing. The public product safety register makes the point better than any argument. Searching it for television on 28 September 2026 returns two reports, both dated 15 June 2026, and both about the same component in different boxes.

Report 2606-0078 covers Vestel UK plugs fitted to televisions sold between November 2024 and February 2026 under Toshiba, JVC, Mitchell & Brown, Polaroid, Finlux, Techwood and Panasonic badges — models running from the Toshiba 32WF2F53DB to the Panasonic TV-65W80AEY. The fault is that "the plug's fuse does not meet the dimensional requirements of BS 1362", giving a risk of fire; the origin is Turkey, the breach is of the Electrical Equipment (Safety) Regulations 2016 or the Plugs & Sockets etc (Safety) Regulations 1994, it was notified by local authority Trading Standards, and the remedy is a free replacement power cable or fuse. Report 2606-0164 is the same defect in Bush TiVo televisions sold by Argos.

Seven brand names, one manufacturer, one fuse. Amazon sells several of those brands, and so does everybody else. It is the strongest argument there is for reading the compliance sheet rather than the badge — and Amazon, for its own sets, is one of the few sellers that publishes one you can find.

How Amazon's sets rate for reliability against the rest

The independent measure worth having on a television is not a picture-quality score but a fault rate, because a set that dies in year three costs more than one that was £100 dearer. Which? surveyed more than 15,000 members and members of the public in May 2026 about more than 43,000 televisions, with 5,911 sets behind the brand rankings and 14 brands covered, Amazon among them alongside LG, Sony, Samsung, Hisense, TCL, Sky, Toshiba, Sharp, Bush, Panasonic, JVC, Philips and Hitachi.

The headline finding that is readable without a subscription is blunt: of those fourteen brands, "just four of them manage five stars for reliability". The three most common faults reported were picture problems such as dead pixels and fuzzy images, sound faults including crackling and speaker failure, and hardware failure that stops the set powering on at all. The per-brand fault percentages and the average time to first fault sit behind the Which? paywall, so we cannot tell you where Amazon lands — and we are not going to guess at it. Anyone deciding between two sets on reliability should pay for one month of access and read the table, because it is the only UK figure of its kind.

The set is also a screen Amazon sells advertising on

Amazon's television business is not really a hardware business. The company says customers have bought 300 million devices with Fire TV built in, and it sells advertising against that audience in more than 20 countries including the UK. Its own case for Fire TV advertising cites 93% incremental reach when added to a Prime Video campaign and 88% exclusive reach, measured in the United States between 1 July and 31 December 2025.

Amazon also publishes the number that explains its interface design: customers spend an average of 12 minutes looking for something to watch, up from 10.5 minutes in 2023. The redesigned interface launched on 5 January 2026 and updated on 22 June 2026 claims 20% to 30% speed gains on existing hardware, and the newest set, the Ember Artline at 55 and 65 inches, is a picture-frame television with more than 2,000 works of art built in. A buyer should go in knowing that the home screen is inventory. That is the trade behind a 4K set at £429.99, and it is a defensible one — but it is a trade, not a discount.

Where it wins

  • Publishes a safety and compliance sheet per model giving on-mode watts, standby draw and, on the older Omni, the energy class — detail most television retailers never put in front of a shopper
  • One-year manufacturer guarantee on every Amazon-built set, given by a named company at a stated address, with statutory rights expressly preserved
  • Seven years of spare parts and product support stated on Amazon's own sheet, matching what the ecodesign rules require
  • Networked standby of 0.53 W on the Omni 65 is well inside the 2.0 W legal ceiling, which keeps the always-on cost near £1.21 a year
  • Four own-brand lines from £249.99 to £849.00 plus almost every rival brand on the same site, so the range genuinely spans the market
  • Fire TV software is licensed to Hisense, Panasonic, TCL and Xiaomi, so the interface a buyer learns is not locked to Amazon's own hardware

Where it falls short

  • The 2025 compliance sheets for the 4-Series and Omni QLED publish on-mode wattage but no energy efficiency class at all, while the older Omni 65 sheet gives one — a step backwards in disclosure on the figure the label regime exists to communicate
  • Three Amazon documents give three different support periods for the same television: 8 years of software updates on the compliance sheet, at least 4 years from purchase or 31 December 2030 on the customer support page, and 7 years of spare parts
  • No UK address appears anywhere in the chain. Conformity is declared by Amazon.com Services LLC in the United States and the guarantee is given by Amazon EU S.à r.l. in Luxembourg
  • One year of manufacturer cover is short against a television's working life, and on any set Amazon did not build it adds nothing at all — the cover is the maker's or the seller's, and it is not consistent across the marketplace
  • Standby draw of 0.44 W on the Omni 65 is inside the 0.5 W legal cap by six hundredths of a watt, which leaves no headroom at all
  • The newer wattage figures are published without the SDR and HDR split the older sheet used, so they cannot be compared with it or with a rival's label figure without guesswork
  • In Great Britain the label's QR code points at the supplier's own website rather than a public database, so there is no independent register to check a claim against — a Northern Irish buyer can still use EPREL

Common questions

How long is the guarantee on an Amazon Fire TV?

One year from the date of original retail purchase, given by Amazon EU S.à r.l. in Luxembourg, covering defects in materials and workmanship in ordinary consumer use. Remotes and other accessories bought separately get 90 days. It sits on top of your statutory rights, which run for six years in England, Wales and Northern Ireland and five in Scotland.

What does a Fire TV cost to run?

The 65-inch Fire TV Omni draws 104 W watching standard content. Four hours a day for a year is 151.8 kWh, or £39.65 at the 26.11p capped direct debit rate for July to September 2026, rising to £39.96 at the 26.32p rate from 1 October. HDR content draws 164 W, which takes the same viewing to £62.52.

Why is the energy rating G?

Because the A to G scale for televisions was rescaled from 1 March 2021 and set deliberately hard. Class A needs an Energy Efficiency Index under 0.3 and class G covers 0.9 and above, so most large 4K sets from every brand sit at the bottom. A G rating is not a sign that one television is worse than its rivals — compare the kWh per 1,000 hours instead.

Can a Fire TV be repaired?

The ecodesign rules oblige the maker to supply professional repairers with the internal power supply, connectors, capacitors and other listed parts for seven years after the last unit of that model is sold, delivered within 15 working days, and to hand over wiring diagrams and fault codes within one working day of a request. Amazon's own sheet states seven years of spare parts and seven of product support.

How long will Amazon send security updates to a Fire TV?

Amazon's support page promises at least four years from the day you buy it new, or a published date if that is later. Every current Fire TV television and stick carries 31 December 2030; the first-generation Fire TV Stick 4K carries 31 December 2029. The compliance sheet for the Omni 65 says eight years, which no customer-facing page repeats.

Does Amazon guarantee televisions made by other brands?

No. Amazon's one-year warranty applies to devices Amazon makes. On any other brand the guarantee is the manufacturer's or the seller's and it varies by listing, so check who is named on the set's own guarantee before ordering.

Our verdict

Amazon is worth buying a television from for a reason that has nothing to do with the price: it publishes more hard numbers about its own sets than almost any shop publishes about anybody's. The 65-inch Omni's 104 W in SDR, 164 W in HDR and 0.53 W in networked standby are the difference between guessing at a running cost and working one out — about £39.65 a year at four hours a day on the capped rate in force when we checked. The guarantee is one year, which is short, and the support dates disagree with each other across three Amazon documents. Read the compliance sheet for the model you want before you buy, and if the set is not one of Amazon's own, find out from the listing who is actually giving you a guarantee.
