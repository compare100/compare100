Amazon Women's Fashion: Pre-Owned Designer, Marketplace Rules and the Chemistry Small Print

Rates and terms checked 28 September 2026 · Women's Fashion · Compare100 editorial team

Amazon has sold pre-owned designer womenswear in the UK since 6 February 2024, when Hardly Ever Worn It — a London resale business trading since 2012 and shipping to more than 40 countries — became the first second-hand seller inside Luxury Stores at Amazon, launching in the UK, Germany, Spain and Italy on the same day. Figures here were checked on 28 September 2026 against Amazon's own newsroom, its UK selling fee schedule, Companies House and the government's product safety register.

Everything else is the marketplace: third-party sellers listing new clothing, shoes, bags and jewellery beside Amazon's own retail stock, with free returns on fashion items for 30 days and no minimum spend. On 5 January 2026 Amazon cut the referral fee it charges those sellers on clothing to 5% on anything priced at or under £15, rising to 10% up to £20 and 15% above it — the lowest entry rate in any category it publishes, and the reason a great deal of cheap clothing has arrived on the site.

The rest of this page is the part a product listing does not show: which rules a dress, a pair of earrings or a leather jacket has to satisfy before it can legally be sold at all, what the recall register has stopped at the border this year, and what Amazon does and does not publish about the clothes it marks as sustainable.

Where the second-hand womenswear actually sits

Two routes, and they are not the same thing. The first is Luxury Stores at Amazon, where Hardly Ever Worn It has sold pre-owned designer pieces since 6 February 2024 — women's ready-to-wear, shoes, accessories, jewellery and watches, screened by the partner rather than by Amazon, and launched across four markets at once. Ruth Diaz, then vice-president of Amazon Fashion Europe, announced it alongside the resale company's chief executive, Tatiana Wolter Ferguson. The business itself dates from 2012 and ships to over 40 countries, so the inventory is not new even if the shop window is.

The second is Amazon Resale, which is customer returns sold on at a discount. Amazon grades them Like New, Very Good, Good and Acceptable, prices them at up to 50% off recommended retail, and covers them under the ordinary returns policy rather than a separate clearance rule. Its Second Chance pages, updated 27 August 2025, name apparel explicitly in the list of what can turn up there, alongside electronics, kitchen appliances, office supplies, books and toys.

What does not exist is a route in the other direction. Amazon's own guidance for people selling clothing states plainly that only new clothes may be listed and that used or refurbished clothes cannot be sold, and that apparel must be authentic with permission obtained from the retailer, wholesaler, manufacturer or designer. So the pre-owned shelf is Amazon's own supply chain and one invited partner; nobody else gets to put a worn dress on the site.

Who takes the money for a pre-owned dress

Hardly Ever Worn It UK Ltd sits at Companies House under number 16812843, incorporated on 27 October 2025 with a registered office at 167-169 Great Portland Street, London W1W 5PF and a single trading classification, 47910 — retail sale via mail order houses or via the internet. It is an active company with nothing filed: first accounts are due by 27 July 2027 for a period ending 31 October 2026, and the first confirmation statement by 9 November 2026.

That gap between a brand trading since 2012 and a UK company eleven months old is worth understanding rather than worrying about — group structures get rearranged, and a new entity for a new market is ordinary. But it does mean the usual check a careful buyer makes before a large purchase is unavailable. There are no accounts, no turnover, no balance sheet and no filing history to read, and on a pre-owned designer handbag that can be a three-figure or four-figure decision.

One thing could not be established. Amazon's own shopping pages refuse automated reading, so whether the storefront is still live on the UK site in 2026 could not be checked from a primary source. The announcement is dated and real, the UK company is active on the register, and the rest needs a look at the site or a question to Amazon's customer service.

The chemistry rules behind a £6 pair of earrings

Costume jewellery is where womenswear meets its strictest rulebook. Anything in prolonged contact with skin — necklaces, bracelets, chains, anklets, finger rings, wristwatches and their components — must release less than 0.5 micrograms of nickel per square centimetre per week, and anything inserted into a pierced ear or body part must stay under 0.2. The reference method is BS EN 1811. Lead is prohibited in jewellery at 0.05% or more by weight, and cadmium is capped at 0.01%, which is 100 mg/kg. The official trading standards guidance carrying those figures was last reviewed in April 2024, and local authority Trading Standards enforce them.

Fabric has its own set. Azo colourants that can release listed aromatic amines are limited to 30 mg/kg in dyed textiles and leathers that touch skin, tested to EN ISO 14362-1:2017 for textiles and EN ISO 17234-1:2024 for leather. Chromium VI in finished leather must be under 3 mg/kg, tested to EN ISO 17075-2, which matters on a dyed leather jacket because the dye masks the result under the older method.

Then there is the restriction that swept up everything else. Since 1 November 2020 a single entry has capped 33 substances classed as carcinogenic, mutagenic or toxic for reproduction across clothing, footwear, textile accessories and household textiles: formaldehyde at 75 mg/kg, quinoline and three disperse dyes at 50 mg/kg, benzene at 5 mg/kg, eight polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons at 1 mg/kg, the solvents NMP, DMFa and DMAC at 3,000 mg/kg, five phthalates at 1,000 mg/kg, and extractable cadmium, chromium VI, arsenic and lead at 1 mg/kg each. Garments made entirely of natural leather, fur or hide sit outside it, as do medical devices and personal protective equipment. None of this appears anywhere on a listing, which is the point worth taking away: compliance here is a promise made by the seller, not a published figure the shopper can read.

What the register stopped at the border this year

The government's product safety alerts, reports and recalls database is the check a shopper can actually run, and it is free. Searched on 28 September 2026, the keyword nickel returns 33 reports, and the three most recent are all jewellery and all rated serious.

A Bees Jewellery hand chain, bangle and earring set, reference 2601-0411, was published on 3 February 2026: the earrings exceeded the permitted limits for both lead and nickel and the hand chain and bangle exceeded the nickel limit, a breach recorded against the REACH Enforcement Regulations 2008, country of origin India, notified by local authority Trading Standards. The import was rejected at the border and destroyed. A Lost Souls 'S' initial necklace, 2512-0348, was published on 9 January 2026 and also destroyed. A Shop TJC diamond silver bracelet, 2512-0330, was published on 7 January 2026 and rejected at the border.

None of the three names Amazon as the seller, and that is worth saying rather than leaving implied. The register is evidence that the system catches cheap jewellery with too much nickel in it wherever it is sold, and the sensible use of it is as a habit: search the brand name on the register before buying unfamiliar costume jewellery, from any shop.

The green tick on a listing, and who stands behind it

Amazon's sustainability label is Climate Pledge Friendly, which covers over 1.4 million products and leans on more than 50 external certifications rather than any judgement of Amazon's own. For clothing the one that matters is STANDARD 100 by OEKO-TEX, which tests every component of a textile product — thread, buttons and trims included — against harmful chemicals; for leather goods it is the Leather Working Group. Amazon co-founded The Climate Pledge in 2019 and last updated its explainer on 21 April 2025.

The regulator's line on this sits just outside Amazon's door. The Competition and Markets Authority opened an investigation into environmental claims by ASOS, Boohoo and George at Asda on 29 July 2022 and closed it on 27 March 2024 on formal undertakings: published criteria for any named green range, the actual percentage behind an organic or recycled claim, filters that match what the garment is made of, and no natural imagery overstating a benefit. Its sector guidance of 18 September 2024 sets almost no thresholds and offers at least 50% recycled content only as an example of a filter label that works. Amazon was not one of the three firms investigated, and leaning on a third party's certification rather than an in-house claim is the approach the guidance points towards.

What is missing is an outside score. Fashion Revolution's What Fuels Fashion reviewed 250 brands and retailers with turnover above 400 million US dollars against over 70 data points on climate disclosure, producing an average score of 18%, a top score of 75% and 32 brands on zero. Amazon does not appear in it, and no UK consumer body publishes a rated survey of clothing shops, so there is no independent figure to set beside the company's own.

Fees, fakes and the numbers Amazon does publish

The selling costs explain the shape of the range better than any description of it. Clothing pays 5% at or under £15, 10% between £15 and £20 and 15% above, with a 25p minimum, after the cut dated 5 January 2026. Footwear, handbags, rucksacks and luggage pay a flat 15%. Watches pay 15% of the first £225 and 5% above it. Jewellery pays 20% of the first £225 and 5% above — the steepest rate anywhere in fashion, and a reason a small jeweller may price higher here than on its own site. Sellers using Amazon's warehouses also pay monthly storage of £0.62 to £0.76 per cubic foot from January to September, rising to £0.82 to £1.51 from October to December.

On counterfeits, Amazon's Trustworthy Shopping Experience report of 22 April 2026 gives the year to 31 December 2025: 15 million counterfeit products identified, seized and disposed of worldwide; more than 32,000 bad actors pursued through litigation and criminal referrals across 14 countries since the Counterfeit Crimes Unit opened in 2020; over 100 websites shut down; more than 70 raids alongside Chinese law enforcement; and 12 million products analysed through its Omniscan system. Those are real, dated, auditable numbers of a kind most clothing retailers never publish.

They are also group-wide. Not one of them is broken out for apparel, footwear or jewellery, which are the categories a shopper buying a designer-lookalike bag would want them for. The figures say the enforcement machine is large; they do not say how well it works on the shelf this page is about.

The gaps a shopper cannot close from the listing

Three things sit unresolved after a day of reading Amazon's own documents. The seller's compliance with the nickel, azo and 33-substance limits is asserted nowhere the shopper can see, so on marketplace jewellery the practical protection is the returns policy and the recall register rather than anything on the page. There is no independent rating of Amazon as a clothing retailer from any consumer body or transparency index, which is unusual for a company this size and leaves its own reporting unchecked. And the pre-owned shelf, which is the best thing on this page, rests on a single partner whose UK company is eleven months old with no accounts filed.

None of that makes the site a bad place to buy a coat. It does mean the confidence a shopper can take from a listing runs out sooner here than at a retailer that owns its whole supply chain, and that the £6 end of the jewellery range deserves more care than the £600 end.

Where it wins

  • The only large UK general retailer with a pre-owned designer womenswear shelf built in, rather than a separate app or a marketplace of private sellers.
  • Returns on clothing, shoes, handbags, jewellery and watches are free for 30 days with no minimum spend, and the prepaid label is paid for by Amazon on anything it sold or dispatched.
  • The referral fee cut of 5 January 2026 took the rate on sub-£15 clothing down to 5%, which is the cheapest selling rate Amazon publishes and shows up in the price of basics.
  • Amazon Resale sells graded customer returns at up to 50% off recommended retail prices under the ordinary returns policy, so a reduced item is not a final sale.
  • Amazon publishes an annual account of its counterfeit enforcement with dated figures — 15 million products removed in 2025 and more than 32,000 sellers pursued since 2020 — which most clothing retailers do not publish at all.

Where it falls short

  • No test evidence is published against a listing. Nothing on a product page shows whether a pair of earrings was tested against the 0.5 microgram nickel release limit or a dress against the 30 mg/kg azo dye limit, and on a marketplace the duty sits with whichever seller shipped it.
  • There is no independent transparency or customer score for Amazon as a clothing retailer. Fashion Revolution's 250-brand review of climate disclosure does not include it, and no consumer body publishes a rated survey of clothing shops, so there is nothing external to weigh the company's own numbers against.
  • Second-hand runs one way only. Amazon's own seller guidance states that used or refurbished clothes cannot be sold on the site, so a customer has no route to resell a garment bought here, and the pre-owned shelf depends entirely on one curated partner.
  • Hardly Ever Worn It UK Ltd was incorporated on 27 October 2025 and its first accounts are not due until 27 July 2027, so a buyer spending several hundred pounds on a pre-owned handbag has no filed financial information to look at.
  • Whether the Hardly Ever Worn It storefront is still trading on Amazon UK in 2026 could not be confirmed from any Amazon page that can be read automatically; the announcement stands, the UK company is active, but the current position needs confirming directly with Amazon.
  • The counterfeit figures are group-wide and cover every category. Amazon publishes no apparel, footwear or jewellery breakdown, which are the categories fakes are most often reported in, so the numbers cannot be read as a statement about fashion specifically.

Common questions

Can you buy second-hand clothes on Amazon UK?

Yes, by two routes. Pre-owned designer womenswear is sold inside Luxury Stores at Amazon by Hardly Ever Worn It, live in the UK since 6 February 2024, covering ready-to-wear, shoes, accessories, jewellery and watches. Separately, Amazon Resale sells graded customer returns at up to 50% off recommended retail across categories including apparel. What you cannot do is sell used clothing yourself: Amazon's seller guidance allows only new clothes on the site.

Are returns on Amazon clothing really free?

On fashion items, yes for 30 days with no minimum purchase, covering clothing, shoes, handbags, jewellery and watches. The prepaid label is paid for by Amazon on anything it sold or dispatched itself. Items must go back new and unworn with original packaging, tags and any certificate of authenticity, and resized or altered items are refused.

How do I check whether cheap jewellery is safe?

Search the brand or product name on the government's product safety alerts, reports and recalls register, which is free to use. On 28 September 2026 it held 33 reports under the keyword nickel, including a Bees Jewellery set published 3 February 2026 that exceeded both the lead and nickel limits and was destroyed at the border. The legal limits are 0.5 micrograms of nickel per square centimetre per week for anything worn against skin and 0.2 for piercing posts.

What does Climate Pledge Friendly actually certify?

Nothing on its own. It is a badge Amazon applies to over 1.4 million products that hold one of more than 50 external certifications. For clothing the relevant one is usually STANDARD 100 by OEKO-TEX, which tests every thread, button and trim for harmful chemicals; leather goods may carry Leather Working Group certification instead. The certification is the substance, so it is worth reading which one a garment holds rather than the badge.

Our verdict

As a place to buy women's clothes, Amazon is two businesses wearing one logo. One is a curated, authenticated pre-owned designer shelf with a named partner behind it and free 30-day returns; the other is an open marketplace where a 5% referral fee on sub-£15 clothing has drawn in a very large number of very cheap sellers, and where the metal and dye limits that make a garment legal are enforced after the fact by Trading Standards rather than before the fact by the shop. Buy the pre-owned items and the branded stock with confidence; on the £6 jewellery, check the seller's name and keep the receipt, because the 33 results the recall register held for nickel on 28 September 2026 are what enforcement looks like when it works.
