Aviva Pension Review: Charges, Transfers and the Cash Margin

Rates and terms checked 4 October 2026 · Pensions · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

The Aviva Pension charges 0.35% a year on the first £500,000 you hold in it and nothing at all above that line, takes £25 a month or a £5,000 lump sum to open, and gives access to more than 5,000 funds plus UK shares, exchange-traded funds and investment trusts at £4.99 a trade. Those figures were read on 4 October 2026 from Aviva's own charges page and its key features document, reference RD01002 07/2026.

Behind the product is the biggest wealth business in British retail finance. Aviva reported £233bn of Wealth assets under management at 31 March 2026 across 5.8 million customers, took £2bn of workplace pension net flows in the first quarter of 2026 alone — up 71% on the £1.2bn a year earlier — and now looks after more than 4.8 million workplace pension members. Defaqto's half-year analysis of adviser recommendations, published on 12 August 2026, put Aviva top of the self-invested personal pension market.

One thing to settle before you start. Three different things get called an Aviva pension, and they do not cost the same. The 0.35% above belongs to the Aviva Pension you can open yourself online. If your pension came through an employer, your charge was negotiated by that employer and is not published anywhere; if it came through an adviser, it sits on a different Aviva platform with a different fee scale. The section below sets out how to tell which one you have.

Three products share the name, and only one has a published price

Search for an Aviva pension and you can land on any of three products. Sorting out which one you are looking at is the first useful thing you can do, because the charge is the main thing that differs and only one of the three publishes it.

The Aviva Pension, sometimes badged as a SIPP. This is the one you can open yourself in an afternoon. It sits on Aviva's consumer platform alongside its stocks and shares ISA and investment account, and the Aviva Charge is 0.35% a year on the first £500,000 of what you hold across all three, with nothing charged above that. Aviva's own worked example: 0.35% of £100,000 is £350 a year.

The workplace pension. Aviva's group personal pension carries more than 4.8 million members and is sold to employers with 10 staff upwards, with Aviva taking direct applications up to 100 employees. The annual management charge is quoted scheme by scheme and depends on how many members there are and how much they pay in, so there is no figure to look up — only the one on your own annual statement. The default investment is the My Future Focus Long Term Growth Fund, which holds everything until 15 years before your chosen retirement date, moves into My Future Focus Growth at that point and into My Future Focus Consolidation from 10 years out.

Pension Portfolio. The adviser-sold version, on Aviva's advised platform. It has its own charge scale and, as the cash section below shows, its own arrangement on interest. If a financial adviser set your pension up, this is almost certainly the one you hold.

The simplest test is who you pay. A charge appearing on a statement you receive at home, with no employer and no adviser in the picture, is the 0.35%. Anything else and the number you need is on your scheme documentation rather than on Aviva's website.

What 0.35% buys, and the four charges that sit on top

The Aviva Charge is worked out daily on the average value of the pension and taken monthly out of the cash account. If there is not enough cash sitting there, the terms (RD01003 06/2025) say Aviva sells across your investments proportionately to raise it, adding a 10% buffer so it does not have to come back for more a month later. That is tidier than it sounds: it means a pension fully invested with no cash float will be nibbled at twelve times a year by small forced sales.

Four things are charged separately from the 0.35%, and all four are easy to miss.

The first is the fund's own cost, shown as an ongoing charges figure. Aviva's example is blunt enough to be useful: £1,000 in a ready-made fund with an OCF of 0.35% costs £3.50 a year, and a fund with an OCF of 1% on £10,000 costs £100. On a portfolio of active funds this is routinely larger than the platform charge itself, which makes the 0.35% a floor rather than a total.

The second is dealing. Funds are free to buy and sell. Anything traded on an exchange — a UK share, an ETF, an investment trust — costs £4.99 a go, so a four-holding rebalance is £19.96. The third is tax and levies: 0.5% stamp duty reserve tax on UK share purchases, and the £1.50 Takeover Panel levy on trades over £10,000.

The fourth is the one nobody looks for, and it is covered in its own section at the end of this page, because it is money Aviva keeps rather than money it charges.

Getting money in: £25 a month, 30 days to change your mind, and the transfers Aviva will not take

The entry points are low. £25 a month opens the pension; so does a single £5,000 payment, or £1,000 if you also set up £25 a month. You can open one from 18, keep paying in to 75, and transfer in at any age. Tax relief works the usual way at source — £80 of your money becomes £100 in the pension — with higher and additional rate taxpayers claiming the rest back, up to 45%, or 48% in Scotland.

The allowances are set by HMRC rather than Aviva, and Aviva's own pages print the current ones: £60,000 a year, tapering towards £10,000 once income passes £200,000 or once you have flexibly accessed a pension, a lump sum allowance of £268,275 for 2026/27 and a lump sum and death benefit allowance of £1,073,100.

Transfers in are free from Aviva's side, take three to six weeks in its own estimate, and currently come with cashback of between £100 and £2,500 for new Aviva Pension customers transferring at least £20,000, applied for by 23 November 2026 and completed by 22 May 2027. Existing Aviva pensions do not qualify.

There are hard limits on what can come across. Aviva will not take a defined benefit or final salary pension, a pension you have already started drawing, or one carrying a guaranteed annuity rate or other safeguarded benefit. Its own transfer page also lists what you might be giving up elsewhere: a tax-free lump sum above 25%, loyalty bonuses, enhanced life cover, or the right to take the money before 55. Transfers of safeguarded benefits worth more than £30,000 require regulated advice before they can proceed.

The cooling-off period is 30 days from Aviva receiving your first payment. Cancel inside it and the single payment comes back, less any fall in the value of the investments in the meantime — so the refund is not guaranteed to be pound for pound.

Two Aviva companies, two reference numbers, and one that appears twice

The Aviva Pension is assembled from two regulated entities. Aviva Wrap UK Limited, registered in England and Wales as company 4470008, runs the platform. Aviva Pension Trustees UK Limited, firm reference 465132, provides the pension itself and acts as trustee of the scheme the money sits in. Both are part of Aviva plc, so there is no third-party administrator between you and the brand on the statement — which is not true of every pension sold under a household name.

One detail needs confirming with Aviva rather than asserting here. Firm reference number 231530 appears on the product page against the platform operator, and the key features document prints the same number against a different Aviva company in connection with the MyAviva service. One of the two is a documentation error. It makes no practical difference to a customer, since both firms are Aviva, but anyone checking the chain on the Financial Conduct Authority register should expect the mismatch and ask which entity holds which permission.

Compensation cover is where a pension platform differs from an insured pension fund, and the distinction is worth more than it looks. The key features document puts Financial Services Compensation Scheme protection at £85,000 per person for the collective investments, not the unlimited 100% cover that applies to insured long-term funds. Cash held in the pension's cash account is protected to £120,000 per bank, the higher limit the Prudential Regulation Authority confirmed on 18 November 2025 and which took effect on 1 December 2025.

For a workplace pension there is a second layer that the direct product does not have. Default funds in automatic enrolment schemes are capped by law at 0.75% a year under regulation 7 of the Occupational Pension Schemes (Charges and Governance) Regulations 2015, and Aviva's workplace book is overseen by an Independent Governance Committee that reports publicly every year. Mark Thompson was announced as its incoming chair in October 2026.

What Aviva's own governance committee found

That committee's 2026 report, covering the year to 31 December 2025, is the most useful document on Aviva's pensions anywhere, and it is unusual in being written by people whose job is to criticise the firm paying them. Its conclusion is favourable: Aviva "continues to provide good value for money for members of its workplace pension schemes". The detail is more mixed.

On price, 65% of Aviva policyholders pay 0.5% a year or less. The full distribution is 22.3% under 0.3%, 23.4% between 0.3% and 0.4%, 19.1% between 0.4% and 0.5%, 10.1% between 0.5% and 0.6%, 24.8% between 0.6% and 0.75%, and 0.3% above 0.75%. Read the other way round, a quarter of Aviva's workplace members pay between 0.6% and 0.75% — up to twice what the direct Aviva Pension charges — and a small group sit above the statutory cap, which is possible because the cap applies to default arrangements rather than to every fund a member can choose.

Performance to 31 December 2025 was strong in the growth funds: My Future Growth returned 14.3% over the year, My Future Focus Long Term Growth 14.2% and My Future Focus Growth 13.1%, against inflation of 3.3%. The committee was blunter about the Stewardship range, saying the fund "has not performed well against its benchmark over recent time periods", and it has gone on pressing Aviva to rename it so the label reflects what it actually invests in. It also criticised the pace of Aviva's own fund migration: a "significant cohort of members have not yet benefited from the new My Future Strategy because of the extended delay in their transition", and asked for that to be accelerated. On investment pathways, used by roughly 250,000 policyholders holding around £20bn, it judged asset allocation changes planned for late 2026 or early 2027 as possibly not going far enough.

Two service figures from the same report are worth having. Aviva traced about 41,000 lost members during 2025 and re-established contact with 44% of them, leaving its "gone away" population at 7% — in line with the industry average, which is another way of saying roughly one member in fourteen cannot be contacted. And 71,218 members attended one of 1,348 seminars during the year.

20,143 complaints in six months, and what they were about

Aviva publishes its own complaints returns, which most providers bury. The numbers for 1 January to 30 June 2026 are the strongest argument against the product on this page.

In the decumulation and pensions category Aviva opened 20,143 complaints and closed 20,070 in those six months, a rate of 2.4 per 1,000 policies in force. It resolved 25.5% within three days and 63.2% within eight weeks, which leaves better than a third of complainants waiting longer than the regulatory window. And it upheld 72.4% of them. The single largest cause is recorded as "delays / timescales".

An uphold rate that high is not evidence of a firm being hard on customers — upholding a complaint means agreeing with it. It is evidence of a firm generating a very large number of complaints it then agrees were justified, overwhelmingly about taking too long. For a product whose worst-case failure is a transfer or a drawdown payment arriving late, that is the risk worth pricing in. The investments category on the same return is worse on uphold rate, at 83.8% of 4,005 closed cases, at 4.8 complaints per 1,000 client accounts.

For context from outside Aviva, the Financial Ombudsman Service published its 2025/26 annual data on 21 May 2026: 214,600 new complaints across all financial products and an average uphold rate of 30%. Personal pensions accounted for 3,777 cases with a 49% uphold rate. Aviva's own first-stage figure is well above both, though the two are not directly comparable — the Ombudsman sees only cases a firm has already refused.

The cash in your pension earns 2.08% while the bank pays 3.05%

Aviva does not charge for holding cash in the pension. It makes money on it instead, and it publishes exactly how in document RD06102, dated April 2026.

Interest earned on the pension's cash account is split in bands. Aviva passes on 60% of the first 1.5 percentage points, 75% of the next 1.5, and 90% of anything above 3%. The worked example in its own factsheet: at an underlying rate of 3.05%, the customer receives 2.08% and Aviva retains 0.97%. If the rate from the bank is zero or negative, Aviva keeps nothing.

Measured as a charge, 0.97% on cash is almost three times the 0.35% Aviva charges on invested money. On £5,000 of cash that is £48.50 a year that does not reach you. The practical lesson is that the cash account should hold what the monthly charge needs and little more, because money parked there is the most expensive money in the pension.

The split is also not the same across Aviva's platforms. Its separate note on charge changes for the advised platform — Pension Portfolio and its siblings, with the change applying no earlier than 17 August 2026 — sets the first band at 50% to the customer rather than 60%. Two Aviva documents, two first-band splits, so check which applies to your own plan rather than assuming the better of the two.

  • Over 5,000 funds plus UK shares, ETFs and investment trusts, with ready-made options for people who do not want to choose
  • Defaqto put Aviva top of the SIPP market for adviser recommendations in the first half of 2026
  • No charge to transfer in, three to six weeks typical, and £100 to £2,500 cashback on £20,000-plus transfers applied for by 23 November 2026
  • The workplace book is independently reviewed every year and the report is published in full, criticisms included
  • 30 days to cancel, and both companies involved are Aviva rather than an outsourced administrator

Where it falls short

  • Only 63.2% of those complaints were closed within eight weeks, so more than a third of complainants waited longer than the regulatory window
  • On cash, Aviva retains 0.97 percentage points of a 3.05% rate — nearly three times what it charges on invested money
  • The first-band cash split differs between Aviva's own documents, 60% on the consumer platform and 50% on the advised one

  • Badged a SIPP, but the published asset list is funds, UK shares, ETFs and investment trusts — not the commercial property and unlisted holdings a full SIPP allows

Common questions

What does the Aviva Pension actually cost a year?

The Aviva Charge is 0.35% a year on the first £500,000 you hold across your Aviva pension, ISA and investment account combined, and nothing above £500,000. On £100,000 that is £350, which is Aviva's own worked example. On top sit each fund's ongoing charges figure, £4.99 per trade on shares, ETFs and investment trusts, 0.5% stamp duty on UK share purchases and a £1.50 levy on trades over £10,000. The charge is calculated daily and taken monthly from the cash account.

How do I find out what my Aviva workplace pension charges?

Your annual statement, or your scheme documentation from your employer. Aviva quotes the annual management charge on its group personal pension scheme by scheme, based on member numbers and contribution levels, so there is no published figure. For scale, its governance committee reported that 65% of policyholders pay 0.5% a year or less, 24.8% pay between 0.6% and 0.75%, and 0.3% pay more than 0.75%. The 0.75% statutory cap applies to default arrangements, not to every fund you can pick.

Can I move an old pension into the Aviva Pension?

In most cases yes, and Aviva does not charge for it — three to six weeks is its own estimate. It will not accept a defined benefit or final salary pension, one you have already begun taking money from, or one with a guaranteed annuity rate or other safeguarded benefit. Check what you would lose before moving anything: a tax-free lump sum above 25%, a loyalty bonus, enhanced death benefits or an early access right can all be worth more than a charge saving. Transferring safeguarded benefits over £30,000 requires regulated advice.

When can I take the money out?

From 55 at present, rising to 57 on 6 April 2028 unless you hold a protected pension age. Normally 25% comes out tax free, up to the lump sum allowance of £268,275 for 2026/27, and the rest is taxed as income. On the consumer platform the income route is drawdown; buying a guaranteed income for life means moving the money to an annuity provider, which can be Aviva or anyone else. Anyone over 50 can get a free appointment with Pension Wise through MoneyHelper before deciding.

Is my money safe if Aviva fails?

The pension's assets are held separately from Aviva's own, and the scheme's trustee is Aviva Pension Trustees UK Limited, firm reference 465132. The key features document puts Financial Services Compensation Scheme cover at £85,000 per person on the collective investments — not the unlimited protection that applies to an insured pension fund, which is a real difference between a platform pension and an older-style insured one. Cash in the pension's cash account is covered to £120,000 per bank, the limit that took effect on 1 December 2025.

Our verdict

As a place to hold a pension you manage yourself, the Aviva Pension is priced in the middle of the market and built better than its price suggests: 0.35% to £500,000 with nothing above, free fund dealing, £4.99 exchange trades, 5,000-plus funds and a £25 monthly minimum. The free transfer in and the cashback offer running to 23 November 2026 make consolidating old pots into it cheap to do, provided none of them carries a guarantee worth keeping. Two things should temper the decision. The first is service: Aviva's own published return shows 20,143 pensions complaints in six months with 72.4% upheld and delay the leading cause, which is the thing most likely to go wrong and the thing a glossy charges page will never tell you. The second is cash, where Aviva keeps 0.97 of 3.05 percentage points — fine if you hold a float for charges, expensive if you drift into cash and leave it there. If you already have an Aviva workplace pension, none of the 0.35% applies to you; find your own annual management charge on your scheme statement and compare that instead, because a quarter of Aviva's workplace members are paying between 0.6% and 0.75%.
