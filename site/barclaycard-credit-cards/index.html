Barclaycard Credit Cards Review 2026: Nine Cards, 24.9% to 80.1% APR

Rates and terms checked 5 October 2026 · Credit Cards · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Barclaycard lists nine personal credit cards at representative APRs running from 24.9% to 80.1% variable, and its longest balance transfer offer is 0% for up to 29 months on a 3.45% transfer fee. Every representative example on the range is built on an assumed £1,200 credit limit. Figures checked on 5 October 2026 against Barclaycard's own card pages, summary boxes and fee schedules.

This is the oldest card brand in the country — Barclaycard issued the first UK credit card in 1966 — and the range is unusually honest about where the money is made. Four of the nine cards are Platinum balance transfer variants that earn nothing at all while the 0% runs, and the Barclaycard Rewards card charges no annual fee, no over-limit fee, no returned payment fee and no non-sterling fee on purchases abroad, with a minimum credit limit of £50. The paperwork behind them is better than most: Barclaycard publishes a full summary box per card, including the simple monthly interest rate alongside the annual one, and its eligibility checker will tell you which offer you have actually been given before you commit to an application.

One eligibility rule is worth knowing before you start. Barclaycard will not accept a transfer from anything already inside its own group: in its own words, it "can't accept transfers from other credit card or loan accounts held with Barclays Bank UK PLC or any other Barclays Group company. This includes our Partnership card – Hilton Honors." That covers loans as well as cards, so a Barclays current account customer carrying a Barclayloan cannot move it here, however long the 0% offer runs.

What Barclaycard actually puts on the shelf

There are nine personal cards, and they do four separate jobs. Four of them are Platinum balance transfer variants, all at 24.9% APR representative and a 24.9% p.a. variable purchase rate, separated only by how long the 0% runs and what the transfer costs: up to 29 months for a 3.45% fee, up to 21 months on purchases and transfers together for 3.45%, up to 20 months for 1.99%, and up to 12 months for no fee at all. The Amazon Barclaycard sits at 28.9% APR representative with six months at 0% on purchases and pays 1% back on Amazon spending and 0.5% everywhere else. Barclaycard Rewards is also 28.9% and pays 0.25% cashback. Barclaycard Forward, the credit-building card, is listed at 33.9%.

The two Avios cards are where the ladder breaks. Barclaycard Avios charges nothing and earns 1 Avios per £1 of eligible spending at 29.9% APR representative. Barclaycard Avios Plus charges £20 a month, earns 1.5 Avios per £1 at the same 29.9% purchase rate, and carries an 80.1% APR representative. The 50 percentage points between the two cards are not a difference in borrowing cost at all — both charge 29.9% on purchases. They are the £240 of annual fees expressed as an interest rate, because the representative APR has to include compulsory charges. It is a useful piece of arithmetic to have published rather than hidden, and it also means the 80.1% figure tells a borrower almost nothing about what interest they would pay.

Every one of the nine representative examples is calculated on an assumed £1,200 credit limit. The published minimums are a long way below that: £500 on the Amazon Barclaycard and, in the Barclaycard Rewards summary box, £50. An applicant approved at £500 on the Amazon card pays the same 28.9% but has a quarter of the headroom the example assumes, and on a card with a £50 floor the £1,200 example describes a customer the applicant may not turn out to be.

The 0% clock starts before the balance arrives

On all four Platinum variants the promotional period is counted "From the date you open your account", and the balance has to be moved "within 60 days" of that date to qualify for the offer at all. Those two conditions pull in opposite directions, and nothing in the advertising reconciles them. An applicant who opens the account and transfers the same week gets very nearly the full 29 months of 0% on the transferred balance. An applicant who waits until day 59 — still inside the rules, still entitled to the offer — gets about 27 months, because two of them have already been spent on an account with nothing on it.

Put that in money. On a £3,000 balance, Barclaycard's 24.9% standard rate costs roughly £747 over a year, or about £62 a month. Two months of the promotional period lost to a slow transfer is therefore worth about £124 of borrowing — more than the £103.50 the 3.45% fee cost in the first place. The offer is not misdescribed; the word "up to" is on the front of every one of these cards. But the thing that decides whether "up to 29 months" means 29 or 27 is how fast the customer acts after approval, and that is the opposite of the advice an applicant usually gets, which is to take time over a transfer.

The transfer rules themselves are set out in the terms for the mid-length Platinum offer: a minimum of £250 across up to five separate transfers for a new customer, £100 for an existing one, and balances accepted only from "cards issued by other banks". The offer is described there as available to new customers only, and the promotional term in that document is 24 months — a fourth number for a card family the main grid presents as four fixed options.

£103.50, £59.70, or nothing at all

The four Platinum variants invite a sum that Barclaycard does not print, which is the fee expressed as a monthly cost of the interest-free months it buys. On a £3,000 transfer the 3.45% fee is £103.50 for up to 29 months, which is £3.57 a month. The 1.99% fee is £59.70 for up to 20 months, which is £2.99 a month. The no-fee card gives 12 months for nothing, which is unbeatable per month but runs out first.

So the shorter paid card is the cheaper one per month of 0%, by about 16%, and the longer one is only worth its extra £43.80 to someone who genuinely needs the extra nine months. A borrower who can clear £3,000 in twenty months pays £59.70 on the 1.99% card and £103.50 on the 29-month card for a benefit they never use. A borrower who can clear it in twelve pays nothing at all. The fee is a one-off percentage added to the balance, so it also becomes part of the debt being repaid, which on the 3.45% card means repaying £3,103.50 rather than £3,000.

Against the alternative, all three are cheap. Carrying £3,000 on a 24.9% card for a single year costs in the region of £747 in interest; £103.50 buys more than two years without it. That is the case for the product, and it survives the arithmetic above intact — the arithmetic only decides which of the four to pick.

The transfer Barclaycard will not take

Barclaycard's help pages are blunt about the limit of the offer: "we can't accept transfers from other credit card or loan accounts held with Barclays Bank UK PLC or any other Barclays Group company. This includes our Partnership card – Hilton Honors." The balance transfer terms put the same rule from the other direction — "you can't make a transfer to any other Barclaycard or our partner cards" — and the mid-length Platinum terms restrict incoming balances to "cards issued by other banks".

Two things make this wider than the group exclusions other issuers publish. The first is that it covers loan accounts, not just cards, so a Barclayloan cannot be moved onto a 0% card inside the same group. The second is that it reaches a co-branded card most customers would not think of as a Barclays product at all: the Hilton Honors partnership card is named in the exclusion by Barclaycard itself. For anyone already banking with Barclays and carrying a balance there — and Barclays UK had 14.1 million digitally active customers at 30 June 2026 — this is the first question to settle, because it decides whether the whole range is available or none of it is.

Both ends of the same transaction

Barclaycard is unusual among UK card brands in sitting on both sides of a card payment: it issues cards to shoppers and it also acquires card transactions for merchants, which is where a large share of the brand's revenue comes from. The Payment Systems Regulator measured that side of the business in its card-acquiring market review, MR18/1.8, published in November 2021. Over 2014 to 2018, it found, Barclaycard and Worldpay together "accounted for [70-80]% of card transactions by volume and [60-70]% by value", and the two of them supplied card-acquiring services to 50 to 60% of large merchants. Mastercard and Visa transactions, which is what the review covered, were about 98% of all UK card payments in 2018.

The precise splits between the two firms are redacted in the public report, so the honest reading is that Barclaycard is one of two dominant acquirers rather than that it holds any particular share on its own. It matters to a cardholder for one reason: the interchange a merchant's acquirer collects is part of what funds the 0% offers, the cashback and the Avios on the issuing side. A card paying 0.25% back on everyday spending is not a gift; it is a share of the fee the shop pays, returned to the customer to keep the card in the wallet.

Where £20 a month earns its keep, and where it does not

The two Avios cards are the clearest case on the range of a fee-paying and a free version of the same product, and Barclaycard publishes enough to settle the comparison without guessing at what an Avios is worth. The free card earns 1 Avios per £1, pays 5,000 Avios for £1,000 of spending in the first three months, and gives a British Airways cabin upgrade voucher — or 7,000 bonus Avios instead — at £20,000 of spending in twelve months. Avios Plus costs £20 a month, earns 1.5 Avios per £1, pays 25,000 Avios for £3,000 in the first three months, and reaches the same voucher at £10,000.

Because both cards price the voucher's alternative at 7,000 Avios, Barclaycard has effectively published its own exchange rate, and the comparison can be done in Avios rather than in pounds. At £10,000 of annual spending, Avios Plus returns 15,000 Avios plus the voucher; the free card returns 10,000 and no voucher. The £240 fee therefore buys 12,000 Avios, or 2p each. At £20,000 of spending the free card earns its own voucher, so Plus returns 30,000 plus a voucher against the free card's 20,000 plus a voucher — 10,000 extra Avios for the same £240, which is 2.4p each. Below £10,000 neither card earns a voucher and the difference is only the extra half an Avios per pound: at £5,000 of spending that is 2,500 Avios for £240, or about 9.6p each.

The fee card is therefore at its best in a fairly narrow band, roughly £10,000 to £20,000 of annual spending, and gets slightly worse rather than better above it. The first year is a separate calculation: the extra 20,000 Avios in the welcome bonus is worth considerably more than £240 on any sensible valuation, which makes Avios Plus a good first-year card and a questionable second-year one. Barclaycard publishes no redemption value for an Avios anywhere on its own site, so the 2p and 9.6p figures above are costs per Avios, not statements about whether that is good value — that depends on what the holder redeems them for, and only they can decide it.

Cash is priced on a different system

The interest-free period on purchases does not exist on cash. Barclaycard charges 27.9% compound equivalent on cash borrowing "daily, from the date we add the transaction to your account, until you've repaid your cash balance in full" — and its own fee page spells out that this applies "even if you pay your total outstanding balance in full by your payment due date". On top of the interest there is a transaction fee of £2.99 on withdrawals under £100 and 2.99% above it, with gambling transactions treated as cash at the same typical 2.99%.

The rest of the charge list is short. The default fee is £12 for each late payment, missed payment or payment below the minimum, and Barclaycard caps it: "we won't charge this fee more than four times in a year." A default fee carries no interest for 28 days from the date the customer is told about it, and the standard rate after that. Non-sterling transactions cost 2.99%, with the Barclaycard Rewards card the exception — its summary box records no non-sterling fee on purchases, a 0.00% cash transaction fee, no over-limit fee and no returned payment fee.

The minimum repayment, as published in the Amazon Barclaycard summary box dated July 2024, is the highest of £5, 1.00% of the balance, or 100% of the interest plus 100% of any default fees plus 1.00% of the rest. Payments are applied to the highest-rate balance first. The same document shows how the interest rate is actually quoted: a simple rate of 25.65% against a compound equivalent of 28.9%, with 2.1375% as the monthly figure — the number that decides what lands on a statement.

Four rules the regulator wrote for cards and nothing else

Credit cards have their own chapter of post-contract rules in the FCA's Consumer Credit sourcebook, and they are worth knowing because they are entitlements rather than features. CONC 6.7.4R, in its current form from 1 October 2021, requires that a firm "must first allocate a repayment to the debt subject to the highest rate of interest" — which is why a Platinum cardholder who spends on a 0% transfer card still sees overpayments attack the 24.9% purchase balance first rather than the 0% one.

CONC 6.7.8R, from 19 March 2019, requires firms to let a customer "reduce or decline offers to increase the credit limit" at any time, and to decline receiving such offers altogether. CONC 6.7.7R, from 19 December 2018, goes further and stops a firm raising a limit at all where the customer has asked for no increases or is at risk of financial difficulties. Both are opt-outs a cardholder has to exercise; neither happens by default.

The fourth is the persistent debt regime. CONC 6.7.27R, also from 19 December 2018, requires a monthly assessment of whether a customer has paid more in interest and charges than principal over the preceding 18 months, and CONC 6.7.30R and 6.7.31R set out what the firm must then do to help the balance come down without causing financial harm. On a card taken out for a 29-month transfer, the 18-month mark falls while the 0% is still running, which is exactly when the rule is most useful and least likely to be noticed.

Three Barclaycard pages, three different Platinum offers

Reading Barclaycard's own site on 5 October 2026 produced three incompatible descriptions of the same card family, and none of them is marked as superseded. The main card grid and the balance transfer landing page both listed four Platinum variants at 24.9% APR representative — up to 29 months at 3.45%, up to 21 months on purchases and transfers at 3.45%, up to 20 months at 1.99% and up to 12 months at 0%. A separate Platinum purchase-and-transfer page on the same domain gave 20.9% APR representative, 18 months at 0% on both purchases and transfers, and a 2.9% transfer fee. The Barclays-branded version of the range, on barclays.co.uk, offered a 30-month transfer card and two 22-month variants. The terms document for the mid-length offer refers to a 24-month promotional period.

The Rewards card has the same problem in a sharper form. The card grid listed it at 28.9% APR representative and the Barclaycard Rewards summary box agreed, at a 25.65% simple rate and a 28.9% compound equivalent. The dedicated Rewards product page, fetched twice on 5 October 2026, gave 22.9% APR representative and a 22.9% purchase rate, and still carried the line "Get an additional £25 cashback when you spend in the first month of receiving your card. Offer ends 6 March 2020." A page advertising a promotion that closed six and a half years ago is not a second set of terms; it is a page nobody has refreshed. The Forward card shows a milder version: 33.9% on the grid against 34.9% on the eligibility checker page.

The diagnosis matters more than the discrepancy. Where a summary box and a product page disagree, one of them is the regulated disclosure and the other is marketing. Here the summary boxes are current and internally consistent, and it is the product pages that have drifted. The practical consequence is that the eligibility checker — which returns a personalised result without touching the applicant's credit rating, and which on Barclaycard Forward gives a yes or no rather than a score — is the only statement of what any individual has actually been offered. On this range that is the step to take before believing a headline, not after.

The numbers Barclaycard keeps to itself

Three gaps are worth naming. Barclaycard's own UK complaints return could not be read on 5 October 2026: the pages holding it on home.barclays returned a 403 error on every attempt, as did the equivalent page on barclays.co.uk, so the January to June 2026 figures could not be quoted or checked. The complaints route itself is published clearly enough — 0800 151 0900 or 0333 200 9090, or PO Box 10200, LE18 9ER, with an update promised at four weeks, a final response after eight, and the Financial Ombudsman Service after that — but the volumes and uphold rates behind it were not readable from here.

Second, the acquiring figures above are the newest public ones and they are old: a report published in November 2021 on data from 2014 to 2018, with the firm-level splits redacted. Barclaycard publishes no current figure for merchants served or transactions processed anywhere a search reaches. Third, no ratings agency scores a credit card. Defaqto rates insurance rather than credit, and nothing independent publishes a star rating for a Barclaycard product, so the absence here is a gap in the market rather than a poor result for Barclaycard.

For context on the market the range sits in, UK Finance's Household Finance Review for the second quarter of 2026, published in September 2026, recorded that 47.3% of total card debt was interest-bearing in April 2026 — "the lowest figure on record" — rising slightly to 47.6% by June but remaining effectively at a record low. Slightly more than half of all card balances in the country are now carried by people who clear them every month and pay no interest at all. That is the figure that decides which half of this range makes sense for a given reader: the Platinum cards are built for the interest-bearing half, and the Rewards, Amazon and Avios cards only work for the other one.

Where it wins

  • The longest 0% balance transfer on the range runs for up to 29 months, which is close to two and a half years of interest-free borrowing for a one-off 3.45% fee
  • Four Platinum variants let the borrower pick where the cost sits: 3.45% for up to 29 months, 1.99% for up to 20, or no fee at all for up to 12
  • The eligibility checker returns a result without affecting the applicant's credit rating, and on Barclaycard Forward a yes means approval subject to status rather than a score
  • A full summary box per card, published in HTML rather than buried in a PDF, giving the simple monthly rate, the compound equivalent, the minimum credit limit, the minimum repayment formula and the payment allocation order
  • Barclaycard Rewards charges no annual fee, no over-limit fee, no returned payment fee, a 0.00% cash transaction fee and nothing on non-sterling purchases, with 0.25% cashback on everyday spending
  • Payments are applied to the highest-rate balance first, which is a regulatory requirement under CONC 6.7.4R but is still set out plainly in each summary box
  • Barclaycard Avios Plus pays 25,000 Avios for £3,000 of spending in the first three months, which is five times the free Avios card's welcome bonus for three times the spend

Where it falls short

  • The 0% period is counted from the date the account is opened rather than the date the balance arrives, and transfers are allowed up to 60 days later — so an applicant who transfers late can lose two of the advertised 29 months before the balance has moved
  • Barclaycard's own pages disagree about its own rates. On 5 October 2026 the card grid listed Barclaycard Rewards at 28.9% APR representative while the dedicated Rewards page showed 22.9% and still advertised a £25 cashback offer that closed on 6 March 2020
  • The Platinum purchase-and-transfer page showed 20.9% APR representative with 18 months at 0% and a 2.9% fee, against 24.9% and 21 months at 3.45% for the equivalent card on the main grid, and 22 months on the Barclays-branded version of the same range
  • The group exclusion is wider than most issuers': it rules out transfers from any Barclays Group credit card or loan, and specifically names the Hilton Honors partnership card
  • Cash interest runs daily from the transaction date and is not cleared by paying the statement balance in full, which is the opposite of how the interest-free period works on purchases
  • The £20 monthly fee on Barclaycard Avios Plus only beats the free Avios card between about £10,000 and £20,000 of annual spending, because above £20,000 the free card earns its own upgrade voucher
  • Every representative APR on the range is quoted on an assumed £1,200 credit limit while the published minimum limits are far lower — £500 on the Amazon Barclaycard and £50 on Barclaycard Rewards
  • The newest public figure for Barclaycard's scale as a card acquirer comes from a 2021 regulatory report built on 2014 to 2018 data, and Barclaycard's own UK complaints return could not be read at all on 5 October 2026

Common questions

How long do I have to make a balance transfer on a Barclaycard Platinum card?

Sixty days from the date the account is opened. Barclaycard's balance transfer pages state that transfers "must be made within 60 days" to get the 0% offer. The promotional period itself is counted "From the date you open your account", not from the date of the transfer, so a late transfer shortens the interest-free run. On an "up to 29 months" offer, transferring on day 59 leaves roughly 27 months.

Can I move a balance from a Barclays credit card or loan to a Barclaycard?

No. Barclaycard states that it "can't accept transfers from other credit card or loan accounts held with Barclays Bank UK PLC or any other Barclays Group company. This includes our Partnership card – Hilton Honors." The exclusion covers loans as well as cards, and the balance transfer terms add that "you can't make a transfer to any other Barclaycard or our partner cards" either.

What does a 3.45% transfer fee cost in money?

On a £3,000 balance, £103.50, added to the balance so the amount repaid becomes £3,103.50. The 1.99% fee on the shorter card is £59.70 and the 12-month card has no fee. For comparison, carrying £3,000 at Barclaycard's 24.9% standard rate for a year costs in the region of £747, so £103.50 for up to 29 months at 0% is cheap borrowing even allowing for the fee.

Why is the representative APR on Barclaycard Avios Plus 80.1% when the purchase rate is 29.9%?

Because a representative APR has to include compulsory charges, and Avios Plus carries a £20 monthly fee — £240 a year on an assumed £1,200 credit limit. The free Barclaycard Avios card has the same 29.9% purchase rate and a 29.9% APR representative. The 50-point gap between the two cards is the fee, not the interest, so 80.1% is not what a borrower would pay on a balance.

Is Barclaycard's eligibility checker a full credit application?

No. Barclaycard states that using it "won't have any affect on your credit rating". On most cards it returns a score indicating the likelihood of approval; on Barclaycard Forward, "get a yes and you'll be approved (subject to status)". Given that Barclaycard's own product pages currently show conflicting rates and promotional lengths for the Platinum and Rewards cards, the checker is the more reliable statement of what an individual has been offered.

Does the interest-free period apply to cash withdrawals on a Barclaycard?

No. Cash interest is charged at 27.9% compound equivalent "daily, from the date we add the transaction to your account, until you've repaid your cash balance in full", and Barclaycard confirms this applies "even if you pay your total outstanding balance in full by your payment due date". There is also a transaction fee of £2.99 under £100 or 2.99% above it, and gambling transactions are charged as cash.

Our verdict

Barclaycard is the strongest balance transfer shelf of the five big card issuers covered here, and the four Platinum variants are the reason: 3.45% for up to 29 months, 1.99% for up to 20, or nothing at all for up to 12 is a genuine choice rather than a single offer with a single price. The 1.99% card costs £59.70 on a £3,000 balance against £103.50 for the longer one, which works out cheaper per month of interest-free borrowing, and either beats the roughly £747 a year that 24.9% would cost on the same balance. Two things decide whether the headline applies to you. The 0% clock starts when the account opens, not when the balance lands, so the transfer wants doing in the first week rather than the sixtieth day; and nothing already inside Barclays Group can be moved across, loans included. The rewards end of the range is thinner. Avios Plus at £240 a year is only worth its fee in a narrow band of spending, the Rewards card's own product page has not been updated since an offer expired in March 2020, and the three different Platinum offers live on Barclaycard's site at once mean the eligibility checker, not the advertising, is the only reliable statement of what you have been offered.
