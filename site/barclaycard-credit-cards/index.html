Barclaycard Credit Cards Review 2026: Nine Cards, 24.9% to 80.1% APR

Rates and terms checked 12 October 2026 · Credit Cards · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Barclaycard lists nine personal credit cards at representative APRs running from 24.9% to 80.1% variable, and its longest balance transfer offer is 0% for up to 29 months on a 3.45% transfer fee. Every representative example on the range is built on an assumed £1,200 credit limit. Figures re-read on 12 October 2026 against Barclaycard's own card pages, summary boxes, terms and fee schedules: not one rate, fee or promotional length had moved since the previous check a week earlier.

This is the oldest card brand in the country — Barclaycard issued the first UK credit card in 1966 — and the range is unusually open about where the money is made. Four of the nine cards are Platinum balance transfer variants that earn nothing at all while the 0% runs, and the Barclaycard Rewards card charges no annual fee, no over-limit fee, no returned payment fee and nothing on non-sterling purchases, with a minimum credit limit of £50. The arithmetic in the paperwork stands up to a calculator too: the Rewards summary box gives a monthly rate of 2.1375% and a simple rate of 25.65%, which compounds to 28.9% to the decimal, and the Forward box's 2.4616% and 29.54% compound to 33.9% the same way.

Two rules decide whether any of it is available to you, and both are worth settling before you spend an application. Barclaycard's balance transfer terms state flatly that "Existing customers are not eligible to apply for a new Barclaycard balance transfer card", so a Platinum card is a product for people who do not already hold one. And nothing inside the group can be moved across: Barclaycard "can't accept transfers from other credit card or loan accounts held with Barclays Bank UK PLC or any other Barclays Group company. This includes our Partnership card – Hilton Honors." That covers loans as well as cards.

Nine cards doing four separate jobs

Four of the nine are Platinum balance transfer variants, all at 24.9% APR representative on a 24.9% p.a. variable purchase rate, and the grid separates them by what the transfer costs and how long the 0% lasts: up to 29 months for a 3.45% fee, up to 21 months on purchases and transfers together for 3.45%, up to 20 months for 1.99%, and up to 12 months for nothing. Three of those four also carry a short run at 0% on purchases — up to three months on the 29-month, 20-month and 12-month cards — which the headline offers do not mention and which is worth knowing if the card is going to be used as well as loaded.

The Amazon Barclaycard sits at 28.9% APR representative with six months at 0% on purchases from the date the account opens, and pays 1% back on Amazon spending and 0.5% everywhere else. Barclaycard Rewards is also 28.9% and pays 0.25% cashback. Barclaycard Forward, the credit-building card, is listed at 33.9% with three months at 0% on purchases, a published limit range of £50 to £1,200 and a price promise offering a 3% interest rate reduction rather than cash back.

The two Avios cards are where the ladder breaks. Barclaycard Avios charges nothing and earns 1 Avios per £1 of eligible spending at 29.9% APR representative. Barclaycard Avios Plus charges £20 a month, earns 1.5 Avios per £1 at the same 29.9% purchase rate, and carries an 80.1% APR representative. The 50 percentage points between them are not a difference in borrowing cost — both charge 29.9% on purchases. They are £240 of annual fees expressed as an interest rate, because a representative APR has to include compulsory charges. Having that published rather than buried is to Barclaycard's credit, and it also means the 80.1% figure tells a borrower almost nothing about the interest they would actually pay.

Every one of the nine representative examples is calculated on an assumed £1,200 credit limit. The published minimums are a long way below it: £500 on the Amazon Barclaycard and £50 in the Rewards, Forward and Platinum Purchase summary boxes. An applicant approved at £500 on the Amazon card pays the same 28.9% on about two-fifths of the headroom the example assumes, £700 short of it, and on a card with a £50 floor the £1,200 example describes a customer the applicant may not turn out to be.

Ten balance transfer lengths, all of them called Platinum

Barclaycard advertises the Platinum family on more than one page, and on 12 October 2026 those pages did not agree. The main card grid and the balance transfer landing page both gave four variants at 24.9% APR representative — 29, 21, 20 and 12 months. A separate Platinum purchase-and-transfer page on the same domain gave 20.9% APR representative, 18 months at 0% on both purchases and transfers, and a 2.9% transfer fee. The Barclays-branded version of the range offered 30, 22, 19 and 14 months. The terms document for the mid-length offer refers to a 24-month promotional period. And a pre-approval landing page served one card under two different sets of numbers at once: its own title says "21 month 0% Purchase & 20 month 0% Balance Transfer" while the body of the same page says "22 months for purchases" and "0% interest balance transfer for 19 months".

Collected up, Barclaycard's own pages currently advertise balance transfer periods of 12, 14, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 24, 29 and 30 months on a card family the main grid presents as four fixed options. That is ten different lengths, and five different purchase periods — three, 18, 19, 21 and 22 months — behind two representative APRs, 24.9% and 20.9%, and three transfer fees, 3.45%, 2.9% and nothing.

None of this is hidden. The words "up to" sit on the front of every one of these offers, the pre-approval page says plainly that it is "Only available to new Barclaycard customers" arriving through one comparison group, and the representative example on each card carries the warning that "The balance transfer period and interest rates may differ from those shown." The practical consequence is simply that the advertised length is a ceiling attached to a route rather than a property of a card, so the only number that describes what any individual has been offered is the one the eligibility checker returns — and that is a step to take before believing a headline, not after.

The same fee buys a month more through the other door

The gap between Barclaycard's two retail front doors is small enough to sound like rounding and large enough to be worth a minute of a reader's time. On barclaycard.co.uk the longest Platinum transfer card is up to 29 months for a 3.45% fee. On barclays.co.uk it is up to 30 months for the same 3.45% fee. On a £3,000 balance the fee is £103.50 either way, which is £3.57 a month of interest-free borrowing on the 29-month card and £3.45 a month on the 30-month one. The fee-free card shows the same pattern more sharply: up to 12 months at 0% with no transfer fee on barclaycard.co.uk, up to 14 months on barclays.co.uk, for a cost of nothing in both cases.

The middle of the range is not better or worse so much as differently cut. Barclaycard's grid pairs 21 months on transfers with 21 months on purchases. The Barclays-branded range splits the same idea in two, offering 22 months on transfers with 19 on purchases, or 22 on purchases with 19 on transfers, both at 3.45%. A borrower who knows which half of the card they will use can get an extra month out of the Barclays version; a borrower who wants both gets a month less.

Neither site tells a reader the other exists, and nothing on either marks one as superseded. Both are Barclays Bank UK PLC, both carry firm reference 759676, and an applicant has no way of knowing from inside one journey that the other is a month longer. Checking both before applying costs two minutes and, on the fee-free card, is worth two months of interest-free borrowing.

The 0% clock starts before the balance arrives

On all four Platinum variants the promotional period is counted "From the date you open your account", and the balance has to be moved "within 60 days" of that date to qualify for the offer at all. Those two conditions pull in opposite directions, and nothing in the advertising reconciles them. An applicant who opens the account and transfers the same week gets very nearly the full 29 months of 0% on the transferred balance. An applicant who waits until day 59 — still inside the rules, still entitled to the offer — gets about 27, because two of them have already been spent on an account with nothing on it. The Platinum Purchase summary box ties the fee to the same clock, charging it "on transfers made within 60 days of opening your account".

Put that in money. Barclaycard quotes 24.9% on the Platinum cards as a compound equivalent, so a £3,000 balance carried for a full year at the standard rate costs about £747, or £62 a month. Two months of the promotional period lost to a slow transfer is therefore worth roughly £124 of borrowing — more than the £103.50 the 3.45% fee cost in the first place. The thing that decides whether "up to 29 months" means 29 or 27 is how fast the customer acts after approval, which is the opposite of the advice an applicant usually gets.

The transfer mechanics are set out in two places and they do not quite match either. The general balance transfer terms allow up to five transfers during an application and up to six on an existing account at any time, with a minimum of £250 for a new customer and £100 for an existing one, and give the deadline only as "Make your transfer before your offer close date." The mid-length Platinum terms are the document that puts a number on it: a 24-month period starting from account opening, a transfer that "needs to be taken out within 60 days of opening the account", £250 across up to five transfers, new customers only, and balances accepted only from "cards issued by other banks".

£103.50, £59.70, or nothing at all

The four Platinum variants invite a sum Barclaycard does not print, which is the fee expressed as a monthly cost of the interest-free months it buys. On a £3,000 transfer the 3.45% fee is £103.50 for up to 29 months, which is £3.57 a month. The 1.99% fee is £59.70 for up to 20 months, which is £2.99 a month. The no-fee card gives 12 months for nothing, which is unbeatable per month but runs out first.

So the shorter paid card is the cheaper one per month of 0%, by about 16%, and the longer one is only worth its extra £43.80 to someone who genuinely needs the extra nine months. A borrower who can clear £3,000 in twenty months pays £59.70 on the 1.99% card and £103.50 on the 29-month card for nine months they never use. A borrower who can clear it in twelve pays nothing at all. The fee is a one-off percentage added to the balance, so it also becomes part of the debt being repaid: on the 3.45% card that means clearing £3,103.50 rather than £3,000.

Against the alternative, all of them are cheap. Carrying £3,000 at 24.9% for a single year costs in the region of £747; £103.50 buys more than two years without it. That is the case for the product and it survives the arithmetic intact — the arithmetic only decides which of the four to pick, and the answer is usually not the longest one.

The transfer Barclaycard will not take

Barclaycard's help pages are blunt about the limit of the offer: "we can't accept transfers from other credit card or loan accounts held with Barclays Bank UK PLC or any other Barclays Group company. This includes our Partnership card – Hilton Honors." The balance transfer terms put the same rule from the other direction — "you can't make a transfer to any other Barclaycard or our partner cards" — and the mid-length Platinum terms restrict incoming balances to "cards issued by other banks".

Two things make this wider than the group exclusions other issuers publish. The first is that it covers loan accounts, not just cards, so a Barclays loan cannot be moved onto a 0% card inside the same group. The second is that it reaches a co-branded card most customers would not think of as a Barclays product at all: the Hilton Honors partnership card is named in the exclusion by Barclaycard itself.

Sitting on top of both is the rule in the balance transfer terms that there is nothing to apply for: "Existing customers are not eligible to apply for a new Barclaycard balance transfer card." An existing holder can still move a balance, but only "if you have a balance transfer offer on your existing Barclaycard" — an offer Barclaycard decides to make, not one a customer can go and get. With Barclays UK reporting 13.9 million digitally active customers at 31 December 2025, up from 13.4 million a year earlier and excluding Tesco Bank from both counts, that is a large number of people for whom the first question is not which Platinum card to pick but whether the shelf is open at all.

One company, two famous names, and a gap in the list

The group exclusion is written as a rule about a company and advertised as a list of brands, and since 2024 the two have not described the same thing. On 1 November 2024, after High Court approval on 17 October, Tesco Bank's banking business transferred to Barclays — "credit card, personal loan, savings and Clubcard Pay+ accounts and products", with insurance, travel money, gift cards and ATMs left behind. Barclays booked a £556m day one gain on the acquisition. Tesco Bank's own site now carries the plainest possible statement of what it is: "Tesco Bank is a trading name of Barclays Bank UK PLC."

Barclaycard is a trading name of the same company. So on the face of the rule — no transfers "from other credit card or loan accounts held with Barclays Bank UK PLC" — a Tesco Bank credit card balance is a balance held with the company doing the excluding. Barclaycard's published list of named exclusions mentions only the Hilton Honors partnership card and says nothing about Tesco Bank anywhere a reader would find it.

Which way that falls is not stated on any Barclaycard page read on 12 October 2026, and it is not a question to guess at: it decides whether a Tesco Bank balance can be moved to a 29-month 0% card or whether the application is wasted. Anyone carrying one should ask Barclaycard before applying rather than reading the brand list as exhaustive. The honest version of the criticism is not that Barclaycard has misled anybody but that its exclusion list is two years behind its own corporate structure, and the brand most likely to catch a customer out is the one the list leaves off.

Your standard rate is the Bank's rate plus a margin nobody prints

The 28.9% on a Rewards card and the 24.9% on a Platinum look like fixed properties of the product. They are not. Barclaycard's help pages state that "standard and cash interest rates now move in line with the Bank of England Base Rate", that when the Base Rate changes they move "up or down, by the same amount", and that "This direct link is to make sure customers can see how a change to the Base Rate directly affects their interest rates." The terms and conditions say the same thing in contract language — "Your simple standard and cash interest rates will move up and down in line with changes to the Bank of England Base Rate" — and set out the mechanics: a Base Rate change takes effect the day after the next statement date and needs no notice, while any other rate increase carries "at least 30 days' notice unless we reduce any of your interest rates", and "Your promotional interest rate will not move in line with the Base Rate."

That is a genuinely useful piece of transparency and it has a hole in the middle. Bank Rate has been 3.75% since before the 17 September 2026 decision held it there. A one-for-one link means the Rewards card's 25.65% simple rate is Bank Rate plus a margin of about 21.90 percentage points, and that if Bank Rate rose a point the simple rate would become 26.65% the day after the next statement. Barclaycard publishes the rule and the total but never the margin, so a cardholder can work out what a rate rise will do to them and cannot see how much of today's rate is the Bank's decision and how much is the lender's.

The promotional carve-out is the part worth acting on. A 0% balance transfer rate is fixed for its agreed period and does not track, so a Base Rate rise during a 29-month offer leaves the transferred balance alone and lands entirely on anything spent on the card. On a Platinum variant with three months at 0% on purchases and 29 on transfers, that is the whole of the fourth month onwards.

An instalment plan jumps the repayment queue

Barclaycard's summary boxes carry a product most of its advertising does not mention: an instalment or purchase plan, offered at "0% (we will tell you when you have an offer available)", which carves a chunk of spending out of the main balance and repays it on a fixed monthly schedule. The Rewards box says "Any instalment plan you take out is interest free until the end of the plan" and defines the rest of the card around it: "Your main balance is your current statement balance less any instalment plan balance."

The order in which money is applied is where it matters, and the box prints it. Payments "will be applied in the following order": first "Make any purchase plan payments due for that month"; then reduce the main balance, "start[ing] with the balances charged at the highest interest rate first"; and then, last of all, "If you've taken out any purchase plans, to reduce any remaining purchase plan balance". A plan instalment is therefore taken ahead of the most expensive balance on the card, and the plan balance itself is cleared after everything else.

Nothing about that is improper — a plan instalment is a contractual amount due, not a choice about allocation, and the plan is at 0% so there is no interest being protected. It does change what an overpayment buys. A cardholder paying £200 against a 24.9% purchase balance while a £75 plan instalment is due is attacking the expensive balance with £125, not £200. The minimum payment moves too: the Platinum Purchase box sets it at the highest of £5, 2.25% of the main balance plus any instalment due, or interest and fees plus 1% of the rest plus any instalment due. And the plan rate sits outside the Base Rate link — "Base Rate changes will not apply to your promotional or Instalment Plan interest rates" — so a plan is the one part of the card a rate rise cannot reach.

Where £20 a month earns its keep, and where it does not

The two Avios cards are the clearest case on the range of a fee-paying and a free version of the same product, and Barclaycard publishes enough to settle the comparison without anyone having to guess what an Avios is worth. The free card earns 1 Avios per £1, pays 5,000 Avios for £1,000 of spending in the first three months, and gives a British Airways cabin upgrade voucher — or 7,000 bonus Avios instead — at £20,000 of spending in twelve months. Avios Plus costs £20 a month, earns 1.5 Avios per £1, pays 25,000 Avios for £3,000 in the first three months, and reaches the same voucher at £10,000. It also prices an airport lounge visit at £18.50 per pass per person.

Because both cards price the voucher's alternative at 7,000 Avios, Barclaycard has effectively published its own exchange rate, and the comparison can be done in Avios rather than in pounds. At £10,000 of annual spending, Avios Plus returns 15,000 Avios plus the voucher; the free card returns 10,000 and no voucher. The £240 fee therefore buys 12,000 Avios, or 2p each. At £20,000 the free card earns its own voucher, so Plus returns 30,000 plus a voucher against 20,000 plus a voucher — 10,000 extra Avios for the same £240, which is 2.4p each. Below £10,000 neither card earns a voucher and the difference is only the extra half an Avios per pound: at £5,000 that is 2,500 Avios for £240, or about 9.6p each.

The fee card is therefore at its best in a fairly narrow band, roughly £10,000 to £20,000 of annual spending, and gets slightly worse rather than better above it. The first year is a separate calculation: the extra 20,000 Avios in the welcome bonus is worth considerably more than £240 on any sensible valuation, which makes Avios Plus a good first-year card and a questionable second-year one. Barclaycard publishes no redemption value for an Avios anywhere on its own site, so the 2p and 9.6p figures above are costs per Avios rather than statements about value — that depends on what the holder redeems them for, and only they can decide it.

Cash is priced on a different system

The interest-free period on purchases does not exist on cash. Barclaycard charges interest on cash borrowing "daily, from the date we add the transaction to your account, until you've repaid your cash balance in full", and its own page spells out that this "always applies, even if you pay your total outstanding balance in full by your payment due date". On top of the interest there is a transaction fee — "£2.99 if your transaction is less than £100, and 2.99% if your transaction is more than £100" — with gambling transactions "always charged as a percentage, which is typically 2.99%".

The rate itself is not one number, which the advertising does not make obvious. Barclaycard's worked example on its cash page uses a 27.9% compound equivalent and then tells the reader "You can find your cash interest rate on your statement." The Platinum Purchase summary box prices cash at a 1.7325% monthly rate, a 20.79% simple rate and a 22.9% compound equivalent. The Amazon Barclaycard's summary box prices cash at exactly the same rate as purchases — 2.1375% a month, 25.65% simple, 28.9% compound — and the Forward box at 2.4616% a month and 29.54% simple. So cash on a Barclaycard can be cheaper or dearer than purchases depending which card it is, and the 27.9% in the illustration belongs to none of the three.

The rest of the charge list is short. The default fee is £12 for each late payment, missed payment or payment below the minimum, and Barclaycard caps it: "we will charge you a late payment fee no more than four times in a year." A default fee carries no interest for 28 days and the standard rate after that. Non-sterling purchases cost 2.99% with no minimum, with the Rewards card the exception — its box records no non-sterling fee on purchases, a 0.00% cash transaction fee, £0 over-limit and £0 returned payment. The over-limit fee is not uniform either: £12 in the Platinum Purchase box against £0 in the Rewards and Forward ones, and absent from the general fees page altogether.

Four rules the regulator wrote for cards and nothing else

Credit cards have their own chapter of post-contract rules in the FCA's Consumer Credit sourcebook, and they are worth knowing because they are entitlements rather than features. CONC 6.7.4R, in its current form from 1 October 2021, requires that a firm "must first allocate a repayment to the debt subject to the highest rate of interest" — which is why a Platinum cardholder who also spends on the card sees overpayments attack the 24.9% purchase balance before the 0% transfer, and why the instalment plan ordering above is the one thing that comes ahead of it.

CONC 6.7.8R, from 19 March 2019, requires firms to let a customer "reduce or decline offers to increase the credit limit" at any time, and to decline receiving such offers altogether. CONC 6.7.7R, from 19 December 2018, goes further and stops a firm raising a limit at all where the customer has asked for no increases or is at risk of financial difficulties. Both are opt-outs a cardholder has to exercise; neither happens by default, and on a card whose representative example assumes £1,200 against a £50 floor, a limit increase is the most likely thing to arrive uninvited.

The fourth is the persistent debt regime. CONC 6.7.27R, also from 19 December 2018, requires a monthly assessment of whether a customer has paid more in interest and charges than principal over the preceding 18 months, and CONC 6.7.30R and 6.7.31R set out what the firm must then do to help the balance come down without causing financial harm. On a card taken out for a 29-month transfer at 0%, the 18-month mark falls while the promotion is still running and no interest is being charged at all — which is exactly when the rule is least likely to bite and the balance most likely to still be there when the rate arrives.

The side of the business that is on its way out

Barclaycard has long been unusual among UK card brands in sitting on both sides of a card payment: issuing cards to shoppers and also acquiring card transactions for merchants. The Payment Systems Regulator measured that second business in its card-acquiring market review, MR18/1.8, published in November 2021. Over 2014 to 2018 it found that Barclaycard and Worldpay together "accounted for [70-80]% of card transactions by volume and [60-70]% by value", and that the two of them supplied card-acquiring services to 50 to 60% of large merchants. Mastercard and Visa transactions, which is what the review covered, were about 98% of all UK card payments in 2018. The firm-level splits are redacted, so the honest reading is that Barclaycard was one of two dominant acquirers rather than that it held any particular share alone.

That arrangement is being unwound. On 17 April 2025 Barclays announced what its own annual filing calls a "Long-term strategic partnership for Payment Acceptance business", with Brookfield Asset Management Ltd, "to grow and transform Barclays' Payment Acceptance business, previously referred to as the Merchant Acquiring business". Brookfield's own announcement sets out the shape: the two "will work to create a standalone entity over time", Barclays will invest "approximately £400 million in the Business", "the majority of which will occur during the first three years", and Brookfield may then acquire "an approximately 70 percent ownership interest" after the partnership's third year and up to its seventh, rising to "approximately 80 percent" if an incentive converts, leaving Barclays with "an ownership interest of approximately 20 percent".

It matters to a cardholder for one reason. The interchange a merchant's acquirer collects is part of what funds the 0% offers, the cashback and the Avios on the issuing side. A card paying 0.25% back on everyday spending is not a gift; it is a share of the fee the shop pays, returned to the customer to keep the card in the wallet. On the current plan the firm collecting that fee will, within a few years, be a business Barclays mostly does not own — and no later development in the partnership could be read from here, because Barclays' own newsroom and investor pages all refuse to load.

Blank boxes, a dead dispute route and a return nobody can read

Four gaps are worth naming, and the first is in the regulated paperwork rather than the marketing. Barclaycard serves its summary boxes as HTML, which is better than burying them in a PDF, but three of the four read on 12 October 2026 had fields missing. The Rewards and Forward boxes both print the heading "Your minimum payment will be the highest of the following:" and then nothing — checked twice on the Rewards box with a request for the text verbatim. The Platinum Purchase box leaves the promotional month counts, the transfer fee percentage and the compound equivalent on purchases and transfers blank, reading "0% interest for up to months from account opening" and "with a % fee". These look like templates whose offer-specific fields are filled in when a customer is given an offer, which is a reasonable way to build them and still leaves the public copy of a regulated disclosure incomplete. The Amazon Barclaycard's PDF, dated July 2024, is the one that is finished — and its minimum repayment formula of £5, 1.00% of the balance, or interest plus default fees plus 1.00% of the rest sits against 2.25% in the Platinum Purchase box, while the terms and conditions redact the figure to "(a percentage*)" and point the reader at their statement.

Second, the complaints page sends customers to a body that no longer exists. Having set out the route to the Financial Ombudsman Service, it offers an alternative: "If it's about one of our products or services you bought online, the Online Dispute Resolution (ODR) regulation may be able to help", explains that "The ODR platform was set up by the European Commission", states that "You can make a complaint to the ODR if you're an EU resident and you entered into your credit card agreement with us online", and begins "To make a complaint, you can fill out a form by visiting their website at". The European Commission's own notice is unambiguous: "The European Online Dispute Resolution (ODR) Platform is discontinued as of 20 July 2025, following the adoption of the Regulation - EU - 2024/3228." The route has been closed for nearly fifteen months and the page still offers it.

Third, Barclays' own UK complaints return still cannot be read. Every page holding it returned an error on 12 October 2026, as it did on 5 October and on 13 September, so the volumes and uphold rates behind that complaints route have now been unavailable on three separate attempts across a month. What Barclays does publish about its card book comes from its annual filing, and it is reassuring rather than otherwise: a 30 day arrears rate on total UK cards of 0.8% against 0.7% a year earlier, a 90 day rate steady at 0.2%, and a UK cards total coverage ratio easing to 4.3% from 4.8% in December 2024. Fourth, no ratings agency scores a credit card at all — Defaqto rates insurance rather than credit — so the absence of an independent star rating here is a gap in the market rather than a poor result for Barclaycard.

For context on the market the range sits in, UK Finance's Household Finance Review for the second quarter of 2026, published in September 2026 and re-checked unchanged, recorded that 47.3% of total card debt was interest-bearing in April 2026 — "the lowest figure on record" — ticking up to 47.6% by June but remaining "effectively at a record low proportion". Slightly more than half of all card balances in the country are carried by people who clear them every month and pay no interest. That is the figure that decides which half of this range makes sense for a given reader: the Platinum cards are built for the interest-bearing half, and the Rewards, Amazon and Avios cards only work for the other one.

Where it wins

  • The longest 0% balance transfer runs for up to 29 months on barclaycard.co.uk and up to 30 on barclays.co.uk, for the same one-off 3.45% fee
  • Four Platinum variants let the borrower pick where the cost sits: 3.45% for up to 29 months, 1.99% for up to 20, or no fee at all for up to 12, with up to 14 fee-free months through the Barclays-branded range
  • Three of the four Platinum cards add up to three months at 0% on purchases alongside the transfer offer, which the headlines do not mention
  • The eligibility checker returns a result without affecting the applicant's credit rating, and on Barclaycard Forward a yes means approval subject to status rather than a score
  • Standard and cash rates are contractually tied one-for-one to the Bank of England Base Rate, with 30 days' notice required for any other increase and promotional rates fixed for their term
  • Summary boxes are published in HTML rather than buried in a PDF, and the simple, monthly and compound rates in them reconcile to the decimal — 2.1375% and 25.65% compounding to 28.9%, 2.4616% and 29.54% to 33.9%
  • Barclaycard Rewards charges no annual fee, no over-limit fee, no returned payment fee, a 0.00% cash transaction fee and nothing on non-sterling purchases, with 0.25% cashback and a £50 minimum credit limit
  • Barclaycard Avios Plus pays 25,000 Avios for £3,000 of spending in the first three months, five times the free Avios card's welcome bonus for three times the spend
  • Barclays' own filing shows a UK cards 30 day arrears rate of 0.8% and a 90 day rate of 0.2%, with the coverage ratio easing to 4.3% from 4.8% a year earlier

Where it falls short

  • Existing Barclaycard holders cannot apply for a balance transfer card at all — the terms say so outright, and an existing customer can only transfer if Barclaycard has already put an offer on their account
  • The 0% period is counted from the date the account is opened rather than the date the balance arrives, and transfers are allowed up to 60 days later, so a late transfer can cost two of the advertised 29 months before the balance has moved
  • Barclaycard's own pages advertise ten different balance transfer lengths — 12, 14, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 24, 29 and 30 months — on a card family the main grid presents as four fixed options, and one pre-approval page gives 21 and 20 months in its title against 22 and 19 in its body
  • The two retail front doors sell different cards: up to 29 months and 12 fee-free months on barclaycard.co.uk against 30 and 14 on barclays.co.uk, for identical fees, and neither site mentions the other
  • Barclaycard Rewards is listed at 28.9% APR representative on the grid and in its summary box, while the dedicated Rewards product page shows 22.9% and still advertises a £25 cashback offer that closed on 6 March 2020
  • Tesco Bank has been a trading name of Barclays Bank UK PLC since the banking business transferred on 1 November 2024, but Barclaycard's list of excluded brands names only the Hilton Honors card, so whether a Tesco Bank balance can be moved is not stated anywhere
  • Three of the four summary boxes read had fields missing — no minimum payment formula at all on Rewards or Forward, and blank month counts, transfer fee and compound equivalent on Platinum Purchase
  • The complaints page still offers the European Commission's Online Dispute Resolution platform as a route, which was discontinued on 20 July 2025 under Regulation (EU) 2024/3228
  • Cash interest runs daily from the transaction date and is not cleared by paying the statement balance in full, and the rate is card-specific — 22.9% in one summary box, 28.9% in another and 27.9% in Barclaycard's own illustration
  • The £20 monthly fee on Barclaycard Avios Plus only beats the free Avios card between about £10,000 and £20,000 of annual spending, because above £20,000 the free card earns its own upgrade voucher
  • Every representative APR on the range is quoted on an assumed £1,200 credit limit while the published minimums are £500 on the Amazon Barclaycard and £50 on three others
  • Barclays' own UK complaints return could not be read on 12 October 2026, 5 October or 13 September, and the newest public figure for the acquiring side of the business is a 2021 regulatory report built on 2014 to 2018 data

Common questions

Can I get a Barclaycard balance transfer card if I already have a Barclaycard?

No. The balance transfer terms state that "Existing customers are not eligible to apply for a new Barclaycard balance transfer card." An existing holder can still move a balance, but only "if you have a balance transfer offer on your existing Barclaycard" — an offer Barclaycard chooses to make. The minimum transfer for an existing customer is £100 in total, against £250 for a new one, and an account can hold up to six transfers at any time.

How long do I have to make a balance transfer on a Barclaycard Platinum card?

Sixty days from the date the account is opened. Barclaycard's balance transfer pages state that "Transfers must be made within 60 days to benefit from the 0% offer", and the Platinum Purchase summary box ties the transfer fee to the same window. The promotional period itself runs "From the date you open your account", not from the date of the transfer, so a late transfer shortens the interest-free run — on an "up to 29 months" offer, transferring on day 59 leaves roughly 27.

Can I move a balance from a Barclays or Tesco Bank credit card to a Barclaycard?

Barclaycard states that it "can't accept transfers from other credit card or loan accounts held with Barclays Bank UK PLC or any other Barclays Group company. This includes our Partnership card – Hilton Honors." Tesco Bank's own site says "Tesco Bank is a trading name of Barclays Bank UK PLC", following the transfer of its banking business to Barclays on 1 November 2024, so on the face of the rule a Tesco Bank card is covered — but Barclaycard's published list of exclusions does not mention it. Ask before applying rather than assuming either way.

Why does barclays.co.uk show a 30-month balance transfer card when barclaycard.co.uk shows 29?

Both sites belong to Barclays Bank UK PLC, firm reference 759676, and both ranges are live. On 12 October 2026 barclaycard.co.uk offered up to 29 months at 0% for a 3.45% fee and up to 12 months fee-free, while barclays.co.uk offered up to 30 months for the same 3.45% fee and up to 14 months fee-free. On a £3,000 balance the £103.50 fee works out at £3.57 a month of interest-free borrowing over 29 months and £3.45 over 30. Neither site mentions the other, so it is worth checking both.

What does a 3.45% transfer fee cost in money?

On a £3,000 balance, £103.50, added to the balance so the amount to clear becomes £3,103.50. The 1.99% fee on the shorter card is £59.70 and the 12-month card has no fee. For comparison, carrying £3,000 at Barclaycard's 24.9% standard rate for a year costs in the region of £747, so £103.50 for up to 29 months at 0% is cheap borrowing even allowing for the fee — though at £2.99 a month against £3.57, the 20-month card is the better value per interest-free month.

Does my Barclaycard interest rate change when the Bank of England moves Base Rate?

Yes, on the standard and cash rates. Barclaycard states that they "move in line with the Bank of England Base Rate" and will do so "up or down, by the same amount", with the terms adding that a Base Rate change takes effect the day after your next statement date. Any other increase needs "at least 30 days' notice unless we reduce any of your interest rates". Promotional and instalment plan rates are the exception: "Base Rate changes will not apply to your promotional or Instalment Plan interest rates." Base Rate has stood at 3.75% since before the 17 September 2026 decision.

Why is the representative APR on Barclaycard Avios Plus 80.1% when the purchase rate is 29.9%?

Because a representative APR has to include compulsory charges, and Avios Plus carries a £20 monthly fee — £240 a year on an assumed £1,200 credit limit. The free Barclaycard Avios card has the same 29.9% purchase rate and a 29.9% APR representative. The 50-point gap between the two cards is the fee, not the interest, so 80.1% is not what a borrower would pay on a balance.

Does the interest-free period apply to cash withdrawals on a Barclaycard?

No. Interest is charged on cash "daily, from the date we add the transaction to your account, until you've repaid your cash balance in full", and Barclaycard confirms this "always applies, even if you pay your total outstanding balance in full by your payment due date". There is also a fee of £2.99 under £100 or 2.99% above it, and gambling transactions are charged as cash. The rate depends on the card: 22.9% compound equivalent in the Platinum Purchase summary box, 28.9% on the Amazon Barclaycard, and 27.9% in Barclaycard's own worked example.

Our verdict

Barclaycard is the strongest balance transfer shelf of the five big card issuers covered here, and the Platinum variants are the reason: 3.45% for up to 29 months, 1.99% for up to 20, or nothing at all for up to 12 is a genuine choice rather than a single offer with a single price. The 1.99% card costs £59.70 on a £3,000 balance against £103.50 for the longer one, which works out about 16% cheaper per month of interest-free borrowing, and either beats the roughly £747 a year that 24.9% would cost on the same balance. Nothing on the range moved between 5 and 12 October 2026 — every APR, fee and promotional length re-read the same. Three things decide whether the headline applies to you. You cannot apply at all if you already hold a Barclaycard; nothing inside Barclays Group can be moved across, loans included, and Tesco Bank has been part of that group since 1 November 2024 without appearing on the exclusion list; and the 0% clock starts when the account opens rather than when the balance lands, so the transfer wants doing in the first week. It is also worth opening both front doors, because barclays.co.uk sells 30 months for the same 3.45% fee and 14 fee-free months against 12. The rewards end is thinner. Avios Plus at £240 a year only earns its fee in a narrow band of spending, the Rewards card's own product page has not been updated since an offer expired in March 2020, and with ten different Platinum lengths advertised across Barclaycard's own pages the eligibility checker, not the advertising, is the only reliable statement of what you have been offered.
