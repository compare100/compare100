Barclays Mortgage Deals: 5.03% After Two Rate Rises in One Week

Rates and terms checked 16 October 2026 · Mortgage Deals · Compare100 editorial team

Barclays advanced £17.7bn of new mortgages in the first half of 2026, up from £15.4bn a year earlier, and its UK mortgage balances stood at £176.7bn on 30 June 2026 against £166.8bn twelve months before. Those are the bank's own numbers, filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission, and every figure on this page was re-checked on 16 October 2026.

Two things changed in the six weeks before that check, and both widen who Barclays will lend to. Its Fast-Track Remortgage service went live across the whole market on 7 September 2026 after an eight-month pilot with Mortgage Advice Bureau, offering an offer in 24 hours and completion in as little as five days. Then on 15 October 2026 Barclays began counting a limited company's profit after tax towards affordability for directors who own more than half the business, on residential and buy-to-let alike, with the restriction tied to property value removed.

The pricing has moved recently and in one direction. Barclays withdrew a market-leading 4.75% two-year fix and a 4.93% five-year fix on 29 September 2026, raising the two-year range by 30 basis points to 5.05% and the five-year to 5.03%, then repriced 62 further products upward on 2 October — its second increase inside a week. Across the ten biggest lenders, the average cheapest two-year remortgage fix moved from 4.68% at the start of September to 5.11% by the end of it, which on a £200,000 repayment mortgage over 25 years is almost £50 a month. No further Barclays repricing announcement could be found between 3 and 16 October 2026, so 5.03% is the most recent figure the bank has put its name to rather than today's shelf price.

What Barclays does better than most of the high street is lend against somebody else's money. Mortgage Boost puts a relative's income on the application without putting their name on the title deeds. Family Springboard was priced at 4.99% for 95% of a purchase price and 5.19% for the whole 100% in the June 2026 range. And since 8 April 2025 a council or housing association tenant exercising the Right to Buy can borrow the entire discounted price with no deposit of their own.

A year of repricing, rung by rung

Barclays reprices often, and the direction reversed in the autumn. Taking the bank's own announcements in order through 2026, the two-year fix at 60% loan to value with an £899 fee went from 3.57% to 3.65% on 5 February, to 4.25% on 19 March in a round that touched 62 residential products at once, then down to 4.39% on 29 May, 4.30% on 19 June and 4.25% on 10 July, before the September rises took the whole ladder past 5%. Read those two announcements together and a gap appears: the March round left that product at 4.25% and the May round cut it from 4.60%, so it had drifted back up by more than a third of a point in between without a consumer announcement either run of this page could find.

The 9 July round was the deepest cut of the year: reductions of up to 0.66%, which took the fee-free two-year fix at 90% loan to value from 5.45% to 4.79%, the Green Home version of the same product from 5.35% to 4.69%, and the fee-free two-year fix at 95% loan to value to 5.11%. The round Barclays announced on 6 August, and said was live that day, cut the ten-year fix at 60% loan to value with a £999 fee by a full 50 basis points from 5.62% to 5.12% and the 80% equivalent from 6.03% to 5.53%, took the five-year at 60% with an £899 fee from 4.67% to 4.58%, and introduced a Great Escape three-year remortgage fix at 4.88% fee-free with a £50,000 minimum and a £2m maximum loan.

Then it turned. On 7 September the two-year fix at 90% loan to value with an £899 fee rose from 4.96% to 5.14%, the fee-free equivalent from 5.11% to 5.29%, the two-year at 95% from 5.35% to 5.53%, the three-year at 90% from 5.04% to 5.22%, the five-year at 90% from 4.85% to 5.03% and the five-year at 95% from 5.30% to 5.48%. Three weeks later the 4.75% and 4.93% headline deals went, and the 2 October round followed. David Hollingworth of L&C Mortgages put it down to rising funding costs and warned of more to come; his firm's own tracking of the ten biggest lenders is where the 4.68% to 5.11% move comes from, and the £50 a month that costs a £200,000 borrower is about £600 a year.

Bank Rate itself has not moved. The Monetary Policy Committee held at 3.75% on 17 September 2026, against August CPI of 3.1%, and the Bank's own page gives 5 November 2026 as the next decision. Fixed rates are priced off swaps rather than Bank Rate, which is why Barclays could raise its range twice in a week with the base rate unchanged — and why a borrower watching the Bank rather than the swap market will keep being surprised.

£37,500 of income, £270,000 of mortgage: the Mortgage Boost sum

Mortgage Boost is a joint borrower sole proprietor arrangement, and Barclays publishes the arithmetic rather than describing it. A buyer earning £37,500 with a £30,000 deposit could borrow a maximum of £168,375, enough for a £198,375 home. Add a parent earning the same £37,500 to the application and the maximum becomes £270,000, enough for a £300,000 home. That is £101,625 of extra borrowing, a rise of 60%, from a person who will not own a square foot of the property.

Run the two sums against income and something useful falls out. On one income Barclays stretched to 4.49 times earnings. On two it lent 3.6 times the combined £75,000 — a lower multiple, not a higher one, because the deposit stayed at £30,000 and the purchase price rose, pushing the loan to 90% of value. The booster's income buys a bigger loan; it does not buy a better income multiple. Anyone modelling a boost on the assumption that two incomes attract the same 4.49 times would price themselves at £336,750 and be £66,750 out.

The obligations are real and worth reading before the call. Boosters are jointly and severally liable from completion, which means Barclays can pursue either party for the whole debt. Every booster is credit checked, the mortgage appears on their credit file, and each one has to take independent legal advice. Boosters are named on the mortgage and not on the deeds, so they carry the liability without the asset. Mortgage Boost also cannot be combined with Family Springboard, Right to Buy, the First Home Fund, Help to Buy Equity or the Mortgage Guarantee Scheme — it is one route or the other. Sian McIntyre, managing director of life moments at Barclays, framed the product around how much harder a first purchase has become; the mechanism is older than the framing, and the figures are the part to check.

The deposit somebody else puts up

Family Springboard is the other side of the same idea: instead of a relative's income, their savings. In the range Barclays announced on 19 June 2026 the five-year Springboard fix was 4.99% fee-free for 95% of the purchase price and 5.19% fee-free for the full 100%, cut from 5.32% and 5.54%. A 100% advance at 5.19% against a standard 95% five-year fix at 5.09% that same month is a 10 basis point premium for needing no deposit at all, which is a narrow gap by the standards of high loan-to-value lending.

The Right to Buy product, launched on 8 April 2025, is the most generous thing on the shelf and the least advertised. Barclays will lend up to 100% of the discounted price, provided that figure does not exceed 90% of the open market valuation, so the statutory discount does the work of a deposit. A tenant with a 40% discount is treated as having a 40% deposit and priced at 60% loan to value — the cheapest rung of the ladder rather than the dearest. Higher-value homes are capped more tightly, at 100% of the discounted price but no more than 80% of open market value, and the thresholds are published: houses valued at £640,000 or above and flats at £310,000 or above. Above those lines a deposit is still needed. Lee Chiswell, head of mortgages, described the point as removing the deposit barrier entirely, and on the published terms that is what it does for a tenant under the value caps.

Barclays is also lending at the top of the range far more than it was. Loans above 90% loan to value were 4.4% of new UK home lending in the first half of 2026, against 1.6% in the same period of 2025 — roughly £780m against £250m. Balances above 90% are now 2.1% of the portfolio, up from 1.7% at December 2025.

Retained profit started counting on 15 October 2026

The newest change is the one that will matter most to a reader who runs a company, and it arrived the day before this check. Barclays now includes profit after tax in its affordability assessment for limited company directors, counted in proportion to the applicants' combined shareholding — 60% ownership means up to 60% of the profit after tax can be considered. The applicant must own more than 50% of the business, individually or jointly, and qualifying gross annual income including the profit being counted must be at least £75,000 for a sole application or £100,000 for a joint one. The change covers residential and buy-to-let lending, and Barclays says eligibility is no longer dependent on property value, which is the restriction that used to confine this kind of assessment to larger purchases.

Two details decide whether it helps. Where profit after tax and dividends are both declared from the same business, only the profit after tax counts, so a director already drawing most of the profit as dividends gains nothing — the measure is aimed at owners who leave money in the company. And the declared figure has to be validated by the applicant's accountant by reference, which Barclays says is unchanged from its existing policy. Roland McCormack, head of intermediaries, put the reasoning plainly: "Many limited company directors retain profits to support future growth and investment." There are more than 5 million limited companies in the UK on Companies House's own count, which is the size of the pool this reaches into.

Alongside it Barclays published its own service times for intermediaries: live chat answered in under 30 seconds on average, a new case reviewed in an average of one day, a valuation completed in under three days, and residential purchase offers averaging eight days. Those are the bank's own measurements rather than an audited statistic, and they are the kind of claim worth repeating back to a broker who tells you a case is taking a fortnight.

What Premier status is worth, measured on one day

Barclays runs a Premier range alongside the core one, and the gap between them is measurable because both move in the same announcement. On 7 September 2026 the Premier five-year fix at 90% loan to value rose from 4.65% to 4.83% while the standard five-year at the same loan to value rose from 4.85% to 5.03%. That is a flat 20 basis point discount for Premier customers on identical terms. On a £250,000 loan over 25 years, 20 basis points is about £29 a month, or close to £8,700 across the full term if the gap held.

Premier pricing shows up elsewhere in the range too. The two-year tracker Barclays introduced on 19 June 2026 at 3.99% with a £999 fee up to 60% loan to value was a Premier product; in the 18 June round the Premier two-year fix at 75% loan to value was 4.47% against 4.50% for the standard product with an £899 fee; and in the 29 May round the Premier five-year at 90% went to 4.75% against 4.95% standard, the same 20 point gap four months earlier. Existing borrowers have a third range again, the Existing Mortgage Customer Reward: on 6 August its two-year fix at 60% loan to value was 4.66% with a £999 fee, and its ten-year fix at 75% was 5.12% with a £749 fee — the lowest product fee in any Barclays range this year.

Barclays does not publish the Premier qualifying test on any page this review could read, so the income or balance needed to reach that 20 basis points could not be confirmed and is worth asking about before an application is built around it.

£177.8bn falls due this half, and Barclays has built a product to chase it

Barclays publishes an analysis of the CACI Mortgage Market Database, and the June 2026 edition, using data to March 2026, found £177.8bn of UK mortgages maturing in the second half of 2026 — £154.5bn residential, up 5.1% on 2025, and £23.3bn buy-to-let. With the half now two-thirds gone, the heaviest part is still ahead: December alone carries £38bn of residential maturities against £16.2bn in November. By region the residential total splits £33.6bn in London, £31.1bn in the South East, £18.5bn in the East of England, £9bn in Scotland and £2bn in Northern Ireland. It is a whole-market figure, not Barclays' own book.

What happens to maturing loans is the single most revealing statistic in this market. UK Finance's half-year figures for 2026 show £143bn of internal product transfers against £41bn of external remortgaging — three and a half times as much money staying put as moving. Its December 2025 forecast put the full year at £261bn of product transfers against £77bn of remortgaging, and 2027 at £272bn against £80bn. Total gross lending was £145bn in the first half against a £300bn full-year forecast, of which £82bn was house purchase and £5bn buy-to-let. Barclays' own £17.7bn is about 12% of that half-year total.

Fast-Track Remortgage is Barclays attacking the easier half of that split from the outside. Victoria Thompson, head of strategic partnerships, described it as designed to prove a real alternative to product transfers, and the service is pointed squarely at customers approaching a product end date whose cases are straightforward: an offer in 24 hours, completion in as little as five days. A lender competing to take other lenders' maturing loans is the useful kind of competition, and the 24-hour offer is the first thing in this market to make an external remortgage sound as easy as clicking the switcher screen. Eligibility is subject to individual circumstances and criteria that Barclays has not published, which is the part to ask about.

A product transfer is also the one route where nobody has to look at the wider market. Under MCOB 4.8A.10R a lender can vary a rate on an execution-only basis provided there is no additional borrowing beyond fees and it shows the customer every product they are eligible for. MCOB 4.8A.14R then requires the firm to say plainly that it is not assessing suitability and that the customer loses the protections an advised sale carries. Those rules are a floor, not a recommendation: a borrower clicking through a switcher screen has been told, in terms, that nobody checked whether the deal suits them. Execution-only is barred outright for a statutory Right to Buy, for debt consolidation and for shared equity agreements under MCOB 4.8A.7R, and high net worth customers are exempted from parts of it under MCOB 4.8A.9R.

For scale, the Office for National Statistics and HM Land Registry put the average UK house price at £273,000 in July 2026, an annual rise of 1.4% and down from 2.0% in the year to June: £293,000 in England, up 1.1%, and £215,000 in Wales, up 2.6%. Annual growth has halved since May, when it stood at 3.0%. A 10% deposit on the English average is £29,300, which is within £700 of the £30,000 in Barclays' own Mortgage Boost illustration.

Who is actually lending, and what the complaints record shows

The lender is Barclays Bank UK PLC, firm reference 759676, company number 09740322, incorporated on 19 August 2015 and registered at 1 Churchill Place, London E14 5HP, with accounts to 31 December 2025 and SIC code 64191. That entity is the ring-fenced UK bank created out of the old Barclays Bank PLC, and it is the counterparty on a residential mortgage. The distinction matters when you go looking for figures: Barclays Bank PLC is a separate entity with its own regulatory returns, and the two are easy to mix up. The FCA's register is a JavaScript application that does not return firm data to an automated request, so the firm reference above was taken from a published FCA final notice rather than from the register; it is worth two minutes to check it yourself.

That final notice, dated 14 July 2025, fined Barclays Bank UK PLC £3,093,600 for breaches of Principle 3 and SYSC 6.1.1R over account opening checks for WealthTek. It concerns client money controls in another part of the bank, not mortgage lending, and it should not be read as evidence about either.

The complaints picture has improved since this page was first written, because the figure Barclays will not serve is published by its regulator instead. The FCA's firm-level complaints return for the second half of 2025 shows Barclays Bank UK PLC opening 85,996 complaints in the six months to 31 December 2025, of which 80,470 sat in banking and credit cards and 5,356 in home finance. Set that 5,356 against the 75,658 home finance complaints the FCA counted across the whole market in the same period and Barclays accounts for about 7% of them, while writing about 12% of the half-year's gross lending — a comparison in the bank's favour, and one nobody else draws. The same return gives 6.01 on the FCA's Context A measure for home finance; the regulator describes that only as complaints seen against the number of accounts or policies provided, without stating the denominator on the page that publishes it, so it is reported here as the FCA reports it. Note too that the firm-level set covers only firms reporting 500 or more complaints in six months.

On independent complaints outcomes, the numbers are thinner than they look. The Financial Ombudsman Service received 53,600 new complaints in the quarter to 30 June 2026, upholding 26% of them. First charge residential mortgages accounted for 1,291 of those with 26% upheld, buy-to-let 153 at 21% and lifetime mortgages 71 at 13%. That remains the Ombudsman's latest quarterly publication, dated 22 July 2026, when re-checked on 16 October. Mortgages are a quiet product at the Ombudsman compared with credit and motor finance, and that is worth saying plainly. The FCA's own aggregate data for the second half of 2025, first published on 28 April 2026 and revised on 15 May, recorded 1.74m complaints across financial services, with home finance falling 3.8% from 78,616 to 75,658; no newer aggregate had been published by 16 October 2026.

What is still missing is Barclays' own version. The bank publishes a six-monthly UK complaints return by product group, and the January to June 2026 page refuses automated access — a third attempt on 16 October 2026 was refused like the two before it. So the uphold rate, the three-day closure rate and the eight-week closure rate for its home finance line are not on this page. Those are the figures that would place Barclays against its peers on outcomes rather than volume, and they need confirming directly.

Three live documents, three different base rates

A review is only as good as the paperwork behind it, and here the paperwork is the story. Barclays' intermediary rate sheets and its criteria and packaging guide still return a 403 to an automated request, and so does every page on its corporate domain. The consumer pages now open, but the rate table on the fixed-rate mortgage page is a JavaScript component that serves "Loading mortgage data. Please wait..." to anything that is not a browser, so Barclays' current rates cannot be read from Barclays. Every rate quoted above therefore comes from the bank's own dated repricing announcements as reprinted by the mortgage trade press, not from a rate table and not from a comparison site, and the scale figures come from its SEC filing.

Two documents that do open are more revealing than the ones that do not. The Existing Mortgage Customer Reward customer rate sheet at Barclays' own mortgages document path is headed "Rates effective from 21 January 2022" and states the follow-on rate in terms: "3.74% variable BEBR (currently 0.25%) +3.49% for the term" on residential and "4.74% variable BEBR (currently 0.25%) +4.49% for the term" on buy-to-let. The two halves reconcile to the decimal, so the reading is sound: Barclays' reversion rate is base rate plus a stated margin for the whole remaining term, not an open-ended standard variable rate the bank can move at will. That is genuinely better than the alternative, and it is the single most useful thing on the page for anyone pricing the years after the fix. On the 3.75% base rate held since 17 September 2026 those margins would give 7.24% and 8.24% — this page's own arithmetic on a document nearly five years old, not a rate Barclays currently quotes. The same sheet shows the early repayment charge shape too: 2%, 3% and 5% of the balance repaid, falling away at dated milestones, with trackers at either no charge or 1% for two years.

The second is the Barclays Tariff of Mortgage Charges, which carries item reference 9914226_UK 09/2021 and print dates of 25 and 26 August 2021. It is more useful than its age suggests. The product fee is "Typically £999"; switching product between offer and completion costs £0; a funds transfer fee is £35 and a re-inspection £90, payable at each stage of a staged-release renovation mortgage; buy-to-let valuation fees run by value band from £175 to £1,945 with the top band "By negotiation"; and Barclays states that it does not charge for unpaid or returned Direct Debits or cheques, which several lenders do. The early repayment charge is the one thing it will not quantify: "The fee will typically be a percentage of the loan amount", with "Details available on request by calling 0800 022 4022". Note the mismatch in wording between the two documents — a percentage of the loan amount in the tariff, a percentage of the balance repaid on the rate sheet. On a part repayment those are very different sums, and it is a fair question to put in writing.

The third document is where the dates stop being an interesting footnote. Barclays' own page for mortgage customers, headed "Base rate change and mortgages", explains what a base rate move means for a fixed or tracker deal and then tells the reader: "It's the rate the Bank of England charges other banks and other lenders when they borrow money, and it's currently 5.25%." Bank Rate is 3.75%, held on 17 September 2026 and unchanged since. The page carries no last-updated date. So a Barclays mortgage customer looking up the one number that drives their tracker, or the follow-on rate waiting at the end of their fix, is handed a figure 1.5 percentage points too high on the bank's own website. The same page does carry one real term worth knowing: on a tracker, "The minimum interest rate that we'll charge is your current tracker rate", so if base rate fell below 0% the rate would floor rather than follow it down.

Collected up, three live Barclays mortgage documents quote three different base rates: 0.25% on the rate sheet, 5.25% on the explainer, and the 3.75% that is actually in force. None of that makes a figure on this page wrong, and none of it is evidence that Barclays is mispricing anything. It is evidence about how the paperwork is maintained, and a borrower who cannot get a reversion rate or an early repayment charge in writing should read the maintenance record as part of the product.

On independent ratings, the position has moved. Smart Money People scores the Barclays fixed rate mortgage 3.56 out of 5 from 95 reviews and the tracker 3.98 from 15, with the brand last reviewed within seven days of this check. Small samples, and a score to treat as a signal rather than a verdict — but it exists, and it is notably the fourth-lowest of the 22 Barclays products the site rates, against 4.31 for the current account and 4.51 for the personal loan. Defaqto does not rate mortgage ranges at all, and Fairer Finance's mortgages customer-experience table still returned "Sorry, there are no ratings to show here just yet" on 16 October 2026, the third such check since 22 September. Say which kind of absence each one is.

The credit signals in Barclays' own half-year numbers are mixed and belong here rather than higher up. Average loan to value on new lending held at 70%, but the whole portfolio rose from 54% to 57%, lending above 90% loan to value nearly tripled as a share of new business, and the credit impairment charge rose from £237m to £338m year on year. The bank also reported 14.1 million digitally active customers at 30 June 2026, against 13.7 million a year earlier, excluding Tesco Bank. None of that is alarming for a book of this size; all of it is the shape of a lender reaching further up the loan-to-value ladder, and a borrower at 95% should know they are part of that shift. Barclays' half-year results to 30 June 2026 remain its latest published figures as at 16 October 2026.

Common questions

What is the cheapest Barclays mortgage rate right now?

The most recent figure that can be verified from Barclays' own announcement is 5.03% on the five-year fix, effective 29 September 2026, replacing a 4.93% deal; the two-year equivalent went to 5.05%. Barclays then repriced 62 further products upward on 2 October 2026 without publishing the individual rates, and no later announcement could be found when this page was re-checked on 16 October 2026. Its own rate table is a browser-only component and its rate sheets refuse access, so treat 5.03% as a floor rather than today's number and ask for the current sheet.

What does a Barclays fixed rate revert to when it ends?

The last Barclays rate sheet that can be read, effective 21 January 2022, puts the residential follow-on at the Bank of England base rate plus 3.49% for the remaining term, and buy-to-let at base plus 4.49%. That is a tracking margin rather than a standard variable rate the lender can move at will, which is the better of the two structures. On the 3.75% base rate in force since 17 September 2026 those margins would work out at 7.24% and 8.24%, but the document is nearly five years old and the margin may have changed, so get the current figure in writing.

How much more can Mortgage Boost let me borrow?

On Barclays' own illustration, a buyer earning £37,500 with a £30,000 deposit could borrow £168,375. Adding one person earning another £37,500 took that to £270,000 — £101,625 more, a rise of 60%. Note that the multiple falls rather than rises: 4.49 times one income becomes 3.6 times the combined £75,000, so anyone assuming 4.49 times would price themselves £66,750 too high. The booster is named on the mortgage, not on the title deeds, is jointly and severally liable for the whole balance, is credit checked, has the mortgage recorded on their credit file and must take independent legal advice.

Can a limited company director use retained profit on a Barclays application?

Since 15 October 2026, yes. Barclays counts profit after tax in proportion to the applicants' combined shareholding, so 60% ownership allows up to 60% of the profit to be considered. You must own more than half the business, and qualifying gross annual income including that profit must be at least £75,000 on a sole application or £100,000 on a joint one. It applies to residential and buy-to-let lending, and eligibility is no longer tied to property value. If you already declare dividends from the same company, only the profit after tax is counted, so the change helps owners who leave money in the business rather than those who draw it out. Your accountant has to validate the figure.

Can I get a Barclays mortgage with no deposit?

In two situations. Family Springboard was priced at 5.19% fee-free for 100% of the purchase price in the June 2026 range, with a helper's savings standing behind it. And since 8 April 2025 a Right to Buy tenant can borrow 100% of the discounted price, capped at 90% of open market value, with the statutory discount doing the work of a deposit — though homes valued at £640,000 or more, or flats at £310,000 or more, are capped at 80% of open market value and still need a deposit.

Is it worth switching to a new Barclays deal or remortgaging elsewhere?

UK Finance recorded £143bn of internal product transfers against £41bn of external remortgaging in the first half of 2026, so most borrowers stay. An internal switch is quick and, under MCOB 4.8A.10R, can be done execution-only with no additional borrowing — but MCOB 4.8A.14R means the lender has told you it is not assessing whether the deal suits you. Barclays now sells against its own switcher screen with Fast-Track Remortgage, which promises an offer in 24 hours and completion in as little as five days. With £177.8bn of UK mortgages maturing in the second half of 2026 and £38bn of it in December alone, it is worth at least pricing the external market first.

Who regulates Barclays mortgages and where do I complain?

The lender is Barclays Bank UK PLC, firm reference 759676, authorised and regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority. Complain to Barclays first; if you are not satisfied, or eight weeks pass, you can take it to the Financial Ombudsman Service, which upheld 26% of the 1,291 first charge residential mortgage complaints it received in the quarter to 30 June 2026. For scale, the FCA's own firm-level return shows Barclays Bank UK PLC opening 5,356 home finance complaints in the six months to 31 December 2025.

Our verdict

Barclays is at its most useful when a borrower needs somebody else's income, somebody else's deposit or their own company's retained profit. The Mortgage Boost figures are published rather than hinted at, the Right to Buy product prices a statutory discount as a deposit, the profit after tax change of 15 October 2026 reaches owners who leave money in the business, and Fast-Track Remortgage is a serious attempt to beat the product transfer at its own speed. The last follow-on rate Barclays published was base rate plus a stated margin for the term, which is better than a standard variable rate, and its tariff of charges is cheap and specific. Against that, the paperwork is badly maintained: the readable rate sheet is from January 2022, the tariff from August 2021, and the bank's own explainer still tells mortgage customers the base rate is 5.25% when it is 3.75%. The rate position is unstable, with two increases in the week to 2 October 2026 and nothing published since. Go to Barclays for what it will lend and on whose income; get the current follow-on rate and the early repayment charge schedule in writing before you sign anything, because the published versions are years old.
