British Gas Plumbing and Drains Cover

Rates and terms checked 16 October 2026 · Plumbing Insurance · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

British Gas sells plumbing and drains cover at £6.12 a month — £73.44 across a year — with a £60 excess, unlimited call-outs and no cap on what the repairs themselves cost. On top of that sits £1,000 per repair, including VAT, to get at the pipe and put the floor, the wall or the driveway back afterwards. Every figure here was read on 16 October 2026 from British Gas's own product page, summary of cover and insurance documents.

Three separate companies stand behind that price and all three sit inside one group, which is unusual in this corner of the market. British Gas Services Limited (company 03141243, firm reference 490568) sells the policy and administers it. British Gas Insurance Limited (company 06608316, firm reference 490565) carries the risk and is authorised by the Prudential Regulation Authority in its own right. And the engineer who arrives for a stubborn blockage works for Dyno-Rod Limited (company 01046906, incorporated 21 March 1972), which the Companies House register records as 75% or more owned and controlled by British Gas Services Limited — notified on 6 April 2016, with the same registered office in Windsor. Centrica's 2025 strategic report counts 7,000-plus field service engineers across the operation and 2,939,000 Home Services customers at the end of the year.

Three rules decide whether this policy is open to you before price enters the argument. You must own the home you are insuring, the cover is for domestic use on the mainland United Kingdom only, and claims in the first 14 days are not covered at all. British Gas prints that waiting period on the page where it takes your money, which is more than several of its rivals manage — but it still means a leak in the first fortnight is your bill.

£73.44 a year, one £60 excess, and nothing capped above it

The advertised price is £6.12 a month, which is £73.44 over twelve months. What that buys is repairs to the plumbing system in your home and outbuildings, the water supply pipe within the boundary of your property, and the unblocking and repair of drains and waste pipes to restore flow. Accidental damage is in. Parts and labour are in, for emergencies and non-emergencies alike. Where a part cannot be repaired it is replaced.

Two things about the money are worth reading twice. First, there is no cap on repairs and no cap on the number of claims — British Gas's own words are "unlimited call-outs – and no cap on claims either". That is a genuinely better position than the per-claim ceilings of £250 to £5,000 that this product line usually runs on. Second, the £1,000 including VAT for access and making good is a separate allowance applied to each repair, and it is the part that pays for lifting a floor or digging up a path.

The £60 excess falls due once per completed repair, not once a year, and British Gas's help pages are clear about the timing: the card is checked when the appointment is booked and the money is taken once the repair is finished. Every repair carries a 12-month guarantee, so a second visit for the same fault inside that year does not trigger a second excess.

Where this gets harder to compare is the excess ladder. British Gas states that "the higher the amount of your excess, the lower the yearly cost of your product", and the £6.12 is quoted as a "from" price at £60. The other excess levels, and the prices against them, are not published anywhere on the site — so the only figure a shopper can hold up against another provider is the one at a single excess setting. For scale: the same page prices HomeCare Classic, which adds the boiler, central heating, radiators, hot water cylinder and an annual boiler service, at £27.63 a month. The boiler half of that bundle therefore costs £21.51 a month more than the plumbing half on its own.

On speed, British Gas publishes a figure and dates it: it attends 99% of emergencies the same day when booked before 4pm, footnoted to twelve months of data to March 2025, with emergencies defined as severe leaks, no fresh water, no useable toilet and overflowing sewage. A dated claim with a stated definition behind it is worth more than a promise, and most of this market offers the promise.

Who sells it, who insures it, and who rings the doorbell

In most home emergency policies these are three different businesses with three different incentives, and working out which one owes you anything is a job for the certificate. Here they are one group, and the register proves it rather than the marketing claiming it.

British Gas Services Limited is the seller. Its "About our insurance services" document — reference BR230 06/21 — describes it as "an insurance intermediary", states "we only offer Financial Services products from British Gas Insurance Limited", and says plainly that "we represent you and not the insurer for the sale of insurance products". It is paid by "commission from the insurer, which is made up of a percentage of the premium". British Gas Insurance Limited is the one that carries the risk, and it is not a badge: it holds its own Prudential Regulation Authority authorisation under firm reference 490565 and files its own regulatory accounts.

Then there is the doorstep. British Gas's own guide says the plumbing and drains cover comes "from Dyno-Rod, part of British Gas", and that "for stubborn drain blocks, our expert engineers from Dyno-Rod will get things flowing in no time". The register backs the sentence up. Dyno-Rod Limited is company 01046906, incorporated on 21 March 1972 and known as Dyno-Rod Public Limited Company until 27 January 2005. Its single person with significant control is British Gas Services Limited, holding 75% or more of shares and voting rights with the right to appoint and remove directors, notified on 6 April 2016. Both companies are registered at Millstream, Maidenhead Road, Windsor SL4 5GD.

Scale is the practical consequence. Centrica's 2025 strategic report puts 7,000-plus field service engineers behind the business; HomeServe's own group accounts for the year to 31 March 2023 put its directly employed engineer count at about 700. Neither figure is broken down by trade, and a drain in a village at 9pm on a Sunday is still a drain in a village — but on the published numbers this is the deepest directly employed network in the category by an order of magnitude.

The same single-group structure has an awkward edge, and it is worth knowing before you buy rather than before you click. British Gas Insurance Limited's own report states that "some material services are outsourced by BGIL to BGSL, including claims handling & fulfilment and policy administration". The company that decides your claim is the company that sold you the policy. Nothing about that arrangement is improper and it is disclosed in a regulatory document; it is simply not the separation of seller and decider that a reader might assume. Both firms are covered by the Financial Services Compensation Scheme, where general insurance is protected for 90% of a claim with no upper limit.

£800.8m in, £351.2m out: the insurer publishes its own accounts

This is the one thing no other provider in the category can offer, and it exists only because British Gas insures its own customers through a regulated insurer. Solvency II obliges that insurer to publish a Solvency and Financial Condition Report, and British Gas Insurance Limited's — covering the year ended 31 December 2024 and signed on 27 March 2025 — is free on Centrica's own site.

It says the company "underwrites general insurance risks in England, Scotland, and Wales" covering "the breakdown of domestic boilers and central heating systems" along with "plumbing & drains, and electrical and gas appliances". Then it gives the numbers:

  • Gross written premium £800.8m, against £793.0m in 2023
  • Claims incurred £351.2m, against £368.2m in 2023
  • Combined operating ratio 91.8%, against 94.5% in 2023
  • 5.5 million policies in force at 31 December 2024, down from 5.7 million
  • Solvency Capital Requirement £66.6m against eligible tier 1 own funds of £95.4m — a coverage ratio of 143%, from 144%
  • "No reinsurance contracts were entered into during the current or prior years"

Two sums follow, and they are ours rather than the insurer's. Claims incurred came to about 44p of every £1 of premium in 2024, against roughly 46p in 2023. And £800.8m spread across 5.5 million policies is an average of about £146 a year per policy across the whole book — boiler plans included, which is why the plumbing-only £73.44 sits well under it. Label both as arithmetic on published figures, not as numbers British Gas states.

The honest reading is mixed and it is better than guessing. A book that falls by 200,000 policies, about 3.5%, while premium income rises is a book being priced upward rather than grown. A 91.8% combined ratio and a 143% solvency margin describe an insurer that is comfortably capitalised and making money on underwriting. Carrying no reinsurance at all is a deliberate choice that works because the claims are many and small rather than few and catastrophic. No report for the year to 31 December 2025 could be found on 16 October 2026, so these remain the most recent audited figures published.

From the boundary in, the pipe belongs to you

The part of this policy that is hardest to get elsewhere is the water supply pipe, and the reason is a division of responsibility most homeowners discover at the worst possible moment. Ofwat sets it out plainly: water companies "are responsible for the maintenance of communication pipes and mains", those communication pipes run only as far as the property boundary, and a company stop-tap at that point "will normally mark the end of pipework that is the responsibility of the company". Past it, the supply pipe — and, in Ofwat's words, "the drains, water pipes and plumbing fixtures on your property" — are the owner's.

It is not a soft obligation. Where a water company traces a leak it cannot attribute to its own pipes, it can serve a notice under the Water Industry Act 1991, and if the work is not done in the stated time "the company can carry out the works and pass on the costs to you". Metered customers whose bill spiked because of a leak since repaired may be able to claim an allowance for the water lost, and some companies will repair a customer's pipe free of charge once — both worth a phone call before a claim.

British Gas's cover is drawn to that boundary and no further: the supply pipe "within the boundary of your property", with water supply pipes that do not supply your home excluded outright, and shared drains excluded as well. That second exclusion matters in a terrace, a maisonette or anything with a joint run, because the drain that backs up is very often the shared one — and a shared drain is usually the water company's responsibility rather than yours, which is the better news buried inside a worse-sounding clause.

Fixing the pipe is not the same as paying for the flood

British Gas states the boundary itself, on a comparison guide updated on 22 January 2026, and the sentence deserves credit because most of this market leaves it to the small print: HomeCare "can fix the pipe, insurance pays for the water damage caused". Its own worked example is blunter — "a pipe bursts and floods your kitchen. Insurance pays for the water damage repairs."

So the £1,000 access-and-make-good allowance is not damage cover and should not be read as any. It pays to reach the fault and to restore what had to be cut, lifted or dug out to reach it. The ruined kitchen floor, the soaked ceiling below and the contents are a buildings and contents claim, under a different policy, with a different excess. Read the two together and the division is sensible: this policy buys you an engineer and a repair without an argument about cost; your buildings insurer buys you the reinstatement.

It is also where the disputes are. The Financial Ombudsman Service's annual data for 2025/26, published on 21 May 2026, records 6,399 new buildings insurance complaints at a 38% uphold rate, against 214,600 complaints across all financial products and a 30% average. Knowing in advance which of your two policies owns which half of a flood is the cheapest thing you can do about that.

The pre-sale document that names no insurer

The Insurance Product Information Document is the standardised summary a customer is handed before buying, and the one British Gas serves for Plumbing and Drains Cover is good on cover and silent on three things it should not be silent about.

It names no insurer at all. The only company it identifies is British Gas Services Limited, "authorised and regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority", registered number 03141243 — which is the intermediary, not the underwriter. It carries no document code and no date, so there is no way to tell which version you are reading. It does not mention the 14-day waiting period that the sales page discloses. And it gives no excess amounts, saying only that an excess is payable "if you choose to pay" one.

What does name the insurer is the Summary of Cover, which states that British Gas Insurance Limited underwrites the product and British Gas Services Limited is the administrator. Its footer reads 711763-20 - 02/17 — February 2017, nine and a half years old. A document from 2017 being more informative than the current regulated one is a reasonable thing to raise; the "About our insurance services" leaflet behind it is dated 06/21, which is June 2021.

Two further gaps are worth naming. The full terms refer the reader to a cancellation charges table on page 34, and the terms PDF refused to open to an automated reader on 16 October 2026 under the site's own robots file, so those charges could not be read or quoted here and should be asked for before signing. And the offer terms still displayed alongside the prices carry end dates of 8 September 2026 for the HomeCare Classic promotion and 15 October 2026 for the HomeCare one — the day before this check. Neither is an argument about the £6.12, which is the standing price; both suggest the page furniture is not being swept as often as the prices are.

1,631 complaints across the product line, and 40% of them upheld

Home emergency cover finally has its own named line in the ombudsman's figures, and it is not a flattering one for anybody selling it. The Financial Ombudsman Service's 2025/26 annual data, published 21 May 2026, records 1,631 new home emergency insurance complaints at a 40% uphold rate. Set that against 214,600 complaints across every financial product and a 30% average, and this is a small product line that argues above its weight — as you would expect of a contract whose disputes are mostly about whether the sales promise was kept.

For the firm itself, the FCA's own firm-level table is the primary source and it is readable. For the half year 1 July to 31 December 2025, first published 28 April 2026 and revised 15 May 2026, British Gas Services Limited reported 49,016 insurance and pure protection complaints opened. Two cautions on that number: it is reported jointly across the group's entities, and the FCA's table gives volumes rather than outcomes, so there is no uphold rate for British Gas specifically in it. Only firms reporting 500 or more complaints in six months appear at all, which is why most of this market is absent from the table entirely.

Centrica's own preliminary results of 19 February 2026 put some direction on it. Home Services complaints per UK customer fell to 4.8% from 5.3%, engineer net promoter score rose to 76 from 73, protection contract retention held at 87% against 86%, and on-demand jobs grew 28% while the traditional protection portfolio shrank 3%. A rising engineer score alongside a falling book is the shape of a business keeping the customers it has rather than winning new ones.

Independent ratings are thin. Smart Money People rates British Gas Boiler Repairs at 3.71 out of 5 from 42 reviews and its home insurance at 4.00 out of 5 from 34, both small samples, and lists no plumbing or drains product at all. Defaqto runs a home emergency category but its public page carries no British Gas entry. So there is no independent score for this specific product from any source worth quoting — which is true of almost every policy in the category, and is a fact about the category rather than about British Gas.

Common questions

How much does British Gas plumbing and drains cover cost?

£6.12 a month at the £60 excess, which is £73.44 over twelve months. That is a "from" price: British Gas says a higher excess lowers the annual cost, but it does not publish the other excess levels or their prices, so £6.12 at £60 is the only comparable figure available. HomeCare Classic, which adds the boiler, central heating, hot water cylinder and an annual service, is £27.63 a month.

Who actually turns up when a drain blocks?

British Gas says the drains cover comes "from Dyno-Rod, part of British Gas", and Companies House confirms it: Dyno-Rod Limited (company 01046906) has British Gas Services Limited as its only person with significant control, holding 75% or more of shares and voting rights since 6 April 2016, registered at the same Windsor address. Centrica reports 7,000-plus field service engineers across the business.

Does the policy pay for water damage caused by a leak?

No, and British Gas says so itself: HomeCare "can fix the pipe, insurance pays for the water damage caused". The £1,000 per repair is for gaining access and making good — the floor that had to come up, the wall that had to be opened — not for a ruined kitchen or soaked contents. That is a buildings and contents claim on a separate policy with its own excess.

Is the water supply pipe to my house really my responsibility?

Past the boundary, yes. Ofwat states that water companies maintain the mains and communication pipes up to the property boundary, and that the supply pipe beyond it, along with "the drains, water pipes and plumbing fixtures on your property", belong to the owner. A company can serve a notice under the Water Industry Act 1991 and, if the repair is not done in time, do the work and bill you. Some companies will fix a customer's pipe free once, and metered customers may be able to claim a leak allowance, so ask before you claim.

Who insures the policy, and is my money protected?

British Gas Insurance Limited (company 06608316, firm reference 490565) underwrites it and is authorised by the Prudential Regulation Authority. British Gas Services Limited (03141243, firm reference 490568) sells and administers it as an insurance intermediary paid by commission from the insurer. Both are covered by the Financial Services Compensation Scheme, where general insurance is protected for 90% of a claim with no upper limit. Note that the Insurance Product Information Document names neither company as the insurer — only the nine-year-old Summary of Cover does.

What is not covered?

Claims in the first 14 days; showers and their parts, shower pumps, sanitary ware and sealant; shared drains; pre-existing and design faults; faults caused by someone else you used for a repair; system improvements and upgrades; purely cosmetic repairs; pipes running between your home and outbuildings; water supply pipes that do not supply your home; and excavation directly under the property where there is a risk to the foundations. Cover is for homeowners, domestic use and the mainland United Kingdom only.

Our verdict

At £6.12 a month with a £60 excess, British Gas plumbing and drains cover is not the cheapest line in this market, and it is the only one where the company that sold you the policy, the company that insures it and the company whose van pulls up are all demonstrably the same group — proved on the Companies House register rather than asserted in a brochure. No cap on claims, no cap on repair costs and £1,000 per repair to get at the pipe put it ahead of the per-claim ceilings most of its rivals sell, and the insurer behind it publishes £800.8m of premium, £351.2m of claims and a 143% solvency margin for anyone who wants to check it is solvent. The weaknesses are documentary rather than financial: an IPID that names no underwriter, one published excess level out of several, a cancellation charges table nobody outside can read, and a bathroom exclusion list that will disappoint anyone with a pumped shower. If you own your home, want the supply pipe covered and value a repair being someone's job rather than someone's claim, it earns its £73.44 a year — provided you read the exclusions before the fortnight is up.
