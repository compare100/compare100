Brittany Ferries Ferry Tickets: Routes, Fares and the Rules Behind Them

Rates and terms checked 7 September 2026 · Ferry Tickets · Compare100 editorial team

Brittany Ferries runs the long Channel crossings — the overnight ones, the ones with a cabin and a restaurant, the ones that put you in Normandy or Brittany or northern Spain instead of at the wrong end of France with six hours of motorway still to drive. It was founded in 1972 by the Breton farmer Alexis Gourvennec and a group of his neighbours who wanted to get cauliflowers and artichokes to British supermarkets. Their first ship was a freighter named Kerisnel, after the village the cauliflowers came from, and the company describes itself as "officially born on a bleak New Year's Day in 1973". The unusual part is that the arrangement held: the operator's own history page states that "the Breton farming groups who founded the company remain its main shareholders to this day". Fifty-three years on it is a farmers' co-operative that happens to own a fleet of cruise ferries.

The scale is real. At its last full published results the company was running 12 ships on 14 maritime routes through 12 ports — Roscoff, Saint-Malo, Cherbourg, Caen, Le Havre, Plymouth, Portsmouth, Poole, Cork, Rosslare, Santander and Bilbao — with around 2,500 employees including 1,600 seafarers on a full-time equivalent basis. Five new vessels joined the fleet between 2019 and 2025, four of them LNG-powered. Fares are not the budget-airline sort, but they are lower than most people assume: on the Portsmouth to Santander route, a 24 to 32 hour crossing sailing three times a week aboard the flagship Pont-Aven, the operator's own October price guide starts at £265 for a car and passengers on the Saturday 08:45 Économie sailing, or £314 on a Cruise sailing with an inside four-berth cabin included. Portsmouth to Caen, which the company calls its most popular route, takes six to eight hours.

One thing to know before you plan a 2027 trip: the map is being redrawn. On 30 June 2026 the company announced that Portsmouth to Le Havre closes in October 2026 and Poole to Cherbourg in November 2026, with the 1992-built Barfleur and the 2007-built Cotentin both to be sold. A new daily Portsmouth to Cherbourg service replaces part of that, and from 1 November 2026 the Islander takes a triangular Portsmouth–Guernsey–Cherbourg rotation while the high-speed catamaran Voyager works Poole to Guernsey with an onward St Malo option. Bookings for that one opened on 6 August 2026 and run to 7 November 2027: the Voyager does up to 42 knots, carries 850 passengers and 280 cars, and reaches St Malo from Poole in six hours. If your usual sailing is one of the two being withdrawn, check the timetable before you book anything else.

The company on the ticket is not the company on the funnel

Two legal entities sit behind a British booking and the terms say so plainly. The carrier is BAI SA, registered at Port du Bloscon, CS 60 072, 29688 Roscoff Cedex in France. It sells here through "BAI UK Limited, a company registered in England and Wales under company number 01080495, and whose registered office is at Millbay, Plymouth, PL1 3EW", described in the booking conditions as its appointed sales agent. Companies House shows that firm as B.A.I. (U.K.), Limited, incorporated on 7 November 1972 — five weeks before the first ship sailed — active, private limited, filed under SIC codes 50100 for sea and coastal passenger water transport and 50200 for freight, with accounts last made up to 31 October 2025 and a confirmation statement dated 7 July 2026.

That split matters for one practical reason and it is a reassuring one. Although the carrier is French and the package holiday terms are issued by BAI SA in Roscoff, the ferry booking conditions state that they "and any dispute or claim in connection with your booking will be governed by the law of England". A British passenger arguing about a refund is arguing under English law with a Plymouth address to write to, which is not something every continental operator offers.

Three fare names, three quite different contracts

The ferry-only booking uses three ticket types and the differences are larger than the price gap suggests. Early Bird is "the cheapest fare, ideal if you're confident your travel plans will not change", and is only sold 60 or more days before departure. Standard is the middle option and allows amendment or cancellation "within certain conditions". Flexi carries the useful promise: "the freedom to make free amendments up until four hours before your outbound sailing". Four hours before a Portsmouth sailing is a genuinely late cut-off, and on a route where a sick child or a broken-down car is the usual reason for changing plans, it is the feature worth paying for.

Two rules cut across all three. Where the booking is made inside 60 days of the outward journey, "the full price will have to be paid at the time of booking for all ticket types" — so the deposit structure only exists for people booking well ahead. And the deposit itself, in the conditions' own words, "is always non-refundable in case of amendment and/or cancellation". Read that twice: not only cancelling but changing the booking forfeits the deposit, whatever the cancellation table says about the fee on top. There is a 24-hour cooling-off period in which you can decide you no longer want to continue, with a full refund, and a separate facility to hold a quoted price for 24 hours pending payment.

Promotional fares are a fourth category and they are the strict one. The promotional terms state flatly "This fare is non-refundable", set "an amendment fee on this promotional fare of £25" for any change plus any fare difference, require full card payment at booking, and add a trap that catches people every summer: "if the outward portion of any return itinerary is not used, we will cancel the return and no refunds will be paid". Miss the boat out and the boat back disappears with it.

What a late ship actually owes you

Ferry passengers have statutory rights that almost nobody claims, because almost nobody knows the numbers. They come from Regulation (EU) No 1177/2010 on the rights of passengers travelling by sea, which remains part of UK law after Brexit and is enforced here under the Merchant Shipping (Passengers' Rights) Regulations 2013. The trigger throughout is 90 minutes.

Article 17 says that once departure is delayed beyond that, the carrier must supply "snacks, meals or refreshments in reasonable relation to the waiting time" free of charge, and where an overnight stay becomes necessary must provide "adequate accommodation on board, or ashore" — with the cost of shore accommodation capped at €80 per night, for a maximum of three nights, transport to and from the port excluded. Article 18 gives the passenger a choice at the same 90-minute mark: re-routing "under comparable conditions, as set out in the transport contract, at the earliest opportunity and at no additional cost", or reimbursement of the ticket price, payable within seven days.

Article 19 is the money. Compensation of 25% of the ticket price becomes payable for an arrival delay of one hour on a scheduled journey of up to four hours, or two hours on a journey of more than four but not more than eight hours. Double those delays and the figure becomes 50%. On a six-hour Portsmouth to Caen sailing that means two hours late pays a quarter of the fare and four hours late pays half of it; on the 24-hour-plus Santander run the bands are longer again. The regulation does not reach the very short hops — Article 2 excludes services where "the distance of the overall passenger service is less than 500 metres, one way" or where the crew numbers no more than three — but every route on this network is comfortably inside it.

Injury, luggage and the unit nobody explains

Liability at sea runs on the 2002 Athens Convention, given effect through Regulation (EC) No 392/2009, and the operator publishes the limits on its own passengers' rights page. For death or personal injury caused by a shipping incident the carrier is liable up to 250,000 SDR without proof of fault and up to 400,000 SDR where fault or neglect is shown; for a non-shipping incident the ceiling is 400,000 SDR on proof of fault. Cabin luggage is covered to 2,250 SDR, vehicles to 12,700 SDR, and other luggage and valuables to 3,375 SDR. Mobility equipment sits outside the caps: under Article 4 the measure is replacement value or the cost of repair.

Two provisions in the same regulation are worth knowing because they work in the passenger's favour quickly. Article 6 requires an advance payment "within 15 days of the identification of the person entitled to damages" to cover immediate economic needs, with a floor in the UK version of £18,500 in the event of death — paid without any admission of liability and set off against whatever is eventually agreed. Article 7 obliges the carrier to give passengers comprehensible information about these rights at the point of sale and before departure, which is why the summary sits on the website at all rather than being buried in a claims file.

The SDR is the International Monetary Fund's Special Drawing Right, a basket unit whose sterling value moves daily. The operator's page prints the SDR figures and does not convert them, so a passenger reading it cannot tell from that page alone whether a suitcase is insured for four figures or five. As a rough guide the unit has traded well above £1 for years, which puts vehicle cover in the tens of thousands and the injury ceiling in the hundreds of thousands — but check the current rate rather than relying on that sentence.

Dogs, cabins and the €400 muzzle rule

The pet offer is the reason a large number of people choose this operator over flying at all. Animals travel in one of three ways — in your own car on the vehicle deck, in an onboard kennel, or in a pet-friendly cabin — and pet-friendly cabins are fitted on the cruise ferries including Pont-Aven, Armorique, Galicia, Normandie and Saint-Malo, in two-berth and four-berth, inside and outside, and in Commodore grade. A dog that would spend a flight in a cargo hold instead spends the night on the floor of your cabin.

The conditions attach a penalty to the one rule people forget. Non-compliance with the obligation to muzzle a dog "will incur a fine of €200 for the first offence", rising to €400 for repeated breaches. That is the operator's own charge rather than a French customs penalty, and it is easily the largest single number a pet owner is likely to meet on the crossing. Carry a muzzle even if your dog has never worn one.

Cabins are where the fare structure gets easier to compare than it looks. On the Spanish routes the cabin is bundled: the £314 October Cruise fare quoted above includes an inside four-berth with an occupancy discount, and a September sailing at £470 includes an inside two-berth. On the high-speed services the trade-off runs the other way — no cabins, and "luggage sizes in public areas on high-speed services are limited to 45cm", which is a cabin-bag rule rather than a ferry one and catches people who have packed for a car deck.

The border step that did not exist two years ago

Anyone who last took this crossing before autumn 2025 will meet something new. The European Union's Entry/Exit System started on 12 October 2025 and applies to British passport holders entering the Schengen area. Travellers are asked to give fingerprints and have a photograph taken at dedicated booths; the resulting digital record is valid for three years, so the slow version happens once and subsequent crossings are quicker.

Where it happens depends on the port, and the government's guidance — last updated on 10 April 2026 — is specific. At the Port of Dover and Eurotunnel, "any EES checks will be completed at the border, before you leave the UK", because French officers are stationed on this side. Passengers using other ferry ports complete the checks on arrival in the destination country instead. For a Portsmouth or Plymouth sailing that means the queue is at the far end, after a night at sea, rather than on the quayside at home. The system also counts your days: the total stay in the Schengen area must be no more than 90 days in every 180, which is the rule second-home owners in France and Spain have to watch.

The Irish routes are unaffected. The Republic of Ireland and Cyprus are outside the Schengen area, so EES does not apply to Cork or Rosslare sailings at all — a small but real advantage of the western network that nothing on the booking page tells you.

A ferry ticket carries far less protection than a holiday

This is the part that deserves reading before you decide how to book, because the difference between two purchases on the same website is substantial. Book a package — crossing plus hotel or gîte — and the organiser is BAI SA, which provides "full financial protection for our package holidays through a bond held with the Association of British Travel Agents" under ABTA membership Y6117, including refunds and repatriation if the company were to fail, as required by the Package Travel and Linked Travel Arrangements Regulations 2018. The package terms take a 10% deposit, want the balance at least 30 days before travel, and charge £25 for each change.

Book the ferry on its own and none of that attaches. A crossing is a single travel service, so it is not a package, so there is no insolvency protection, no bond and no repatriation promise; there is no ATOL cover either, because ATOL is the Civil Aviation Authority's flight-based scheme and no flight is involved. The ferry-only conditions say as much by omission — they state that if you have booked a ticket together with accommodation other than onboard accommodation, "these Booking Conditions will not apply", because you have bought the protected product instead.

There is no ombudsman either. Financial services have the Financial Ombudsman Service, telecoms have two Ofcom-approved adjudicators, and sea travel has neither. What it has instead is prosecution: the Merchant Shipping (Passengers' Rights) Regulations 2013 name the Maritime and Coastguard Agency as the designated enforcement body, and breaches of the assistance, re-routing and compensation articles carry "a fine not exceeding level 5 on the standard scale" on summary conviction, with the more serious categories exposed to an unlimited fine on indictment. A fine paid to the court does nothing for the passenger. Your own route is the county court, and the deadline is tight: proceedings must be instituted "before the end of the period of six months beginning when the infringement complained of occurred", extendable by three months if conciliation is started inside that window. Six months is short. Diary it the day the crossing goes wrong.

Where the small print thins out

Three gaps are worth naming. The first is the weather exemption, and on the western Channel it is not theoretical. Article 20 disapplies both the assistance duty and the compensation entitlement where "the cancellation or delay is caused by weather conditions endangering the safe operation of the ship". A February gale in the Bay of Biscay is exactly the scenario in which a passenger most wants the 50% and exactly the scenario in which it is least likely to be paid. That is the law rather than the operator's choice, but it means the compensation right is worth less on this network than the percentages suggest.

The second is the £10 charge. The conditions provide that "an extra £10/€10 service charge will apply if you choose to make the amendment through our Contact Centre, where the amendment could have been made on our website". It is a small sum and a common policy, but it lands hardest on the customers least able to use the website, and nothing in the wording carves out a passenger who cannot manage the online journey.

The third is that no independent body rates ferry operators. Defaqto rates insurance products; Ofcom measures telecoms complaints; the national tourist boards assess accommodation. None of them reaches a shipping line, and there is no published acceptance rate, uphold rate or star band for any carrier on this network. The nearest thing to independent oversight is the Maritime and Coastguard Agency's safety role and the enforcement powers above, neither of which produces a score. Anyone comparing operators is comparing timetables, fares and documents — which is a reason to read the documents rather than the marketing.

Where it wins

  • Long crossings that land you in Brittany, Normandy or northern Spain rather than at the Calais end of France
  • Still majority-owned by the Breton farming co-operatives that founded it in 1972
  • Flexi fares allow free changes up to four hours before the outbound sailing
  • Pet-friendly cabins across the cruise fleet, so a dog need not travel in a hold or a car
  • Cabin included in the headline fare on the Spanish routes, from £314 in October
  • Ferry booking conditions governed by English law with a Plymouth address to write to
  • Package holidays bonded with ABTA under membership Y6117
  • Irish routes fall outside the Schengen Entry/Exit System entirely

Where it falls short

  • The deposit is non-refundable when you amend a booking, not only when you cancel it — a date change costs money before any fee is added
  • Promotional fares cannot be refunded at all, cost £25 to change, and the return leg is cancelled outright with no refund if the outward leg goes unused
  • Two routes are being withdrawn: Portsmouth to Le Havre in October 2026 and Poole to Cherbourg in November 2026
  • Statutory delay compensation of 25% or 50% falls away where the delay is caused by weather endangering safe operation, which is the commonest cause of disruption on these routes
  • There is no ombudsman or ADR scheme for ferry travel: enforcement is by prosecution through the Maritime and Coastguard Agency, and a passenger's own claim must reach court within six months
  • A ferry-only ticket carries no ABTA bond, no insolvency protection and no ATOL cover, unlike the same company's package holidays
  • The Athens Convention limits are published in Special Drawing Rights with no sterling conversion anywhere on the page
  • Amending by telephone costs £10 more than doing the same thing online, with no exception for customers who cannot use the website
  • No ratings agency covers ferry operators, so there is no independent score for this carrier or any of its competitors

Common questions

What happens if my Brittany Ferries sailing is delayed or cancelled?

Past 90 minutes the carrier must provide refreshments and, where an overnight stay becomes necessary, accommodation costing up to €80 a night for up to three nights. You may choose re-routing at no extra cost or a refund of the fare within seven days. Arrival delays attract 25% of the ticket price at one hour on journeys up to four hours or two hours on journeys of four to eight hours, and 50% at double those figures — unless the delay was caused by weather endangering safe operation, in which case nothing is payable.

Is a Brittany Ferries ticket financially protected if the company fails?

A package holiday is: BAI SA holds an ABTA bond under membership Y6117 covering refunds and repatriation under the Package Travel Regulations 2018. A ferry-only crossing is a single travel service and therefore not a package, so it carries no bond, no insolvency protection and no ATOL cover. Paying by credit card gives separate protection on purchases above £100 under section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act 1974; confirm the position with your own card issuer.

Can I change or cancel a Brittany Ferries booking?

It depends which fare you bought. Flexi allows free amendments up to four hours before the outbound sailing. Standard permits changes and cancellation within set conditions and a fee scale by notice period. Early Bird is the cheapest and the least forgiving, and promotional fares cannot be refunded at all and cost £25 to amend. In every case the deposit is stated to be non-refundable on amendment as well as cancellation, and amending by phone rather than online adds £10.

Do I need to register for the EU Entry/Exit System before sailing?

Registration happens at the border rather than in advance. The system began on 12 October 2025 and takes fingerprints and a photograph, creating a digital record valid for three years. At Dover and Eurotunnel the checks are done before you leave the UK; from other ferry ports, including Portsmouth and Plymouth, they are done on arrival in the destination country. Sailings to Cork and Rosslare are exempt because Ireland is outside the Schengen area.

How much can I claim if my luggage or car is damaged on the crossing?

The 2002 Athens Convention limits apply: 2,250 SDR for cabin luggage, 12,700 SDR for a vehicle and 3,375 SDR for other luggage and valuables, with personal injury covered to 250,000 SDR without proof of fault and 400,000 SDR where fault is shown. Mobility equipment is compensated at replacement or repair cost rather than under a cap. SDR is an IMF unit, so check the current sterling rate before assuming a figure.

Can my dog travel in the cabin with me?

Yes on the cruise ferries, which carry pet-friendly cabins in two-berth and four-berth, inside and outside, and in Commodore grade on ships including Pont-Aven, Armorique, Galicia, Normandie and Saint-Malo. Kennels and the option of leaving the animal in your vehicle are the alternatives. Bring a muzzle: the booking conditions impose a fine of €200 for a first breach of the muzzling obligation and €400 for repeat breaches.

Our verdict

For a car, a dog and a fortnight in France or Spain, this is the operator whose product genuinely differs from the short-hop alternatives: a cabin, a restaurant, a night at sea and a landfall that saves several hundred miles of driving. Fares from £265 with a car are competitive once the fuel and the overnight stop you no longer need are counted. Buy the Flexi fare if there is any chance your plans move, because the four-hour amendment window is the best term on offer and the deposit on the cheaper fares does not come back even when you are only changing the date. Book the crossing and the accommodation together if you want ABTA protection, and check the 2027 timetable before assuming your usual sailing still exists.
