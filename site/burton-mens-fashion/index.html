Burton Menswear Review: What a Return Costs, and How the Sizing Works

Rates and terms checked 16 October 2026 · Men's Fashion · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Sending a Burton parcel back costs £2.49 through InPost or ASDA and £2.99 through Evri or Royal Mail, deducted from the refund, and Burton prints all four prices by carrier on its own returns page instead of leaving them to be discovered at checkout. Delivery is free over £60, a year of free delivery on every method costs £14.99, and the suit menu is split into eight ranges and four named fits with a Big & Tall department beside them. Figures checked 16 October 2026 against Burton's own pages.

There is no Burton shop to try the suit on in, and that is worth knowing before you click. boohoo group bought the brand on 8 February 2021 for “£25.2 million in cash”, and its own announcement to the market says the purchase covered “all of the e-commerce and digital assets and associated intellectual property rights, including customer data” plus the inventory, while “the Transaction does not include the HIIT brand and the Brands’ retail stores, concessions or franchises”. Burton has been an online-only name since. There is no branch, no fitting room and no alteration service anywhere in the proposition.

The company you contract with is DBZ Marketplace Online Limited, registered at 49-51 Dale Street, Manchester M1 2HF, and some listings on burton.co.uk are marked “Sold & Delivered by Debenhams” while others belong to concession brands you send returns to directly. The same navigation that sells tweed suits and Oxford shirts also sells duvets, rugs, curtains, garden furniture, home gyms, football boots and electric toothbrushes, because the shopfront runs on the group’s Debenhams platform.

Twenty-one days, twenty-eight days, and which of them is a right

Burton’s returns page answers the fit question twice and the two answers do not agree. One passage reads “From the day you receive your order, you must return your item to us within 21 days to secure your refund or store credit.” Another, on the same page, reads “You’ve got 28 days to send something back to us from the day you receive it.” Both were read verbatim on 16 October 2026. Nothing on the page says which route each sentence belongs to, so a man who opens the box, tries the jacket on a fortnight later and posts it on day 25 cannot tell from the policy whether he is in time.

The 28-day figure is not an act of generosity, and that is the useful half of the finding. Regulation 30(3) of the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 gives a 14-day cancellation period that “ends at the end of 14 days after the day on which the goods come into the physical possession of” the buyer. Regulation 35(4) then allows a further period to send them: the goods must go back “without undue delay and in any event not later than 14 days after the day on which the consumer informs the trader”. Fourteen plus fourteen is twenty-eight, measured from delivery, and no retailer can shorten it. The 21 days looks like Burton’s own commercial window for a refund or store credit; the longer figure is the law.

The charge is lawful too, and for a reason worth stating plainly. Regulation 35(5) puts the direct cost of return on the customer unless the trader agreed to bear it or failed to say the customer would. Burton says it, four times over, with the carrier named against each price: InPost £2.49, ASDA £2.49, Evri £2.99, Royal Mail £2.99. On the refund side the page promises money back “up to 14 days from the date your return is posted”, which starts the clock earlier than regulation 34(5) requires — that provision runs 14 days from the day the trader gets the goods back, or from the day the customer supplies evidence of having sent them, whichever is sooner. Regulation 34(2) also obliges a trader to refund the delivery charge paid, capped at the cheapest common delivery option offered.

One limit sits above all of it. Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015 a return fee cannot lawfully be charged on goods that are faulty, of poor quality or not as described, whatever a returns policy says about carriers and parcels. A shirt that arrives with a split seam is not a £2.99 problem for the buyer, and the £2.49 to £2.99 prices only bite on a change of mind or a size that is wrong.

A tape measure built this brand, and the fit guide has none in it

Burton’s own about page is unusually good on where the name came from. “In 1903, Montague Burton was only 18 years old when he borrowed £100 from a relative” “to open the Cross-Tailoring Company in Chesterfield”, selling made-to-measure suits on the slogan “A five guinea suit for 55 shillings”. “By 1929 Burton had four hundred shops, factories, and mills when the company went public.” He was knighted in 1931 “for his efforts in ‘furthering industrial relations and international peace’”, and when he died in 1952 the company was the largest multiple tailor in the world. The post-war demob suit, shirt and tie was the original Full Monty; the firm suited the England side in 1966 and became the F.A. official supplier in 1999.

What replaced the shop’s tape measure is a page called the online suit fit guide, and it contains no measurement at all. Skinny Fit “completely hugs the body”. Slim Fit garments “follow the natural contours of the body”, and “We’d recommend this one for slimmer to medium builds.” Tailored Fit “sits somewhere between a Regular Fit and a Slim Fit”. There is not an inch or a centimetre on it, no instruction on how to measure a chest or an inside leg, and no advice on whether to size up. The separate size guide page at /pages/informational/size-guide returned a client error on four attempts spread across this check, so the tables behind those four fit names could not be read either — that is a failed fetch rather than proof the page is empty, and it is worth saying which of the two it is.

Behind the label there is less law than a shopper would assume. Fibre composition has to be declared on a garment; size does not. The Trading Standards service’s own guide to textile labelling, last reviewed July 2026, deals only with fibre content and non-textile parts of animal origin, and says nothing whatever about sizes or measurements. The only instrument covering size is a voluntary British Standard, BS EN 13402-3:2017, “Size designation of clothes — Part 3: Size labelling based on body measurements and intervals”, published “under the authority of the Standards Policy and Strategy Committee on 31 October 2017” after CEN approved the European text on 26 June 2017, superseding the withdrawn 2013 edition. It “describes a flexible sizing system based on body dimensions” with pictogram examples and body measurement tables for men, women, boys, girls and infants, and it deliberately excludes garment dimensions. Part 1 covers terms, definitions and the body measurement procedure; Part 2 covers primary and secondary dimensions.

The British committee’s own note inside that standard is the part nobody quotes. It records that BSI was obliged to publish the European text but that the UK committee voted against its approval, objected that the title “Size Labelling” is misleading because the document covers designation rather than labelling requirements, and warned that making its content mandatory “would seriously harm the UK clothing industry”. The standard also states that “Compliance with a British Standard cannot confer immunity from legal obligations.” So a chest size printed on a Burton jacket is the retailer’s own arithmetic, not a regulated figure, there is no authority a buyer can appeal to if a 40 fits like a 38, and the £2.49 to £2.99 in the headline is the real cost of finding out.

Deliver+ is sold by a Delaware company and its clock runs for seven days

Alongside the ordinary return route Burton offers a paid add-on, and the returns page is candid about who sells it: “Burton Deliver+ is powered by Seel and purchase will be direct with Seel in accordance with their terms and conditions.” The same page carries Seel’s timings rather than Burton’s. “You must return the product within 7 days from initiating the return request”, and “Seel shall initiate the refund directly to you within 2 days from when Seel approved the return and refund of the product.” That is a third clock on one order, four days shorter than the shortest Burton window and three weeks shorter than the statutory one.

Seel’s own description of the product it sells to retailers explains the commercial logic. Its Return Assurance lets a shopper “Return to Seel for any reason”, Seel pays the refund once it has the parcel, and “merchants keep the full revenue from the original sale”. The price is not a flat fee: it is “Dynamic pricing based on each order’s probability of return”, estimated before the order is placed, so a shopper the model reads as likely to send things back is quoted more for the cover than one it reads as unlikely to. On a product where fit failure is the normal outcome rather than the exception, that is an unusual thing to be priced on.

Who the buyer is dealing with matters as much as the price. Seel’s terms of service name “Seel, Inc.”, are “governed and interpreted by and under the laws of the State of Delaware”, and send court disputes to “state courts located in San Francisco County, California” or “federal courts located in the Northern District of California”. A British customer who buys Deliver+ on a Manchester-registered company’s website has bought an American contract. Burton’s own Deliver+ page at /pages/deliver-plus could not be read on any attempt during this check, so the price range and the exact scope of the UK version are unconfirmed, and anyone considering it should read what Seel sends them before paying — particularly since the 2013 Regulations already give a free right to change their mind, and the Consumer Rights Act already covers a faulty garment at no cost.

Free over £60, or £14.99 for a year of it

The banner across burton.co.uk reads “Free Delivery On Orders Over £60”, which is a high threshold for shirts and a low one for a suit. Under it sits Unlimited Delivery, and that page does read cleanly: “For only £14.99 a year”, a one-off payment with no automatic charge at the end of the term, “you will have 12 months from the date of purchase” to use “free delivery across all of our delivery methods for a whole year”. The membership also carries next-day delivery, VIP member days, exclusive offers and savings that carry across the group’s other sites. There is a 14-day cancellation period, cancellation is by email, and an existing member cannot buy a second year until the current one runs out. Express Delivery “may take a little longer” to the Highlands and Islands and “is not available in the Channel Islands”.

The sum a reader actually wants — how many paid deliveries £14.99 replaces — cannot be done from Burton’s published figures, because the delivery page at /pages/informational/delivery returned a client error on four separate attempts across this check while the returns and Unlimited Delivery pages on the same host loaded normally. No individual delivery price is therefore quotable here. The Frequently Asked Questions page refused in the same way. What can be said is that the £60 free-delivery threshold and the £14.99 membership are the only two delivery figures Burton puts in front of a shopper who has not yet reached the basket.

Two smaller things sit on that page and are worth a sentence each. The family of brands the membership spans is given as Debenhams, Wallis, Oasis, Dorothy Perkins and Coast — a list that does not include Burton, the site selling the membership. And the address for cancelling it is customerservices@burton.com, while the returns page gives customerservices@burton.co.uk. Neither is likely to lose a customer their money, but a reader checking one page against the other will notice both.

A menswear name sitting on a department store's paperwork

Read the returns policy from the top and it becomes obvious it was not written for a menswear brand. Homeware such as “bedlinen, mattresses, pillows, and camping equipment” must come back “unused and in their original unopened packaging”. Vitamins, medicines and toiletries are excluded once a hygiene seal is broken, along with cosmetics, pierced jewellery, swimwear and lingerie. Installed large appliances and furniture not in saleable condition cannot be returned at all, and where Burton does collect “selected large appliances” a further £50 comes out of the refund. None of that is wrong — the site does sell duvets and home gyms — but a man buying a wedding suit is reading a document about mattresses.

One clause is stranger than the rest. The policy states that items bought in store “can only be returned direct to the store”. Burton has no stores: the 2021 announcement excluded “the Brands’ retail stores, concessions or franchises” by name, and nothing on the site offers a branch finder. The sentence is a department store’s, inherited whole.

The seller behind the badge takes a little work to pin down. Non-concession stock is labelled “Sold & Delivered by Debenhams” even on burton.co.uk, concession stock carries “Sold & Delivered By” a partner brand with the instruction to “return the item direct to the Concessionaire Brand that supplied the item” using that brand’s own process, and the contract itself is “under a contract with DBZ Marketplace Online Limited”. Companies House records that company as 13135555, active, incorporated 14 January 2021 at 49-51 Dale Street, Manchester M1 2HF, under SIC 47910, and shows it trading as Acraman 550 Limited for eleven days, then Debenhams.com Online Limited from 25 January 2021, then Debenhams Brands Limited from 30 March 2023, before taking its present name on 26 November 2024. Its accounts are filed under an audit exemption with a parent-company guarantee, last made up to 28 February 2025.

What could not be read is the paperwork a careful buyer would want next. The terms and conditions page, the concessionaire brand list and the size guide all returned client errors on repeated attempts from a host that was serving its returns page at the same moment, so the governing law, any named dispute-resolution body and the full concession roster are unverified here rather than absent. A reader who wants them should open those four links before ordering and ask customer services for the ones that do not load.

Where the growth sits inside the group, and why Burton is invisible in it

Burton’s owner is boohoo group plc, which trades as Debenhams Group and sits on the market under the ticker DEBS. Its half-year results for the six months to 31 August 2025, announced on 27 November 2025 and filed on the Financial Conduct Authority’s National Storage Mechanism, give the shape of the business the Burton site belongs to. “Revenue was £296.9m, down 23%”. “Gross margin remained broadly stable at 52.9%”, 60 basis points lower. “Adjusted EBITDA increased to £20.0m (H125: £19.0m)”. The loss before tax from continuing operations came in at £2.5m against £130.0m a year earlier, and “Net debt improved to £111.1m (H125: £143.1m)”.

The split inside those figures is the interesting part. The Debenhams brand grew gross merchandise value “20% to £318.8m” on segment revenue of £98.3m, with adjusted EBITDA up 50% at a margin of about 15%, while “Youth Brands GMV decreased 41% to £258.0m” on revenue of £172.4m. Marketplace stock accounted for 31.6% of gross merchandise value before returns. The reported segments are Youth Brands, Debenhams and Karen Millen, and Burton is in none of them by name — so there is no published revenue, order count or customer number for Burton itself, from the group or from anywhere else.

Set that against what the brand cost. The 8 February 2021 announcement priced Burton, Dorothy Perkins and Wallis together at £25.2m and recorded that in the year to 29 August 2020 the three brands had “unaudited revenues of approximately £427.8 million across all channels and an unaudited EBITDA loss of £14.3 million”, of which “The ongoing businesses for the Brands generated unaudited revenues of approximately £178.8 million”. Buying three national names and their customer data for less than a fifth of one year’s continuing revenue tells you what the high street was worth in February 2021, and it explains why the brand now lives on somebody else’s platform. The group’s annual results for the year to 28 February 2026 could not be read from a primary source during this check, because its investor site refuses automated access, so every figure above is the half-year to 31 August 2025 and should be read with that date attached.

The supply-chain statement a Burton shopper is sent to stops in 2022

The footer of every page on burton.co.uk carries a link marked Modern Slavery Statement, and it points off the site to a boohoo group PDF. That document “covers the FY22 reporting period from 1 March 2021 until 28 February 2022” and carries the date 31.08.2022 against a line headed Board Representative with no name or title beside it. Section 54 of the Modern Slavery Act 2015 expects a statement for each financial year, and four financial years have closed since this one. Burton is not mentioned anywhere in it.

Taken on its own terms the statement is numerate, which makes the age of it the only real complaint. It “covers our own operations and product supply chains for all of our group brands” across 13 brands, and reports 1,286 tier 1 factories as at 31 July 2022 across 29 sourcing countries — China 487, Turkey 228, India 157, the UK 80, Pakistan 78 and Bangladesh 71. The group accepts audits from 34 audit bodies provided they are within 12 months, says 95% of tier 1 factories are rated excellent, good or fair, and records 60% of audits graded critical as evidenced corrected. It gives 5,901 people employed directly at 31 January 2022 and 2,254 cotton farmers in a Pakistan programme. A menswear buyer gets none of it broken out: the only menswear-adjacent brand named is boohooMAN.

The public safety record is cleaner and shorter. A keyword search for “burton” on the government’s product safety alerts, reports and recalls service returned 0 reports on 16 October 2026, with the service reporting “There are no matching results.” That is an absence rather than an endorsement, and it is the only independent check available: there is no rated customer-satisfaction survey for clothing retailers from Which?, whose best-and-worst-shops guides cover baby shops, technology and appliances, and furniture, homeware and DIY, and nothing else.

Common questions

How much does it cost to return something to Burton?

Burton deducts the return cost from your refund and publishes it by carrier: £2.49 through InPost, £2.49 through ASDA, £2.99 through Evri and £2.99 through Royal Mail. A separate passage on the same page describes “a one off return fee from £2.99”, and the word “from” is not resolved into a maximum anywhere. Start the return in the returns portal and print the label at home. Collection is only offered on selected large appliances, and that costs a further £50 out of the refund.

How long have I got to send a Burton order back — 21 days or 28?

Burton’s returns page states both, in different passages, and does not say which route each belongs to. The safe reading is that 21 days is Burton’s own window for a refund or store credit and 28 days reflects the statutory route, because regulation 30(3) of the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 gives you 14 days from taking physical possession to cancel, and regulation 35(4) then gives you a further 14 days to send the goods back. If you want to be certain of a refund without an argument, post it inside 21 days.

Can Burton charge me £2.99 for returning something faulty?

No. Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015 a return fee cannot lawfully be charged on goods that are faulty, of poor quality or not as described, whatever a returns policy says about carriers and parcel prices. The £2.49 to £2.99 charge applies to a change of mind or a size that does not fit. If a seam has gone or the item is not what was advertised, say so when you start the return and ask for the postage back as well.

Is Burton Unlimited Delivery worth £14.99?

It is a one-off £14.99 for 12 months from the date of purchase, it does not renew automatically, it covers “free delivery across all of our delivery methods for a whole year” including next day, and it carries member days and offers that work across the group’s other sites. Whether it pays for itself cannot be worked out from Burton’s published figures, because the delivery page would not load on four attempts during this check and no individual delivery price is quotable. Delivery is free over £60 without it, so the membership is worth most to someone buying shirts one at a time.

What is Burton Deliver+, and do I need it?

It is a paid add-on at checkout, and Burton’s own page says it is “powered by Seel and purchase will be direct with Seel”. Seel describes the product as returns cover priced by “Dynamic pricing based on each order’s probability of return”, and the terms on Burton’s page give you 7 days to return after starting a request, with a refund within 2 days of approval. The contract is with Seel, Inc. under Delaware law, with disputes heard in California. Weigh it against the fact that the 2013 Regulations already give you 28 days to change your mind and the Consumer Rights Act already covers a faulty item at no cost.

Are there any Burton shops left?

No. boohoo group bought the brand on 8 February 2021 for £25.2 million in cash, and its announcement to the market says the deal covered the e-commerce and digital assets, the intellectual property and the inventory but “does not include the HIIT brand and the Brands’ retail stores, concessions or franchises”. There is no branch, no fitting room and no alteration service. The returns policy still carries a clause saying items bought in store can only be returned to the store, which is inherited from the Debenhams document it is built on.

Who am I actually buying from on burton.co.uk?

The returns page states that goods are supplied “under a contract with DBZ Marketplace Online Limited” — company 13135555, incorporated 14 January 2021 and registered at 49-51 Dale Street, Manchester M1 2HF. Non-concession stock is labelled “Sold & Delivered by Debenhams” even on the Burton site, and concession items are sold by a partner brand you return to directly under its own process. The terms and conditions page would not load during this check, so the governing law clause and any named dispute-resolution scheme could not be confirmed.

Our verdict

Burton is a 123-year-old tailoring name sold as an online department store, and it is honest about the one number that matters most: £2.49 to £2.99 a parcel to send a suit back, printed by carrier before you buy. The 28 days on the same page is the law’s 14 plus 14, not a favour, and a faulty garment costs nothing to return whatever the policy says. Against that, the paperwork is a department store’s rather than a tailor’s — two return windows, a store-returns clause on a brand with no stores, a fit guide with no measurements and a Delaware add-on on a seven-day clock. Buy the suit on the strength of the range and the £60 free-delivery line, order two sizes if the fit matters, and send the wrong one back inside 21 days to stay clear of the argument.
