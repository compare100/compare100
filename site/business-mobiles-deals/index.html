BusinessMobiles.com Review: Business Contracts Across EE, O2, Vodafone and Three

Rates and terms checked 6 September 2026 · Business Mobiles · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

BusinessMobiles.com is a West London dealer that has been selling mobile contracts to UK companies since 1999 and now says it manages more than 20,000 business mobile lines. What makes it worth a look is the range: it sells EE, O2, Vodafone and Three from one order desk, which very few independents do, and it will quote a sole trader on a single line as readily as a company with fifty. Headline prices at the time of writing start at £1 a month for a business SIM, £16 a month for a Samsung handset deal, £14 a month for mobile broadband and £22 a month for a business laptop, with free next-working-day delivery to most of the UK and a sales line on 0800 999 2999 open 8.30am to 5.30pm on weekdays.

One number needs reading carefully before you compare anything. Every price on the site excludes VAT, and the site says so plainly: "Our prices always exclude VAT." A £16 business tariff is £19.20 leaving the bank account, so setting it against a £19 consumer deal is not the like-for-like comparison it looks like. If the business is VAT registered it reclaims the difference and the ex-VAT figure is the real one; if it is not, the inclusive price is what it pays.

The company is an official partner of all four networks rather than a reseller of one, it won EE's 2025 Business Growth Award for Partners, and it offers an early upgrade from six months into a contract on selected networks — a good deal earlier than most dealers will touch a handset. Contracts run 12, 24 or 36 months.

The name took three goes to arrive at

The trading story on the About page and the registry record agree on the year and differ on the detail, which is worth knowing if you are checking who you are dealing with. Companies House holds the firm as BUSINESSMOBILES.COM LTD, company number 03772025, incorporated on 17 May 1999 and active, at Suite 301, Boundary House, Boston Road, Hanwell, London W7 2QE, under SIC code 61200 — wireless telecommunications activities. Its last accounts were made up to 31 December 2024, the next are due by 30 September 2026, and the most recent confirmation statement was dated 16 May 2026.

The name changed three times before it settled. The company was incorporated as FAST FONES DIRECT LTD, became 31E CELLULAR OPERATIONS LIMITED on 24 May 2000, then 31E CELLULAR LIMITED on 13 July 2000, and finally took its present name on 17 January 2007. The About page tells the same story a year early — "In 2006, we became BusinessMobiles.com" — which is the sort of small drift that creeps into copy nobody has re-read for a while. The 1999 founding date, which is the part that matters, checks out exactly.

Buying as a business moves you outside the consumer rulebook

This is the fact that separates a business mobile purchase from the one a shopper makes, and almost nothing on any dealer's site explains it. The 14-day cancellation right people associate with buying online is a consumer right under the distance selling rules, and it does not follow a company that buys in its own name. Neither does the Consumer Rights Act 2015, whose 30-day short-term right to reject faulty goods is written for an individual acting for purposes wholly or mainly outside a trade or business. A limited company or a partnership buying handsets sits outside both.

Read that way, the return window in BusinessMobiles.com's own terms is a commercial concession rather than a legal minimum. The terms give "a 7-day right of return. The 7 days commencing on the date your order is activated or despatched, whichever is earlier". Seven days is half the consumer period and the clock can start at despatch rather than delivery, so a handset that spends two days in transit has five days left on arrival. That is still seven days more than the law obliges a business seller to give, and it is worth setting against dealers who give none.

Two charges sit alongside it. The customer pays the cost of return carriage, and an Incomplete Restocking Charge of £250 applies where a handset comes back without all of its parts. Keep the box, the cable and the paperwork.

Twelve, 24 or 36 months — and the longest one has a rule attached

The FAQ page puts contract length at "12, 24 or 36 months". The 12-month option is not a courtesy: Ofcom requires providers to offer at least one 12-month contract for each service, and that requirement covers business customers of any size. The 36-month option is the one that needs a question asked, because Ofcom's General Conditions cap the length of most small-business contracts at two years.

Condition C1.11 is explicit: "Regulated Providers shall not include a term in any contract, other than an Instalment Contract for a Physical Connection, with a Relevant Customer, that stipulates a Commitment Period of more than 24 months in duration." A Relevant Customer for that purpose covers consumers, microenterprise and small enterprise customers and not-for-profit organisations, unless the business has expressly agreed otherwise. So a 36-month term is available to larger companies, and to a small one only where it has knowingly signed away the cap. Nothing on the site says which of those applies to a given quote, and it is a fair thing to ask on the phone before you sign.

The same conditions give small business customers end-of-contract notifications and annual best-tariff information, so a company that stays put after its minimum term should be told, in writing, what it is paying and what it could pay instead.

The annual rise is pounds and pence on some tariffs and a percentage on others

Ofcom's statement of 19 July 2024 banned inflation-linked mid-contract increases, and from 17 January 2025 any rise written into a new contract has to be expressed in pounds and pence. The scope is wider than most coverage suggests. The statement applies the rules to services "purchased by residential customers, Microenterprise or Small Enterprise Customers and Not-For-Profit Customers" — small businesses are inside the protection, not outside it.

The catch is that a business tariff is not automatically a small-business tariff, and the networks this dealer sells are running several regimes at once. EE Business raises prices from 31 March each year: contracts taken between 2 September and 18 November 2024 go up by £3.00 a month across every plan type, while contracts from 19 November 2024 onwards go up by £3.00 on handset plans and £1.50 on SIM-only, tablet, laptop, watch and router plans. Anything signed before 2 September 2024 is still on the old CPI plus 3.9% formula.

Three Business steps its increase by tariff size each April — "Plans with an original Monthly Charge of £9.99 or less will increase by £0.50 per month. Plans from £10 to £20 will increase by £0.75 per month. Plans from £20.01 will increase by £1.50 per month" — but its Adapt and Dynamic plans are the exception and rise by 4.5% each April instead. Business broadband goes up £1.60 a month excluding VAT.

Vodafone Business is the clearest illustration that the pounds-and-pence rule has not reached every business bill. Contracts entered on or after 9 December 2020 are rising from 1 April 2026 by CPI of 3.4% plus 3.9%, a combined 7.3% — £1.61 a month on a £22 plan and £2.92 on a £40 one. Older contracts move with RPI at 4.2%, published on 21 January 2026. Vodafone's SOHO customers on contracts from 2 July 2024 get a fixed £1.50 excluding VAT instead, and contracts from 12 November 2025 rise by between £2.08 and £3.00. O2's business price page is built around RPI adjustments and offers a calculator rather than a published schedule; its figures load through JavaScript and could not be read directly, so the amount on an O2 business tariff needs confirming with the dealer.

The arithmetic is worth doing before you sign rather than after. Take the EE Business iPhone 17 deal the site lists at £33.00 a month excluding VAT on a 24-month term, bought in September. It runs seven months at £33.00, twelve at £36.00 and five at £39.00 — £858 across the term, against £792 if you simply multiply the headline by 24. That is £66 the advertised figure does not show, and £1,029.60 once VAT is added to what actually leaves the account.

One phone per employee, and the contract has to be in the company's name

The FAQ says a business contract "is also tax deductible", which is true and slightly undersells the mechanism. HMRC's guidance exempts a mobile phone provided to an employee from tax and from reporting entirely, but on conditions. The exemption applies where "you provide your employee with only one mobile phone or SIM card" and "the phone contract is between you and the supplier". A second handset for the same employee falls outside it, and so does a phone bought on the employee's own account and reimbursed. The exemption also does not apply where the phone comes through a salary sacrifice arrangement, which has to be reported.

That is the practical reason a company buys through a business channel rather than picking up a consumer deal in a shop. The contract has to be in the company's name for the exemption to work, and a dealer selling business tariffs puts it there as a matter of course. It is a genuine advantage of the route, and neither the FAQ nor any network page spells it out.

Who adjudicates a dispute depends on how many people you employ

Ofcom approves exactly two dispute schemes for telecoms: CISAS, the Communication and Internet Services Adjudication Scheme, and the Communications Ombudsman. Of the four networks on this site, O2, Vodafone and Three belong to CISAS while EE belongs to the Communications Ombudsman. Two companies buying the same kind of contract from the same dealer on the same day can end up in front of different adjudicators, depending only on which network they chose.

Eligibility is narrower than most buyers expect. Ofcom's own wording admits a residential customer, "a small business (up to 10 employees)" or a not-for-profit with up to ten working individuals, volunteers excluded. An eleven-person firm has no free adjudicator at all and is left with the courts. A complaint can be taken to the scheme once "it has been at least six weeks since you made the initial complaint", or straight away on a deadlock letter, which stays usable for 12 months.

None of that reaches the dealer itself. BusinessMobiles.com's terms route a dispute through negotiation, then mediation with LawBite, then arbitration if it is unresolved within 60 days. That is a private contractual ladder rather than an Ofcom-approved scheme, so a disagreement about what was promised at the point of sale — rather than about the network service — sits outside both CISAS and the Ombudsman. The terms also exclude liability for "loss of income, profit, business, data, contracts, goodwill or savings", while keeping responsibility for death or personal injury caused by negligence and for fraud.

Where the site's own numbers stop agreeing with each other

The savings claims are the weakest part of the site. The EE page leads on "Save 37% on Phone/SIM" and "over 30% off"; the Three page offers "Save over 20% on network prices". Neither page shows what the 37% or the 20% is measured against, over what term, or on which tariff. The CAP Code sets a clear standard for this: rule 3.34 requires that comparisons with identifiable competitors "objectively compare one or more material, relevant, verifiable and representative feature of those products", and CAP's guidance asks advertisers to signpost how a reader can check. The ASA has upheld against telecoms comparison claims on exactly that ground before, including Vodafone's "The UK's Best Network" in 2021, where the verification detail was there but out of reach. A dealer buying at trade prices very probably can beat a network's list price; publishing the comparison would make the claim worth more, not less.

Two of the company's own figures also disagree. The home page says 27 years of experience; the EE and Three pages both say 26 Years in Business. Counting from May 1999, 27 is right and the network pages are a year behind. The network awards quoted are also ageing: Three's Ookla "Fastest 5G Network" run is cited as five awards ending H1 2023, which is three years old, and predates the merger of Vodafone UK and Three UK.

There is no independent rating for a mobile dealer to fall back on. Ofcom's quarterly complaints data measures networks rather than the shops that sell them, there is no Defaqto equivalent for this trade, and the review score shown on the site is collected on the company's own behalf. The honest position is that the networks behind the contract are measured and the dealer in front of it is not.

Common questions

Do the prices include VAT?

No. The site states that its prices always exclude VAT, so a £16 tariff is £19.20 including VAT at 20%. A VAT-registered business reclaims that; one below the registration threshold pays it. Comparing a business price against a consumer price without adding VAT first is not a like-for-like comparison.

Can a sole trader buy from BusinessMobiles.com?

Yes. Asked whether there is a minimum number of lines, the FAQ answers: "No. We are as passionate about serving our sole traders as we are about serving our corporates." Contracts run 12, 24 or 36 months.

What happens if the handset is faulty or I change my mind?

The terms give a 7-day right of return running from activation or despatch, whichever is earlier, with the customer paying return carriage and a £250 Incomplete Restocking Charge if parts are missing. The 14-day distance selling right and the Consumer Rights Act 2015 both apply to consumers rather than to a company buying in its own name, so this window is a commercial concession and not a statutory minimum.

Will my monthly price go up during the contract?

Almost certainly, and by an amount that depends on the network. EE Business raises prices each 31 March by £3.00 on handset plans and £1.50 on SIM-only and device plans taken from 19 November 2024. Three steps its April increase from £0.50 to £1.50 by tariff size, except Adapt and Dynamic plans which rise 4.5%. Many Vodafone business contracts rise by CPI plus 3.9% — 7.3% from 1 April 2026. Ask for the figure in pounds before signing.

Who do I complain to if something goes wrong?

For a problem with the mobile service, the route runs through the network: EE is a member of the Communications Ombudsman, while O2, Vodafone and Three all belong to CISAS. You can escalate after six weeks, or immediately with a deadlock letter, if you are a business with up to 10 employees. For a dispute with the dealer itself, its terms use negotiation, then LawBite mediation, then arbitration after 60 days.

Our verdict

A long-established West London dealer with a real advantage: it quotes all four networks, it takes single-line customers, and it puts the contract in the company's name, which is what HMRC's one-phone-per-employee exemption actually requires. The prices are ex-VAT and the advertised monthly figure is not the whole term — an EE Business plan at £33 a month costs £858 over 24 months once the 31 March increases land, not £792. Ring 0800 999 2999 and ask three things before signing: what the annual increase is in pounds for the tariff you are being quoted, whether the term offered is 12, 24 or 36 months and why, and which network's dispute scheme you would be using. The answers are all knowable and none of them are on the website.
