Cruise Critic Review: What Its Ratings and Prices Are Built On

Rates and terms checked 23 October 2026 · Worldwide Cruises · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Cruise Critic has been publishing cruise reviews since 1995 and now carries more than 700,000 of them, covering over 1,100 ships, 120-plus cruise lines and almost 500 ports. Its own at-a-glance figures give 2 million members, 55 million opinions, reviews and photos, and 5.6 million visitors a month. Reading all of it costs nothing, and the site says so in the plainest terms: “Cruise Critic is not a booking agent and does not charge any service fees to users of our site”.

That sentence is the most useful thing on the site, because it tells you what the next click does. Cruise Critic does not sell cruises and its terms of use say outright that it “is not a travel agency and does not provide or own transportation services or accommodations”. The fares on its deal cards belong to travel agencies that pay to be there and are named on the cards themselves: on the UK pages on 23 October 2026 those included Onyx Cruises, Panache Cruises and Cruise118, the last of them part of the World Travel Holdings group and both ABTA and ATOL protected. Whoever you click through to is the company that takes your money, holds your booking and answers the phone.

The UK site is a locale of the American one rather than a British business, and two lines of its own small print matter before you compare anything. On cruisecritic.com/en-gb the fares are quoted in sterling and the disclaimer reads “Rates are in GBP and valid for UK and Ireland residents” only, and “Taxes, fees and port expenses not included”. On the American version of the same page the same template says the opposite: “Taxes, fees and port expenses included”. A British reader comparing a sterling fare here against a dollar fare there is comparing a pre-tax price with a post-tax one.

The £599 on the deal card, and what the small print takes off it

On 23 October 2026 the UK deals page carried 107 fares. The cheapest was £599 per person on MSC Euribia to the Canary Islands and the dearest £6,499 on Azamara Quest to South America, a spread of nearly eleven to one. Four of the five cheapest were MSC sailings: Euribia at £599, Fantasia at £649, Virtuosa at £679 and Euribia again at £679. Royal Caribbean’s cheapest was £899 on Rhapsody of the Seas, Celebrity’s £1,029 on Infinity and Cunard’s £1,590 on Queen Anne.

The disclaimer under those fares is fifteen sentences long and worth reading before you treat any of them as a price. The fares are “per person, double occupancy, and are based on specific cabin types and sailing dates” and “may not be available for all cabin types/sailings”. Taxes, fees and port expenses are excluded. A “fuel supplement may apply”. Average prices are “updated nightly”, the site “does not guarantee any specific rates or prices” and it “makes no guarantees for availability of prices advertised on our site”. Listed prices “may have blackout dates, qualifications or restrictions”. And the lowest figure of all is sourced to a company nobody names: “Lowest pricing is based on our 3rd party pricing supplier and valid as of 23 October 2026”.

Fifty-nine of the 107 fares show no number of nights at all, which means more than half of them cannot be set against each other even roughly. The page itself demonstrates the problem: Norwegian Encore to the Mexican Riviera appeared twice at the same £1,199, once as a seven-night cruise with three nights in Los Angeles and once as a fourteen-night sailing. Where nights are shown they run from a five-night Mediterranean cruise on MSC Virtuosa to twenty-nine nights on MSC Sinfonia across the Indian Ocean, so a bare per-person fare on this page can mean anything from about £100 a night to about £250.

One more labelling quirk, and it is on every card. The headline figure is printed as “£1,890 pp” and the detail box beneath it prints the identical number as “Price: £1,890 /night”. Three cards were checked on the UK site and three on the American one and all six did the same thing. A per-person cruise fare presented as a nightly rate is the kind of error that makes a cheap sailing look dear and a long one look like a bargain.

A 3.9 that reconciles to the decimal

The member score is the site’s main product and it is given as a single number out of five with a distribution beneath it. Royal Caribbean International carries 44,045 reviews and an overall “3.9”, built from 18,960 rated Excellent, 11,310 Very Good, 7,554 Average, 3,922 Poor and 2,296 Terrible. Those five bands account for 44,042 of the 44,045 the page counts. Expressed as shares, 43% of reviewers rated the line Excellent, 69% rated it Excellent or Very Good and 14% rated it Poor or Terrible.

Cruise Critic publishes no methodology, so we did the arithmetic. Scoring the bands five down to one and taking a plain average of the 44,042 gives 3.925, which rounds to the 3.9 on the page. That is this review’s own calculation rather than the site’s, and it establishes something the site never states: the score is an unweighted mean of every review ever left, with no recency weighting, no adjustment for sample size and no discounting of old sailings. A ship refurbished last year and a ship reviewed in 2014 contribute equally.

That matters more than it sounds, because the scores are then used to pick winners. The Best in Cruise Awards reached their seventeenth year in 2025 and run to nine categories, with the Cruisers’ Choice strand “based on the best-reviewed cruise lines of the past year from real cruisers” and the rest chosen by editors who “spend hundreds of nights at sea on dozens of cruise lines each year”. The page says “A minimum number of reviews is required to be considered” without saying what the minimum is, and it does not define “the past year” either.

What one review carries, and what it leaves out

An individual review is better documented than most platforms manage. It names the ship and the itinerary, gives a sail date, and identifies the reviewer by screen name, the number of cruises they have taken and an age bracket — one Allure of the Seas review from a September 2026 Western Caribbean sailing reads “5 Cruises” and “Age 20s”. Reviews can be tagged “With children” or “With accessible traveler”, carry member photographs, and can be marked helpful by other readers. Filters cover cruise line, ship and three-month sail-date windows. For working out whether a reviewer is anything like you, that is a genuinely useful set of fields.

What is missing is any statement about verification. Nothing on a review says it has been checked against a booking, and no page on the site sets out whether a reviewer must have sailed, how soon after a cruise a review may be left, or what happens to a review the cruise line disputes. The Competition and Markets Authority’s fake reviews guidance CMA208, in force since 7 April 2025, requires a publisher to take “reasonable and proportionate” steps to detect and deal with fake reviews and notes that it may, though it need not, restrict reviews to people it can verify. Cruise Critic does not say which side of that line it sits on.

The editor ratings are thinner still. The ship index prints an “Editor Rating” out of 5.0 — Noordam at 3.5, Viking Star at 5.0 — with no scale definition, no category breakdown and no explanation of how it relates to the member score. Four ships on the index carry “-- / 5.0” because they are too new to have been sailed, and review counts per ship run from five on Disney Destiny to 2,524 on Celebrity Summit, which is a thirty-fold difference in evidence presented identically.

The AI policy that bans AI from reviewing anything

Cruise Critic publishes an AI use policy, which almost no consumer travel site does, and it is unusually specific. “All opinions, reviews, rankings and editorial recommendations on Cruise Critic are created by humans”. “Cruise Critic does not permit freelance contributors to use AI tools to generate story content”. Quotes and expert commentary must “come directly from a real human expert”. On the member side, “AI-generated reviews are strictly prohibited”, AI-written forum posts are not allowed, member photographs must be original and not AI-generated or manipulated, and suspected AI content may be removed or rejected without notice.

Where AI is allowed, the uses are narrow and named: internal factual summarising for preview ship reviews and port guides from cruise line media materials, deck plans and itinerary maps; research by freelancers that the writer must verify and fact-check; and AI-generated stock-style images or artist renderings in limited cases. Everything so produced is reviewed by editorial staff before publication, “Materials created by AI will clearly be labeled as such”, and preview content is revised once staff have actually been aboard.

The weakness is the one that affects every policy on the site. The AI use policy carries no date and no version number, so there is no way to tell when it was written or whether it has been changed. A policy that forbids undated, unverifiable content would be stronger if it were dated itself.

Tripadvisor owns it and never mentions it

The corporate chain is longer than the site lets on. The privacy policy, effective 16 June 2026, states that “Tripadvisor LLC (d/b/a Cruise Critic) is the controller of personal information we collect” and that the services are owned and operated by that company through The Independent Traveler, Inc., whose name sits in the copyright line as “1995—2026, The Independent Traveler, Inc.”. The acquisition was announced on 23 May 2007, when TripAdvisor LLC — then described as “an operating company of Expedia, Inc.” — bought The Independent Traveler, Inc., publisher of Cruise Critic and IndependentTraveler.com, on undisclosed terms. The release said the business “operates profitably”, gave Cruise Critic “approximately five million visitors per year” and named founder and president Kathleen Tucker, who stayed on. IndependentTraveler.com had launched on America Online in 1990.

Set that five million a year against the 5.6 million a month the site claims now and the growth is roughly thirteen-fold. The UK end of it is thinner than the sterling pricing suggests. The privacy policy names Tripadvisor Limited of 10 Norton Folgate, London E1 6DB as the UK representative and Tripadvisor Ireland Limited, care of Matheson in Dublin, as the EU one. Companies House confirms Tripadvisor Limited as company 05612751, incorporated 4 November 2005 at that address, alongside Tripadvisor UK Holdco Limited (08279085, 2 November 2012) and two limited partnerships registered on 6 November 2012. A representative is not a contracting party, and no company called Cruise Critic or The Independent Traveler appears on the UK register at all.

The parent is listed and publishes audited numbers twice a year, which makes the silence about this business measurable. Tripadvisor’s second-quarter results of 6 August 2026, for the quarter ended 30 June 2026, give revenue of US$441.9 million, down 7% year on year, split between Experiences at US$278.6 million (up 3%) and Hotels and Other at US$163.3 million (down 21%), with net income of US$22.8 million and diluted earnings of US$0.19 a share against a US$10.0 million net loss for the half once discontinued operations are counted. The sale of TheFork to American Express “is expected to be completed by the end of 2026”. Neither the word Cruise Critic nor the word cruise appears anywhere in that announcement, so no revenue, profit or audience figure for this site exists in any audited document.

Massachusetts law, one hundred dollars, and no telephone number

The terms of use are short on comfort for a British reader. “[T]his Agreement is governed by the laws of the Commonwealth of Massachusetts, USA”, and users “consent to the exclusive jurisdiction and venue of courts in Massachusetts, USA”. Liability is capped hard: “the Tripadvisor Media Group Companies’ liabilities will in no event exceed One-Hundred Dollars (US $100.00)”. The site warns in capitals that its content “MAY INCLUDE INACCURACIES OR ERRORS, INCLUDING RESERVATION AVAILABILITY AND PRICING ERRORS” and reserves the right to correct a pricing error even on a pending reservation. Where a booking is made with a third-party supplier, “Cruise Critic will have no liability” for that supplier’s acts or omissions.

Anything you post belongs to the site in perpetuity. Submitting content grants “a nonexclusive, royalty-free, perpetual, transferable, irrevocable and fully sub-licensable right” to use it, while the site “does not edit or control the User Messages” and may remove them at its discretion. Members writing thirty years of forum history on the Roll Calls boards, one section of which carries millions of posts, have signed that licence without any corresponding undertaking about the content’s accuracy.

The terms also appear not to have been looked at for a while. The page states that revision dates appear at the bottom, and no revision date is shown; the footer there reads “2022 The Independent Traveler, Inc.” while every other page on the site reads 1995 to 2026. There is no postal address and no telephone number anywhere on the site, including the contact page. The only channel is feedback@cruisecritic.com and a community help centre for forum problems, and no ombudsman or alternative dispute resolution scheme is named, because none covers a travel review website.

Where the money is protected, and it is not here

Because Cruise Critic takes no payment, nothing it does protects a penny of your holiday. Protection attaches to whoever sells you the cruise, and the two British schemes work differently. ATOL, introduced in 1973 and run by the Civil Aviation Authority, is “financial protection when you book a package trip that includes a flight” and the CAA states flatly that “It is not travel insurance”. You cannot claim from it “for cancellations, complaints, sickness, injury, or substandard hygiene during your holiday”, nor for a delayed flight. A claim must reach the CAA within twelve months of the ATOL holder’s failure; it aims to acknowledge within five working days and assess within twenty-eight working days of receiving everything it needs. Notably, the CAA’s own explanation of the scheme, last modified 30 April 2025, does not mention cruises once.

So a fly-cruise bought as a package should produce an ATOL certificate naming “the ATOL holder who is providing your protection”, and a cruise bought without a flight will not. For that, the protection comes from the seller’s own arrangements under the Package Travel and Linked Travel Arrangements Regulations 2018, which for a high-street cruise specialist usually means an ABTA bond. Cruise118, the sponsored seller against a Cunard sailing on the UK Norway listing, says it is “both ATOL and ABTA protected” and is part of the World Travel Holdings group. That is the right question to ask of any name you click through to, and the answer will not be on Cruise Critic’s pages.

Two further gaps are worth knowing about before you rely on a deal card. The UK home page was running a banner reading “Disney Wish is coming to the UK for the first time ever! Book your cabin now for just £1 pp deposit!* T&Cs apply” — and the asterisk leads to no footnote anywhere on the page. And the disclaimer’s own advice is the correct advice: “When you book with one of our partners, please be sure to check their site for a full disclosure of all applicable fees”.

Eighty-six out of a hundred, twice a year, unannounced

There is one scored, published, independent inspection of a cruise ship, and Cruise Critic does not show it. The United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention runs the Vessel Sanitation Program, and the CDC’s own account of how it works — a Vessel Sanitation Program paper published in the Journal of Environmental Health in March 2008 — sets out the rules. Inspections are “twice-annual, unannounced”, they are “scored on the basis of a possible 100 points”, a passing score is “86 or higher”, a failure means a score below 86 and an unannounced re-inspection within 45 days. The scheme covers ships “sailing from foreign to U.S. ports and carrying 13 or more passengers”, which takes in most of the Caribbean and Alaska fleet a British holidaymaker would consider, and it is funded entirely by fees charged per inspection on a cost-recovery basis according to each vessel’s gross registered tonnage.

A number out of 100 from an unannounced government inspection twice a year is a harder measure than any average of member opinion, and it is free. Cruise Critic rates more than 1,100 ships on an undefined five-point scale and publishes none of it. The CDC’s current inspection-report index could not be opened during this check, so the individual ship scores are not quoted here and should be read from the CDC’s own site before being relied on; the scheme’s thresholds above come from the CDC’s 2008 description of it and should be confirmed against the programme’s current operations manual.

No ratings agency fills the gap either. Defaqto rates insurance, not travel websites; Smart Money People does not list cruise or travel platforms; and the national tourist board assessment schemes grade hotels and hostels rather than booking or review sites. On this page, an absence is the honest answer: there is no independent assessment of Cruise Critic’s ratings by anybody.

2.54 million British cruises, and four different review counts

The market Cruise Critic is writing for is bigger than it has ever been. The Cruise Lines International Association’s UK and Ireland release of 22 April 2026 reports that British and Irish holidaymakers took 2.54 million ocean cruises in 2025, beating the previous high of 2.4 million in 2024. The Mediterranean took 34.5% of them, northern Europe 27.2% and the Caribbean 13.7%, the last growing 12% year on year. The average cruise ran 9.6 days against 9.7 in 2024, the average passenger was 54.6 years old, and the number of children under twelve rose 7%. “Cruise continues to appeal to a wide range of holidaymakers”, said Andy Harmer, managing director of CLIA UK and Ireland. On a market of that size and that age profile, a free library of 700,000 first-hand reviews is worth having.

Which brings us to the counting. The about page says “more than 700,000 cruise reviews” in its text and “more than 650,000 cruise reviews” in its own meta description. The ship index header says “650,000+ reviews”. The UK home page’s meta description — the line a British searcher reads before clicking — says “Over 150,000+ cruise reviews, ship ratings and the largest cruise forum”, which is less than a quarter of the figure two clicks away. Monthly audience is given twice on one page as “more than five million” and “5.6M+”.

None of those numbers is likely to be invented, and the review archive is plainly enormous whichever is right. The point is narrower and it is about maintenance: a site whose entire value rests on how many people have reported back cannot tell you, on four of its own pages, how many that is. The same pattern shows up in the undated AI policy, the 2022 copyright line on the terms, and an asterisk with no footnote.

Where it wins

  • Free with no registration, no booking fee and no service fee of any kind, and the site says so in its own disclaimer
  • More than 700,000 member reviews across 1,100-plus ships, 120-plus cruise lines and almost 500 ports — an archive no cruise line or travel agent can match
  • An individual review names the ship, the itinerary and the sail date and shows the reviewer’s cruise count and age bracket, which is more context than most platforms publish
  • A published AI use policy that bans AI-written reviews, AI-written forum posts and AI-generated member photographs, and requires AI-assisted editorial content to be labelled
  • Editorial independence stated plainly: cruise lines are “forbidden from reviewing or approving any material before publication”, and press trips are disclosed rather than hidden
  • Thirty years of forum history, including per-ship Roll Call boards running to millions of posts, which is where a specific question about a specific sailing gets answered
  • Fares are shown with the seller named on the card, so the company you would actually be buying from is identifiable before you click

Where it falls short

  • No rating methodology is published anywhere. There is no scale definition, no category breakdown, no minimum review count and no stated relationship between the Editor Rating and the member score — and our own arithmetic on Royal Caribbean’s 44,042 banded reviews shows the headline 3.9 is a plain unweighted average of every review ever left, with no recency weighting at all
  • Nothing on the site says whether a reviewer has to have sailed. No review carries a verification marker and no page sets out how reviews are checked, which leaves the site silent on the standard the CMA’s fake reviews guidance has expected since 7 April 2025
  • Taxes, fees and port expenses are excluded from every fare on the UK pages and included on the American ones, so the two cannot be compared; and 59 of the 107 UK fares on 23 October 2026 showed no number of nights, which makes most of them incomparable with each other as well
  • Every deal card prints the same figure twice, once as a per-person fare and once as a nightly rate — “£1,890 pp” and “Price: £1,890 /night” — checked on three UK cards and three American ones
  • The cheapest fare on the page is attributed to an unnamed “3rd party pricing supplier”, so the one number a reader is most likely to act on has no identifiable source
  • Liability is capped at US$100 across the whole Tripadvisor group, the agreement is governed by Massachusetts law with exclusive jurisdiction in Massachusetts courts, and there is no postal address, no telephone number, no ombudsman and no ADR scheme anywhere on the site
  • Several of the site’s own documents are undated: the AI use policy carries no date or version, the terms of use say revision dates appear at the foot of the page and show none, and the footer there still reads “2022 The Independent Traveler, Inc.” while the rest of the site reads 1995 to 2026
  • The review total is given as 700,000, 650,000 and “Over 150,000+” on different pages of the same site, and monthly visitors as “more than five million” and “5.6M+” on one page

Common questions

Does Cruise Critic charge anything to use?

No. Its own pricing disclaimer states that “Cruise Critic is not a booking agent and does not charge any service fees to users of our site”, and there is no membership or registration charge for the reviews or the forums. The site is paid for by advertisers and by the travel agencies whose fares appear on its deal cards. Its parent, Tripadvisor, reported US$441.9 million of revenue in the quarter to 30 June 2026 but publishes no figure for Cruise Critic specifically.

Can you book a cruise through Cruise Critic?

Not with Cruise Critic itself. Its terms of use say it “is not a travel agency and does not provide or own transportation services or accommodations”, and clicking a fare hands you to a third party — on the UK pages on 23 October 2026 those included Onyx Cruises, Panache Cruises and Cruise118. That company takes the payment, holds the booking and handles any problem. The terms add that “Cruise Critic will have no liability” for a supplier’s acts or omissions.

Is a cruise booked this way financially protected?

That depends entirely on the seller, not on Cruise Critic. A package including a flight should come with an ATOL certificate naming the ATOL holder; the CAA describes ATOL as “financial protection when you book a package trip that includes a flight” and says plainly that “It is not travel insurance”. A cruise bought without a flight falls outside ATOL, and protection then comes from the seller’s own arrangements under the Package Travel Regulations 2018, usually an ABTA bond. Ask the company you are buying from and get the answer in writing.

How is a Cruise Critic rating worked out?

The site does not say, which is the central gap on it. A line or ship is given a single score out of five with a five-band distribution beneath it, and separately an “Editor Rating” out of 5.0 with no explanation of how the two relate. Royal Caribbean’s 3.9 can be reproduced by scoring its 44,042 banded reviews five down to one and taking a plain average, which comes out at 3.925 — our calculation, not the site’s. On that reading nothing is weighted for recency, so a 2014 review counts as much as last month’s.

Are the reviews checked to make sure the person actually sailed?

Nothing on the site says so. Reviews show a sail date and the reviewer’s cruise count but carry no verification marker, and no page sets out whether a reviewer must have sailed or how a disputed review is handled. The site does prohibit AI-written reviews outright — “AI-generated reviews are strictly prohibited” — and says suspected AI content may be removed without notice, which is a real control, but it is not the same thing as verifying a booking.

Why do Cruise Critic fares look cheaper than the cruise line’s own prices?

Usually because of what has been left out. On the UK pages the disclaimer reads “Taxes, fees and port expenses not included”, a “fuel supplement may apply”, the fare assumes two people sharing a specific cabin type on specific dates, and the lowest figure comes from an unnamed “3rd party pricing supplier” refreshed nightly. The same template on the American site says those charges are included. Price the sailing on the seller’s own site before comparing it with anything.

Our verdict

Cruise Critic earns its place as the first stop rather than the last. Thirty years of forum history and 700,000-odd reviews of 1,100 ships cost nothing to read, the reviews carry the sail date and the reviewer’s cruise count, and the AI use policy and the ban on cruise lines approving copy before publication are commitments most travel sites do not make in writing. For narrowing 120 cruise lines down to three ships worth pricing, there is nothing else like it in Britain, and the 2.54 million ocean cruises CLIA counted in 2025 are a lot of people who could use it.

Treat the money side as a starting point and not a quote. The fares belong to agencies that pay to appear, the cheapest of them comes from a supplier the site does not name, taxes and port expenses are excluded on the UK pages and included on the American ones, and more than half the UK fares do not say how many nights they cover. Check the figure on the seller’s own site before believing it. And because Cruise Critic takes no payment, nothing here protects your holiday: that comes from the ATOL certificate on a fly-cruise or the seller’s ABTA arrangements on a cruise-only booking, so the name on the deal card is the one worth checking first.
