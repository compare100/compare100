Currys Gaming and Accessories: The Credit, the Cover and the Carve-Outs

Rates and terms checked 23 October 2026 · Gaming and Accessories · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Currys will split a gaming order of £99 or more over 12, 24 or 36 months with no interest at all, or stretch it to 48 months at 9.9%, and it will hold the parcel at any of its 300-plus stores for nothing. Credit is no longer a sideline there: it ran through 23.7% of everything the company sold in the UK and Ireland last year, £1.2bn of sales, up 10% on the year before. Figures checked on 23 October 2026 against Currys' own service pages and its audited results for the year ended 2 May 2026.

The range behind that is wide. The gaming hub lists Console Gaming, PC Gaming, Gaming monitors, Gaming Accessories, Handheld Gaming, Gaming chairs, Components & Upgrades, Simulation gaming, Virtual Reality, Console games and Digital Downloads, with PS5, Xbox Series X, Nintendo Switch 2, Meta Quest and gaming laptops pinned at the top. Around 1,000 repair experts sit behind it, fixing more than a million products a year with a 12-month parts and labour guarantee on the repair itself, and any store will take old tech for recycling free of charge even if it was bought somewhere else. Recurring services revenue reached £873m last year, up 7%.

One thing is worth knowing before you click through. currys.co.uk does not send prices to anything except a browser. Fetched on 23 October 2026, the gaming landing page returned the full navigation, every category and every product name — and no price of any kind. In their place sat unrendered template tokens: $PRICE_10262507$ and $OFFER_10262507$. The delivery and collection page would not open at all across four attempts on the same day. So what follows is about the things Currys does publish in plain text — the credit, the cover, the repair promise and the returns — rather than about what a console costs on the morning you read this.

Spreading the cost stops dead at £99

Currys flexpay is a running credit account, not a point-of-sale loan, and the retailer is clear about its own role in it: “Currys Group Limited is a credit broker and offers the flexpay account under exclusive arrangement” with “the lender Creation Consumer Finance Ltd.” The standard rate is “29.9% Representative APR (variable)”, built on a 29.9% interest rate (variable) and an assumed credit limit of £1,200, with the usual rider that “Credit, offers and APR are subject to status and determined by the lender” and that applicants must be 18 or over.

Three promotional shapes sit on top of that. Interest free means “Pay in monthly instalments, interest free, over terms of 12, 24 or 36 months.” Pay monthly, low interest means “Spread the cost of your tech over 48 months, with a lower purchase interest rate of 9.9%” — twenty percentage points below the standard rate, which is a real saving rather than a presentational one. Buy now pay later runs for 6, 9 or 12 months and reverts: “Pay full balance by the end of the BNPL promotion period to avoid interest.” Miss a payment on any of them and “Late fees/charges may apply to missed payments and impact your credit file.”

The sentence that matters most on a gaming page is the quietest one. “Pay in monthly instalments for purchases £99 and over”, and separately, “paying with credit is available only on orders over £99.” A console, a gaming monitor, a gaming PC or a mid-range headset clears that bar. A controller, a keyboard, a mouse, a webcam, a boxed game or a capture dongle very often does not, and those are the purchases a gaming customer makes between consoles. There is a second restriction worth pairing with it: on the gift card terms, “Gift cards cannot be used towards Currys flexpay purchases when you purchase online”, so birthday money and a credit plan cannot be combined in the same online basket.

The scale of all this is in the audited numbers rather than the marketing. Flexpay sales reached £1.2bn in the year to 2 May 2026, “+10% YoY, with adoption rising +180bps to 23.7% of UK&I sales”, against UK and Ireland revenue of £5,438m. Twenty-three point seven per cent of £5,438m is £1,289m, so the two published figures sit together to within the rounding — that arithmetic is this page's, not the company's. Omnichannel sales reached 33% of UK and Ireland revenue, group adjusted profit before tax was £191m and the statutory figure £153m. A company incorporated in 1952 as a finance house, which then spent seventy years selling cameras and washing machines, now takes close to a quarter of its British sales on its own credit again.

The lender publishes its own complaints figures, and they repay reading

Creation Consumer Finance Limited puts its half-yearly complaints return on its own website, which most lenders do not, and the return covering 1 January to 30 June 2026 is specific. On fixed-term agreements it opened 8,629 complaints and closed 4,768 in the same six months — a gap of 3,861, which means the book of open cases grew over the period rather than shrank. The rate was 2.8492 complaints per 1,000 accounts. The headline cause is recorded as “Product Disclosure Information”.

The speed columns need reading in the regulator's own terms rather than at a glance. The return reports 7.38% closed within three days and 77.81% closed after three days but within eight weeks. Those two are separate buckets, not a running total, so the proportion resolved inside the eight-week deadline is 7.38 plus 77.81, or 85.19% — which also means a little under 15 in every 100 cases ran past it. Of the cases Creation closed, 49.52% were upheld in the customer's favour.

An uphold rate near half is not in itself an accusation; a complaint only reaches the figures because something had already gone wrong, and a firm that upholds readily is behaving better than one that does not. The pairing worth noticing is between that “Product Disclosure Information” heading and the thing a gaming customer is being asked to absorb at the checkout — a 29.9% running account, a 9.9% alternative, three promotional structures and a £99 floor, all decided in the few seconds before a console is paid for. Read the credit agreement on a screen bigger than a phone, and keep it.

Breakdown cover costs more on a controller than on a television

Instant Replacement is the cheap plan, sold in store only, for “most products between £20 and £150 which requires a one-off upfront payment”. It runs “for a total of 3 years, upon which your plan will expire”, covers breakdown “due to an electrical or mechanical fault”, excludes accidental and cosmetic damage, and can be bought on a product up to 12 months old. It can be paid in full or “over 10 instalments — ask in store for details”.

The price table is the interesting part, because Currys sorts products into three groups and gaming is not in the cheap one. Group 1 is televisions, monitors, printers and kitchen and household appliances. Group 2 is floorcare, gaming, headphones and electric transport. Group 3 is audio, home and outdoors, beauty, smart tech and gadgets. Across the six price bands, Group 2 is dearer than Group 1 every single time: £13 against £10 on a £20–£40 item, £17 against £13 at £41–£60, £24 against £17 at £61–£80, £30 against £22 at £81–£100, £35 against £27 at £101–£120 and £40 against £31 at £121–£150.

Set against the item it protects, the top of that ladder is steep. Forty pounds on a product at the bottom of the £121–£150 band is 33% of the purchase price; at the top of it, 26.7%. At the other end, £13 on a £20 accessory is 65% of what the accessory cost. Spread over the three years the plan runs, £40 is £13.33 a year to insure a controller against electrical failure. Currys does not publish the proportion of claims it pays or the average value of a replacement, and no regulator publishes an acceptance rate for retail breakdown plans of this kind, so there is no outside figure to measure that price against. The honest summary is that the premium is published, the payout record is not, and the gaming band is the dearest of the three.

Care & Repair, and the two words in its small print a gamer should find first

Care & Repair is the bigger plan, sold as a 2, 3 or 5-year upfront package or by monthly direct debit, priced on product category, price band and the length of the manufacturer's guarantee. Currys gives a worked example: a television priced £151–£175 with a one-year manufacturer guarantee costs £35 for three years upfront, £45 for five, or £1.50 a month. It covers parts, labour and call-outs, “if no fault is found you won't be charged”, and the stated target is to “repair products within 7 calendar days” — past which “you may request a replacement Product”. After two repairs, “if your tech has already had two repairs and it breaks down again, you can ask for a Currys gift card”. Televisions, computers and peripherals go to the company's repair centre in Newark. Upfront plans carry a 45-day cooling-off period with a full refund if unused; monthly plans carry 14 days.

Two sentences in it deserve attention before a gaming customer signs. The first is the status of the thing being sold: “Care & Repair is not an insurance policy and is an extended warranty plan only.” That wording is Currys' own, and it matters, because a service contract is not a general insurance contract and does not come with the complaints and compensation architecture a buyer might assume sits behind the word cover. The second is the pricing clause: “Care & Repair has fixed prices and we will therefore not price match.” The seven-day price promise described below applies to the console; it does not apply to the plan sold alongside it.

The third point is the one that sends a gamer back to the product page. The plan's extra benefits — the next-day replacement delivery, the 24/7 tech support, the built-in battery support and the annual valet — are not uniform, and the list of products shut out of them names “Monitors, eReaders, Networking, Printers, Scanners, Virtual Reality, Games Consoles”. Virtual Reality and Games Consoles. The two product lines a gaming buyer is most likely to be protecting are the two carved out of the extras, and the plan's own page says so without drawing attention to it. The core repair promise still applies; the add-ons that make the plan feel generous largely do not. Ask in store which benefits attach to the specific box before paying.

Seven days to claim the price match, and a test you can run on the sale price

The price promise is short and generous in outline. “We'll price match against any other UK retailer, in-store or online”, before or after purchase: “If you see the same item for less somewhere else, we'll refund you the difference”, and “We'll even price match 7 days after your purchase!” Currys Business runs a parallel “7 day price-match promise”. What the public page does not do is say which retailers count, which products are out, or how a claim is made; it says only “Exclusions and conditions apply. Claim within 7 days.” On a category where a marketplace seller and a high-street chain can both be the cheapest on a given Tuesday, the definition of an eligible competitor is the whole of the promise, and it is the part not published where the promise is advertised.

There is a free way for a reader to test the other half of the pricing — the struck-through comparison. The Chartered Trading Standards Institute's Guidance for Traders on Pricing Practices, reviewed and updated in October 2025 with a further amendment in November 2025, sets out what a defensible “was / now” claim looks like: the comparison should run for “a period that is the same or shorter than the period during which the higher price was offered”, and it asks “How recently was the higher price offered, compared to when the price comparison is being made?” Its worked examples treat a higher price charged “less than two months ago” as capable of giving a genuine indication of value, and prices “charged many months ago” as unlikely to. On recommended retail prices it is blunter still: “An RRP must not be false; it must not be created purely in order to present the appearance of a discount”, and “You should not recommend your own RRP.”

None of that is a finding about Currys, and it should not be read as one. It is a test a shopper can run without leaving the sofa: note the struck-through price on a gaming monitor today, check whether the same page shows the same comparison in a fortnight, and decide for yourself whether the higher figure was a price the shop actually charged for as long as the sale has run. The guidance exists precisely so that question has a published answer rather than an argument.

What the law calls a wireless controller, and where the hazard actually sits

Almost everything in the accessories half of this range is regulated radio equipment, and the definition is narrower and plainer than the phrase suggests. Regulation 2 of the Radio Equipment Regulations 2017 defines radio equipment as “an electrical or electronic product, which intentionally emits and/or receives radio waves” for “the purpose of radio communication and/or radiodetermination”, or one “which must be completed with an accessory, such as antenna, so as to intentionally emit and/or receive radio waves” for the same purpose. Radio waves are “electromagnetic waves of frequencies lower than 3,000 GHz, propagated in space without artificial guide”. A wireless controller, a Bluetooth headset, a wireless mouse and a VR headset all fall squarely inside that; anything mains-powered sits additionally under the Electrical Equipment (Safety) Regulations 2016.

Currys keeps its own product safety page, listing corrective action programmes and recalls with dates and model numbers, which is more than many retailers of its size publish at all. There were 28 entries on it on 23 October 2026, and the striking thing for anyone shopping the gaming range is what is absent: not one of them is a console, a controller, a headset or a gaming monitor. What is there instead is power. The Anker power banks A1681, A1689 and A1647 were recalled in July 2025 because “the lithium-ion batteries can overheat whilst in use”. Belkin's Power Bank 20K (BPB002) and Auto-Tracking Stand Pro (MMA008) followed in November 2025 over lithium-ion cells that “overheat”. The BenQ GV31 portable mini projector was recalled in June 2026 because its battery “may overheat and pose a fire risk”, and the Nutribullet Go blender in March 2025 over “isolated batteries that could overheat during charging”. In April 2026 Currys listed 44 separate Panasonic, JVC and Toshiba television models made between November 2024 and November 2025, every one of them over “the fuse in certain power cables not complying with the relevant safety standards”.

That pattern is the practical advice this section exists to give. A recall register with an empty gaming column is a good sign about the consoles, and it says nothing at all about the unbranded charger, the third-party dock or the cheap power bank bought to keep a handheld alive on a train. Buy the power supply from somebody who will be around to recall it, and keep the receipt — Currys' own guidance is to “always keep a hold of your sale receipt”, because that is how it reaches an owner if a programme is announced.

Consoles are growing, the headsets are not, and Currys is selling both

The trade body Ukie values the UK consumer games market at £8.76 billion for 2025, up 7.4% on £8.158 billion. Hardware was £2,172m of that, up 3%, and inside hardware the lines pull hard in opposite directions: consoles rose 12% to £811m and gaming PCs rose 11% to £790m, while virtual reality fell 26% to £151m from £201m. The fastest-growing line in the whole report is not a product at all — game culture, meaning merchandise, events, books and the rest, grew 42% to £566 million.

Set that beside what Currys puts in front of a shopper and the shape of the risk is clear. Virtual Reality is one of the eleven categories on the gaming hub and Meta Quest is pinned as a top category, in a segment that lost a quarter of its value in a year and now represents under 7% of all games hardware sold in Britain. There is nothing wrong with stocking a shrinking category — somebody has to — but it is the same category Currys has carved out of the Care & Repair extras, so the buyer is being sold the hardware in a declining line and offered a thinner support package on it than a dishwasher gets. A headset bought now will be supported by the manufacturer's guarantee and the statutory rights, and by rather less than the plan leaflet implies.

The console figures point the other way, and they are the better reason to shop here. A 12% rise in console sales on a market growing 7.4% overall is a line with momentum, and it is the line Currys is strongest in: PS5, Xbox Series X, Nintendo Switch 2 and handhelds including the ASUS ROG Ally X, MSI Claw 8 and Lenovo Legion Go all sit on the front of the gaming hub.

Sending it back, and getting rid of the old one

The returns position is better online than in store, which is the opposite of what most shoppers expect. An online purchase can be sent back “within 30 days — even if you've opened it for inspection”. An in-store purchase also has 30 days, but the item has to be unopened, so a game or a headset opened on the kitchen table after a Saturday trip to the retail park is in a weaker position than the identical item ordered from the sofa. Thirty days is more than twice the fourteen the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 require of a distance seller, so the extra sixteen days are a concession rather than a right. Some items “can't be returned if their seal is broken”, with hygiene given as the reason unless the goods are faulty, and the page points to each product's own Product Information for the exceptions. Faulty goods can be exchanged or refunded “within 30 days from when you bought it or had it delivered”, with damage, neglect, misuse and wear and tear excluded. The page does not say who pays return postage, and it gives no refund timescale.

On disposal Currys is strong and specific. “You can recycle all your tech for free at any of our stores — even if you didn't buy it from us”, items are checked and resold or donated to charity where they still work, and the company publishes its producer registrations in full: WEEE numbers WEE/DE0065TW for Currys Group Ltd and WEE/FD0095VY for Currys Retail Ltd, and battery producer registrations BPRN00339 and BPRN00524. Publishing those numbers is unusual and it is a genuine mark in the company's favour; a shopper who wants to check a retailer is inside the producer compliance regime can do it in one search. A million tonnes of tech have gone through it since 2010.

Two caveats. Free is the in-store position: collecting a large old appliance at the point of delivery is priced separately, at £25 for a washing machine, tumble dryer, dishwasher, cooker, oven, hob or fridge freezer and £30 for an American-style fridge freezer, with the recycling voucher scheme carrying a “Minimum £25 spend to redeem voucher” and a GB-only condition. And the gift card page, which is where a Care & Repair payout lands if a product cannot be fixed, is dense with expiry dates: a standard card runs 24 months, a Care & Repair eGift card 24 months, a No Fix Guarantee card just 3 months, and a loyalty card 3 months. That same page still carries a block of Home Electronics terms headed “Until December 31st 2018”, nearly eight years after that date passed — a small thing, but the kind of small thing that makes a reader wonder what else on the page has not been read recently.

Where it wins

  • Collection is free at any of 300-plus stores in the UK and Ireland, so there is a £0 route into the range whatever home delivery turns out to cost.
  • Interest-free credit over 12, 24 or 36 months, and a 9.9% purchase rate over 48 months against a 29.9% standard rate — twenty percentage points of difference on a long plan.
  • Thirty days to return an online purchase “even if you've opened it for inspection”, against the fourteen a distance seller has to give.
  • Around 1,000 repair experts, more than a million products repaired a year, and a 12-month parts and labour guarantee on the repair itself.
  • Free in-store recycling of any tech whether or not it was bought there, with WEEE and battery producer registration numbers published openly.
  • Its own product safety register is public, dated and model-specific, with 28 corrective action programmes and recalls listed on 23 October 2026.
  • The credit page names the lender, the broker and the APR in plain words instead of burying them, and the lender publishes its own complaints return.

Where it falls short

  • No price on the site can be read without a browser. The gaming landing page returned unrendered tokens such as $PRICE_10262507$ and $OFFER_10262507$ where every price should be, so nothing here can be compared before clicking through.
  • The delivery and collection page would not load on four separate attempts on 23 October 2026, so Currys' own home delivery charge on a small gaming order could not be read or quoted at all.
  • Credit is closed off below £99. Controllers, keyboards, mice, headsets and boxed games mostly sit under that line, which is exactly where a gaming customer spends between consoles.
  • Instant Replacement charges more for gaming than for any other product group in every one of its six price bands — £40 against a television's £31 at the top — and Currys publishes no claims acceptance rate or average payout to set that against.
  • Care & Repair's extra benefits exclude “Virtual Reality, Games Consoles” outright, which is the heart of what a gaming buyer would take the plan for, and the plan is “not an insurance policy and is an extended warranty plan only”.
  • Creation Consumer Finance, the flexpay lender, upheld 49.52% of the complaints it closed in the first half of 2026 and resolved 85.19% inside eight weeks, leaving close to 15% running past the deadline; it opened 8,629 fixed-term complaints and closed 4,768 in the same period.
  • The price promise is advertised with “Exclusions and conditions apply” and the exclusions are not set out on the page — so the one thing a shopper needs in order to use it is the one thing not published beside it.
  • The gift card page still carries a section of Home Electronics terms headed “Until December 31st 2018”, and gift cards cannot be put towards a flexpay purchase online.

Common questions

Can I spread the cost of a console at Currys?

Yes, on orders of £99 and over. Currys flexpay is a running credit account provided by Creation Consumer Finance Ltd, with Currys Group Limited acting as credit broker. The standard rate is 29.9% APR representative (variable) on an assumed £1,200 limit, but promotional terms run interest free over 12, 24 or 36 months, or at a 9.9% purchase rate over 48 months, with buy now pay later usually offered over 6, 9 or 12 months. Below £99 the credit option is not available at all.

Does Currys charge for delivery on a gaming order?

Its own delivery and collection page would not open on 23 October 2026 across four attempts, so the small-item charge could not be read first-hand and is not quoted here. What Currys does state elsewhere is that you “can order online and pick up your tech for FREE” at its stores, of which it counts more than 300 across the UK and Ireland. Large appliances are priced separately, from £20 for all-day standard delivery and from £30 next day, but those bands do not apply to a controller or a console. Check the charge in the basket before paying.

Is Care & Repair worth buying on a games console?

Read the exclusion list first. Care & Repair's headline extras — next-day replacement delivery, 24/7 tech support, built-in battery support and the annual valet — name “Monitors, eReaders, Networking, Printers, Scanners, Virtual Reality, Games Consoles” among the products they do not cover. The core repair promise still applies, with a target of seven calendar days and a gift card if the same product fails after two repairs, but it is an extended warranty rather than insurance and Currys says so in terms. For a cheaper item between £20 and £150 the alternative is Instant Replacement, which costs £13 to £40 for three years in the gaming band.

How long do I have to return something I bought online?

Thirty days, and Currys says you can send it back “even if you've opened it for inspection”. That is more than double the fourteen days the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013 oblige a distance seller to offer, so sixteen of those days are a concession the retailer grants rather than a statutory right. A purchase made in a store also gets 30 days but has to come back unopened. Sealed items can be excluded on hygiene grounds, and the returns page gives no refund timescale and does not say who pays the postage.

Will Currys match a cheaper price I find elsewhere?

It says it will match any other UK retailer, in store or online, and that a claim can be made up to seven days after purchase, with the difference refunded. The page adds “Exclusions and conditions apply” without setting out what they are, so ask which sellers count before relying on it. One exclusion is stated plainly: “Care & Repair has fixed prices and we will therefore not price match”, so the promise covers the console and not the protection plan sold with it.

Can I recycle an old console at Currys?

Yes, free of charge, at any store and whether or not the item came from Currys — the company says it can “recycle almost any tech” and that working items may be resold or donated to charity. Its producer registrations are published: WEEE numbers WEE/DE0065TW and WEE/FD0095VY, and battery producer registrations BPRN00339 and BPRN00524. Collecting a large old appliance from your home at the point of delivery is charged separately, at £25 for most and £30 for an American-style fridge freezer.

Our verdict

Currys is the most complete place in Britain to buy a console in person: 300-plus stores, free collection, a thousand repair engineers, a million repairs a year and free recycling of whatever the new machine replaces. It is also, increasingly, a credit business — £1.2bn of flexpay sales and 23.7% of everything it sold in the UK and Ireland last year — and the credit is genuinely good value if you clear the £99 floor and take the interest-free or 9.9% terms rather than letting a balance sit at 29.9%. Where the offer thins is on the attachments. Instant Replacement prices gaming above every other category it sells, Care & Repair strips games consoles and virtual reality out of its extras, and the price promise advertises exclusions it does not print. Buy the hardware here for the collection network and the repair operation, take the interest-free credit if the basket clears £99, and read the plan leaflet in the shop before adding cover to a console.
