DFDS Ferry Tickets: Fares, Fees and the Contract Behind Them

Rates and terms checked 26 October 2026 · Ferry Tickets · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

DFDS sells a Dover–Calais crossing from £114 for a car and up to five passengers, one way, with the emissions charge already inside the price. The ship takes 1 hour 40 minutes and there are up to 30 of them a day. Dunkirk costs the same and runs up to 22 times a day over two hours. Figures checked 26 October 2026 against DFDS's own route pages and its January 2025 booking conditions.

The company behind that ticket is older than almost anything else you can buy a seat on. DFDS A/S has been running ships since 1866, is listed in Copenhagen, turned over DKK 30,947m in 2025 and ended the year with 68 ferries, around 30 routes and offices in more than 20 countries. It carried 5,247,000 passengers that year, and roughly three quarters of them — 3,862,000 — crossed the Channel.

One thing is worth knowing before you book rather than after. The cheapest Dover fare, the one called Economy, carries a 100% cancellation charge at any time, so the money is gone from the moment you pay it. The Flexi fare on the same route cancels free up to 48 hours before sailing. Which of the two you pick matters more than anything else on the booking page.

Seventy-seven pence in every pound comes from lorries

The single most useful thing to understand about a DFDS ticket is that you are a minority customer on a freight business, and that this is mostly to your advantage. The audited 2025 accounts split the Ferry Division's DKK 16,999m of revenue into DKK 13,104m from freight and DKK 3,895m from passengers. That is 77.1% trailers and 22.9% people. On the Channel in 2025 the ships moved 16,910,000 lane metres of freight alongside those 3,862,000 passengers.

Frequency is the dividend. Lorries need sailings through the night and at awkward hours, and the economics of running a ship that often are carried by the freight deck rather than by your car. Up to 30 Calais departures and 22 Dunkirk departures a day is a timetable no passenger-only operator on that stretch of water could justify, and it is why a missed sailing at Dover is an inconvenience rather than a lost day.

The same accounts show why 2025 was a hard year. Group revenue rose 4.0% to DKK 30,947m, but EBIT fell from DKK 1,506m to DKK 520m and the group recorded a loss for the period of DKK 425m against a DKK 541m profit in 2024. The Logistics Division, which is now almost as large as the ferries at DKK 15,723m of revenue, lost DKK 30m. Passenger revenue across the group fell 17.5% from DKK 4,720m, and headline passenger numbers dropped 22.2% from 6,747,000. Almost none of that decline is on the Channel, where the count went up from 3,824,000; it sits on the Mediterranean and Strait of Gibraltar networks, where the half-year to June 2026 shows passengers down 24.8% again.

The half-year figures also carry a cost that no marketing page mentions and that shows how long shipping disputes run: a one-off charge of DKK 53m booked against the Channel in the second quarter of 2026, arising from a French court ruling about the chartering of two Channel ferries in 2015.

Three routes, six different opening prices

Every DFDS route page advertises a lead-in fare twice, once in the banner and once inside the frequently asked questions, and on 26 October 2026 the two did not agree on any of the three Channel routes.

Dover–Calais shows “From £114 per car + up to 5 people, one way* (INCLUDING ETS)” at the top of the page and repeats £114 in the route table. Its own FAQ, further down the same page, says: “Travel with DFDS by ferry from Dover to Calais from £99 for a car plus 5 passengers one way.” Dover–Dunkirk does exactly the same thing with exactly the same two numbers. Newhaven–Dieppe reverses it — the banner says “From £73 per car + 4 people one way*” while its FAQ says crossings “currently start from £117” on the same basis, and the Dover–Dunkirk page quotes Newhaven–Dieppe at £117 as well.

The likeliest explanation is innocent and it is still a problem. The banner prices carry the words “including ETS” and the FAQ prices do not, which suggests the FAQ copy was written before the emissions charge was folded in and has been maintained on a slower cycle. A shopper reading the FAQ expects to pay £99 and will be quoted £114. The gap is 15%.

The same page has a second disagreement with itself: the Newhaven route is described as running “up to 6 daily sailings” in one section and as departing “twice daily” in another. One ship, the Côte d'Albâtre, works that route in about four hours, or five on certain overnight crossings.

For completeness, the other published lead-in prices: short breaks to France from £79 per car and up to five people; Newcastle to Amsterdam from £74 per person based on four sharing a standard four-berth inside cabin with a car included, on a 16 hour 45 minute crossing leaving at 17:00 daily with check-in closing at 16:15. That route's FAQ says £69.

The cheapest Dover fare cannot be cancelled at all

DFDS publishes its cancellation scale in full in the January 2025 Passenger Booking Terms and Conditions, which is more than many operators manage, and the scale is unusually blunt. Section 14 sets a 100% cancellation charge on an Eastern Channel Economy fare at any time — not within 48 hours, not within a week, but from the moment the booking is made. The Western Channel Transportation fare is the same, and so are Long Sea transport-only tickets and the Mini and City Cruise breaks.

Flexi is the fare that behaves the way most people assume a ferry ticket behaves. On the Eastern Channel it cancels free if you give more than 48 hours notice and costs 100% inside that window. The Channel Islands fares sit between the two: Economy refunds half if you cancel more than 48 hours out and nothing inside it, while Flexi is free to the same 48-hour mark.

Only the Long Sea Holiday Collections and groups of 20 or more get a graduated ladder, and it starts early: 25% at 57 days or more, 50% from 56 to 36 days, 70% from 35 to 22 days, 90% from 21 to 11 days and 100% at ten days or fewer. Group bookings on the Western Channel and Long Sea take a 25% deposit with the balance due at least 56 days before departure.

Changing a booking is cheaper than losing it but it is not free. The amendment fee is £20 or €25 per change on the Eastern Channel and on Long Sea transport-only and cruise breaks, £10 on the Western Channel and £10 or €15 on the Channel Islands routes and for name changes. On top of that you pay the difference between your original fare and the fare on the day you make the change, which on a Dover crossing in August is where the real money goes. Booking by telephone or in person attracts a service fee as well; the terms say the amount is on the website rather than printing it.

Two surcharges in the same document are worth knowing about because they are large. Smoking outside a designated area carries a minimum penalty of €200 or DKK 1,500, and bringing your own alcohol to drink on board costs the bar price for it plus a cleaning surcharge if you drink it in a cabin. The vehicle rules are specific too: up to three gas cylinders with a combined weight of no more than 47 kg, up to 1,000 cartridges or shells per vehicle in original packaging, and no petrol or diesel cans at all.

What the ETS line in your fare is actually paying for

DFDS prints “including ETS” beside its Dover prices and explains the acronym nowhere a shopper will find it. It is the European Union Emissions Trading System, which has covered maritime transport since 1 January 2024 for cargo and passenger ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above.

Two details decide how big that component is, and both work in a Dover passenger's favour at the moment. The scheme counts only 50% of the emissions on a voyage between an EU port and a port outside the EU, which is what a Dover–Calais crossing now is; a sailing between two EU ports counts at 100%. And the obligation is still phasing in. Operators surrendered allowances against 40% of their reported 2024 emissions, 70% of 2025 emissions, and must cover 100% from 2027. Methane and nitrous oxide join carbon dioxide in the scheme from 2026.

So the charge inside today's £114 reflects seventy per cent of half a crossing's emissions, and the scheme's own timetable raises both halves of that. Absorbing it into the headline price rather than adding it at checkout is the better practice of the two available, and worth crediting, but it does mean the fare will carry an increase that has nothing to do with fuel or demand.

The United Kingdom's own emissions scheme does not reach this crossing. Its expansion to maritime covers vessels of 5,000 gross tonnage and above “operating on domestic routes within UK waters and/or generating in-port emissions”, with the draft legislation laid in January 2026, offshore vessels added from January 2027 and a review in 2028 that will consider “potential inclusion of international voyages”. An international sailing from Dover sits outside it until that review says otherwise.

A Special Drawing Right is worth about £1.01, and nobody tells you

Liability at sea is set by the 2002 Athens Convention and published in Special Drawing Rights, the International Monetary Fund's basket unit. DFDS prints the full set in section 16 of its conditions, which is more than most carriers do, and then leaves the reader holding a unit with no price on it. Here is the conversion, using the IMF's own valuation of 23 October 2026, when one SDR was worth US$1.355380 against a sterling rate of 1.33735 dollars to the pound — so one SDR is about £1.01.

Death or injury to a passenger is capped at 400,000 SDR, which is roughly £405,000. A vehicle and the luggage inside it is 12,700 SDR, about £12,871, with a deductible of 330 SDR or about £334 taken off any vehicle claim. Cabin luggage is 2,250 SDR, about £2,280. Other luggage is 3,375 SDR, about £3,421, with a 149 SDR deductible of about £151 per passenger. Money and valuables are excluded altogether, which is the clause that matters if you keep a laptop in the boot.

Set the vehicle limit against the thing it covers. The Society of Motor Manufacturers and Traders puts the average age of a car on British roads at close to a decade, so for most family cars £12,871 is ample; for anything newer or larger it is not, and the gap belongs to your own motor policy rather than to the ferry company. A claim under the Convention has to be notified within two years of the incident.

Delay is a separate régime and a weaker one. Assimilated Regulation (EU) No 1177/2010 gives a passenger the right to refreshments, and where necessary accommodation capped at €80 a night for a maximum of three nights, once a departure runs more than 90 minutes late, and compensation of 25% or 50% of the fare for arrival delays. DFDS reproduces the €80 cap in its own section 15. Article 20 of the same regulation switches the compensation off where the delay is caused by weather endangering safe operation, which in the Dover Strait in January is the usual reason a sailing goes wrong.

A Danish carrier, an English contract and a Lincolnshire registered office

The conditions name the carrier as DFDS A/S at DFDS House, Sundkrogsgade 11, DK-2100 Copenhagen Ø, operating “a subsidiary or branch of DFDS A/S” on any given route. The contract is nevertheless governed by the laws of England and Wales, and section 21 lets a passenger sue either where DFDS has its principal place of business or in their own country of residence where DFDS has a place of business there. For a British buyer that is a better arrangement than the carrier's nationality would suggest, and it is the same choice Brittany's French carrier makes.

Companies House shows the British arm as DFDS Seaways plc, company 01554521, a public limited company registered at Nordic House, Western Access Road, Immingham Dock, North East Lincolnshire DN40 2LZ and still active. It was incorporated on 2 April 1981 and has changed its name six times since: Scandinavian Seaways Limited, Longship Holidays Limited, Scandinavian Seaways again, DFDS plc from 2 January 1996, DFDS Tor Line plc from 8 December 2003 and the present name from 31 August 2010. Accounts were last made up to 31 December 2025 and the next confirmation statement is due by 12 February 2027.

Its registered activity codes are the detail that confirms the shape of the business. The plc files under 52101 for warehousing and storage for water transport, 52220 for service activities incidental to water transportation and 70100 for head office activities. There is no sea passenger transport code on the register at all. That is a filing choice rather than a statement about the service, but it does say where the company thinks its centre of gravity is.

Financial protection follows the same logic. DFDS holds membership 5448 of the Association of Bonded Travel Organisers Trust, which protects package bookings. Buy a crossing on its own and no bond attaches to it, because a single travel service is not a package; there is no ATOL cover either, since no flight is involved.

Jersey has bought twenty years of this company

The newest part of the network is also the clearest sign of how a government assesses a ferry operator when it has to. On 31 December 2024 the Government of Jersey confirmed DFDS as the island's ferry operator on a contract that “will run for 20 years”, with services beginning on 28 March 2025 across Jersey to Poole, Jersey to Portsmouth and Jersey to St Malo.

What DFDS committed to is on the record: “greater frequency of ferries to the UK and France in peak periods”, “faster ferries to the UK”, “investing in three new ferries by 2032” and “two dedicated high-speed ferries for the summer”. The company has since agreed to buy the ro-pax ferry Stena Vinga, currently working the Jersey–Portsmouth route, for delivery in November 2026, and its half-year report notes Jersey passenger volumes running above 2025.

For a traveller the practical point is that the Channel Islands routes run on their own fare rules, which are gentler than Dover's. Economy there refunds 50% if you cancel more than 48 hours ahead, against nothing at all on an Eastern Channel Economy ticket, and the amendment fee is £10 or €15 rather than £20. A twenty-year lifeline obligation and a commercial short-sea route are not the same product even when the funnel is.

Borders, and the one place Dover is easier than everywhere else

The European Union's Entry/Exit System began on 12 October 2025 and changes what happens at the far end of a crossing. Travellers create a digital record with fingerprints and a photograph at dedicated booths, and that record is “valid for 3 years”. The 90 days in every 180 limit on time in the Schengen area applies as before, and ETIAS is expected to start from autumn 2026.

Where the check happens is the part that favours a DFDS Dover booking. UK government guidance updated on 10 April 2026 says that at Dover, Eurotunnel Le Shuttle and Eurostar “any EES checks will be completed at the border, before you leave the UK”, while passengers using other ferry ports “will be registered at the border on arrival in their destination country”. Sail from Newhaven or Newcastle and you queue for biometrics after a night at sea; sail from Dover and you have done it before boarding. That is a genuine operational advantage of the short crossings and nothing on the booking page points it out.

Where the paperwork runs out

Four gaps are worth naming, and none of them is unique to this company.

The complaints window is short. Section 17 requires a written complaint within two months of the journey, sent to uk.customercare@dfds.com with the booking reference in the subject line; DFDS undertakes to say within one month whether the complaint is upheld, rejected or still under review, and to give a final answer on passenger-rights complaints within two months. Damage to a vehicle or luggage has to be reported before or at disembarkation if it is visible and within 15 days if it is not. Two months is less time than the law allows you to go to court, and a claim under the Merchant Shipping (Passengers' Rights) Regulations 2013 must be brought within six months of the infringement.

There is no ombudsman. The 2013 regulations name the Maritime and Coastguard Agency as the designated enforcement body, and enforcement means prosecution, which pays the court rather than the passenger. The conditions offer ABTA or the EU online dispute platform for unresolved matters, neither of which binds the carrier the way the Financial Ombudsman Service binds a bank.

Nobody rates ferry operators. Defaqto rates insurance, the national tourist boards assess accommodation and Ofcom measures telecoms. No agency publishes a star band, an acceptance rate or an uphold rate for any shipping line, so a comparison between carriers has to be built out of timetables, published fares and the documents themselves rather than out of a score.

And two figures a shopper would want are simply absent. The telephone and in-person booking service fee is referred to in the terms but not quantified there. Nor is there a published fee for carrying a dog, although the conditions reserve the right to apply freight tariffs to animals.

Common questions

How much is a DFDS ferry from Dover to Calais?

DFDS advertises Dover–Calais from £114 one way for a standard car carrying up to five passengers, with the EU emissions charge included. Dover–Dunkirk is the same price over a two-hour crossing. Note that the FAQ on DFDS's own route page still quotes £99 for both, a figure that does not mention the emissions charge; the £114 banner price is the one repeated in the route table.

Can I cancel a DFDS ferry ticket and get a refund?

It depends entirely on the fare. An Eastern Channel Economy ticket carries a 100% cancellation charge at any time, so there is no refund however early you cancel. A Flexi ticket on the same route cancels free with more than 48 hours notice and 100% inside that window. Channel Islands Economy refunds 50% beyond 48 hours. Long Sea Holiday Collections use a ladder running from 25% at 57 or more days out to 100% inside ten days.

What does the ETS charge on a DFDS fare mean?

It is the EU Emissions Trading System, which has covered passenger and cargo ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above since 1 January 2024. Only 50% of emissions count on a voyage between an EU and a non-EU port, which is what a Dover crossing is, and the obligation is phasing in — 70% of 2025 emissions and 100% from 2027. DFDS includes the charge in the advertised fare rather than adding it at checkout.

What is DFDS liable for if my car is damaged on the ferry?

Under the Athens Convention limits in section 16 of its conditions, a vehicle and the luggage in it are covered to 12,700 SDR, about £12,871 at the IMF valuation of 23 October 2026, less a deductible of 330 SDR or roughly £334. Cabin luggage is capped at 2,250 SDR and other luggage at 3,375 SDR with a 149 SDR deductible. Money and valuables are excluded, and a claim must be notified within two years.

Is a DFDS ferry ticket financially protected if the company fails?

A package booking is: DFDS holds ABTOT membership 5448, which bonds package holidays. A crossing bought on its own is a single travel service rather than a package, so no bond, no insolvency protection and no repatriation promise attaches to it, and ATOL does not apply because no flight is involved.

Do I need to do EES biometrics before a DFDS crossing?

Yes, on a sailing into the Schengen area, but where it happens depends on the port. UK government guidance updated 10 April 2026 says Dover completes EES checks before you leave the UK, while other ferry ports register travellers on arrival in the destination country. The digital record lasts 3 years and the 90 days in every 180 limit still applies.

How long do I have to complain to DFDS?

Section 17 of the booking conditions requires a written complaint within two months of the journey, with DFDS replying inside one month and giving a final answer on passenger-rights matters within two months. Visible damage to a vehicle or luggage must be reported before or at disembarkation, and hidden damage within 15 days. Court proceedings under the Merchant Shipping (Passengers' Rights) Regulations 2013 must be started within six months.

Our verdict

For a car heading to northern France, this is the operator with the timetable — up to 30 Calais crossings a day at 1 hour 40 minutes, from £114 for a car and five people with the emissions charge already counted, and biometric checks finished before you leave Kent. That frequency exists because lorries pay for 77.1% of the Ferry Division's revenue, and the passenger rides on a freight network with DKK 31bn of group turnover behind it. The thing to get right is the fare type, not the operator: an Economy ticket is unrefundable from the moment you buy it and Flexi cancels free until 48 hours before sailing, so unless your dates are certain the gap between the two prices is cheap insurance. Read the January 2025 booking conditions before you pay, because the cancellation scale in section 14 is blunter than the booking page suggests.
