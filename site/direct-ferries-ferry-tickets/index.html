Direct Ferries Tickets: Fees, Fares and Whose Contract You Get

Rates and terms checked 27 October 2026 · Ferry Tickets · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Direct Ferries puts 314 ferry companies and Eurotunnel on one search screen, across 4,387 routes and around 930 ports, and its Dover–Calais page opens at £21. On that crossing alone it sells Irish Ferries, P&O Ferries and DFDS Seaways side by side, with sailings from 40 minutes. Figures checked 27 October 2026 against Direct Ferries' own pages, its booking conditions and Companies House.

The business has been doing this since 1999, which makes it older than most of the travel sites it competes with. Its own figures: more than 2.5 million customers, over 1.2 million crossings arranged a year, up to 1 million fares compared a day and 36 national sites. One practical thing it does better than most: nothing is charged to your card until the ferry company has confirmed the booking.

It owns no ships. Clause 2 of the booking conditions says Direct Ferries Limited “acts as an agent for the Operators” and that “you will be entering into a contract with the relevant ferry or train Operator”, and where the two sets of terms disagree the Operator Terms prevail. That decides who you ring when a sailing is cancelled, so it is worth knowing before you book rather than after.

Three carriers on one Dover results page

The product is breadth, and on the Channel the breadth is real. Dover–Calais is sold here across Irish Ferries, P&O Ferries and DFDS Seaways, which no single carrier's own website will do for you. The route page gives the usual crossing as 1 hour 35 minutes with the quickest sailing at 40 minutes, and prices starting at £21 against an average of £164.

Away from the Channel the gap between a reseller and a carrier widens considerably. Ferries to France are listed from 12 ferry companies; Greece is listed from 39, with up to 8,172 sailings a week and a cheapest crossing of £3 between Pserimos and Kalymnos. Nobody operating ships sells that range, and in the Aegean, where a dozen small operators run overlapping schedules, having them in one timetable is the whole point of using an agent at all.

The published averages per vehicle type are the other genuinely useful thing on these pages, because carriers rarely print them. On Dover–Calais: £67 for a foot passenger, £160 for one adult with a car, £76 with a motorcycle, £205 for a campervan, £210 for a cabin and £197 for a pet. Dover–Dunkirk is quoted from £22, Portsmouth–Caen from £18 and Newhaven–Dieppe from £30.

Note what the lead-in prices are not. The site says plainly that fares “are generally cheaper for foot passengers”, and neither the route page nor the France page gives a starting price for a car anywhere. The £160 car figure is an average, not a lead-in, so it is not the number to set against a carrier's own advertised from-price.

The fee that is never published, and the two that are

Direct Ferries is paid twice over: commission from the operator, and a charge to you. The charge is described on its own help pages as a service fee “calculated based on the total cost of your reservation”, varying “depending on your ticket type and the number of passengers”, and shown at the final checkout stage before you confirm. The booking conditions refer to a booking fee in two separate clauses. Neither the terms nor the help centre states a rate, a minimum or a maximum anywhere on the site.

That is the one number a shopper most needs in order to compare a reseller against the carrier, and it is the one number that only appears once the basket is full. A percentage fee also behaves differently from a flat one: it is modest on a £21 foot crossing and considerably less modest on a £210 cabin or a summer booking for a car and five people.

The administration charges, by contrast, are published in full and the gap between the channels is wide. An amendment made yourself through My Account costs £10 / €12 / $14 per booking. The same amendment asked for through the messenger or the contact form costs £20 on a booking under £100 and £35 on one above it. Cancelling costs from £10 / €12 / $14 online and from £20 / €25 / $27 offline. Changes made inside the first 24 hours escape the £10 online fee, which is a fair concession and worth using.

Three things then sit on top. The fare difference on the day you amend, which on a Dover crossing in August is where the real money goes. The ferry company's own cancellation terms, which the help pages confirm also affect the total. And card surcharges of 2% on corporate and commercial cards, and another 2% on consumer credit cards issued by banks outside the EEA. Roof boxes, bike racks and similar attachments carry an additional fee, and any deposit paid is not refundable.

The flexible products have to be bought at the moment of booking. Flexi fares and Cancellation Cover “can only be selected when you're making your booking and can't be added afterwards”, so a decision taken in thirty seconds at checkout cannot be revisited the following week when the plan starts to wobble.

Four of its own pages, four different route counts

Count the network from Direct Ferries' own copy and you get a different answer on every page. The home page says “from 4412 routes and 901 ports worldwide” at the top and “over 3000 ferry connections and more than 700 ports worldwide” further down. The about page says “4387 ferry routes across the globe”, “around 930 ferry ports” and “314 Ferry companies and the Eurotunnel”. The booking questions page says “almost 4,000 routes and over 270 ferry operators”. For reference, the senior lender on the company's 2023 refinancing described it in its own deal note as covering about 4,400 routes and more than 280 operators, which sits closest to the about page.

The route pages do the same thing at a smaller scale. The Dover–Calais page gives the average fare as £164 and the service as “24 daily sailings on average” in one place and “31 daily and 212 weekly sailings” in another. The France page gives the same route an average of £194.00 and 190 weekly sailings. That is an 18% difference in the price and twenty-two sailings a week between two pages of the same website.

Port counts drift too. The France page offers ferries “from 34 ports” and then states that “there are 21 ferry ports in France”. The Greece page says there are 100 ferry ports in Greece and that ferries from Greece go to 116. And the arithmetic on the same page does not hold: the cheapest crossing is given as £3 on the Pserimos–Kalymnos route while the average price for that identical route is given as £2.65, which is less than the cheapest.

The explanation is almost certainly dull. These are template figures pulled from live inventory on different cycles, and an average computed over a different window from the minimum beside it. It is still a problem, because the one claim the site makes about itself — that it is bigger than anyone else — is the claim its own pages cannot agree on, and a reader who checks two of them has no way to tell which is current.

Twice the fare is the whole of its liability

Clause 17 of the booking conditions limits Direct Ferries' liability “to a sum equivalent to twice the amount paid by you for a booking”. On the average Dover–Calais car fare of £160 that is £320. The cap does not touch liability for fraud, or for death or personal injury caused by negligence, which the law does not permit anyone to limit.

The clause beside it matters more. Direct Ferries is not liable for loss you suffer as a result of the operator's failure to perform the journey. Clause 15 adds that in events outside anyone's reasonable control you are “not entitled to a refund of the booking fee which you paid to us at the time of booking”. Put the three together and the position is consistent rather than sly: you have bought a search, a basket and a booking record, and the crossing itself is the carrier's promise.

That is also where your statutory rights sit. Assimilated Regulation (EU) No 1177/2010 gives a sea passenger care and compensation when a departure runs late, and those duties fall on the carrier, not on the website that sold the ticket. The Merchant Shipping (Passengers' Rights) Regulations 2013 name the Maritime and Coastguard Agency as the enforcement body, and enforcement there means prosecution rather than a payment to you. Nothing in that chain is Direct Ferries' doing, and none of it is explained on the booking page either.

Which conditions govern the voyage follows the same logic. The booking conditions state that in any conflict or inconsistency with the operator's terms, the operator's terms prevail. In practice you agree to a document you have read and a document you probably have not, and the second one wins.

Fourteen days to complain, and four separate refund clocks

Clause 18 requires a complaint “within 14 days of the completion of your booking”. That is tight. The carriers on this very route allow two months from the journey, and the 2013 regulations give a passenger six months to start court proceedings. Fourteen days from the completion of a booking can expire before a summer sailing has even taken place, and nothing on the page flags it.

Getting money back runs on four different timetables. A card refund is stated at 3 to 5 working days and a PayPal or iDEAL refund at up to 10 working days. A Cancel For Any Reason claim returns 80% of the ferry fare only, excluding the booking fee and the cost of the product itself, and must be requested at least 48 hours before sailing, with a limit of two uses per person per year. The last-minute version of that cover allows a cancellation up to 2 hours before the outbound departure and returns 80% of the ticket cost excluding its own fee, within 5 working days; a separate 48-hour version returns 70% including its own fee. A Best Price Promise claim is paid to the original card in approximately 10 to 14 business days, and the lower price you are matching against must include all mandatory taxes and charges while excluding optional extras.

Two operational details decide how painless any of that is. Cancellations cannot be requested by email — the help pages cite data protection — which leaves My Account or a contact form whose replies are “typically provided within 48 hours”. The telephone and messenger team works 08:00 to 20:00 daily with an automated assistant outside those hours. Anyone who cannot use the website is therefore pushed towards the channel that costs £20 to £35 rather than £10.

No bond, and the insurance the terms tell you to buy instead

Search the booking conditions for ABTA, ABTOT or ATOL and you find nothing at all. What you find instead is a recommendation: Direct Ferries “would recommend that you take out travel insurance which includes cover for End Supplier Failure”. That is an unusually candid sentence for a travel retailer, and it is the correct advice. A ferry crossing bought on its own is a single travel service rather than a package, so the bonding regime does not reach it, and ATOL only applies where a flight is involved.

It does mean the protection a reader might assume comes with booking through a large intermediary is not there. If a carrier stops sailing, the agent that took your money has told you in advance to insure against exactly that, and the £320 ceiling in clause 17 is what remains on an average car fare. The holiday-selling arm that might once have carried a bond is gone: Direct Ferries Holidays Ltd, company 06216807, incorporated 18 April 2007, filed its last accounts to 31 December 2015 as a non-trading company and was dissolved on 9 January 2018.

One structural protection does work in your favour and deserves saying. The booking conditions are governed by the laws of England and Wales with the English courts taking jurisdiction, and a customer resident in Scotland or Northern Ireland may choose their own courts instead. For a British buyer dealing with an Ipswich company that is a better position than the Irish contracting entity on the same terms might suggest.

Ipswich, Dublin, and a bidco called Elmo

Two companies can be on the other side of your booking and the terms name both. The British one is Direct Ferries Limited, company 03865405, incorporated on 26 October 1999 and registered at Units 2 & 3, Three Rivers Business Centre, Felixstowe Road, Foxhall, Ipswich IP10 0BF. The Irish one is Direct Ferries (RoI) Limited, registration 721008, at the Work Hub, 77 Camden Street, Dublin D02 XE80. Which of the two you contract with is set by the site you book on, and the liability and complaints clauses are written to cover either.

The register also shows a company that has recently changed hands. Until last year the controlling party was Direct Ferries (Holdings) Ltd, registered as such since 6 April 2016. On 25 September 2025 that entry ceased and Elmo Bidco Limited was recorded in its place, holding 75% or more of the shares and voting rights with the right to appoint and remove directors. The last full accounts were made up to 31 December 2024 and filed on 29 September 2025, and the latest confirmation statement is dated 26 October 2025. The register inspection address moved from Exeter to Bristol on 13 May 2026.

Behind that is a financing the company itself does not discuss. Investec's own deal record, dated 26 July 2023, describes providing senior debt in a club of three lenders to support a partnership investment by Intermediate Capital Group in what it calls “the leading ferry focused Online Travel Agency in Europe”, and the adviser on the other side records the seller as the private equity firm Livingbridge. The 2023 transaction and the 2025 change on the register are two separate filings and nothing published ties them together, so both are given here as they stand.

One small oddity in the filing is worth a line. The company's registered activity codes are 50200 for sea and coastal freight water transport and 79909 for other reservation service activities. The second describes the business exactly. The first describes carrying cargo by sea, which is not what this company does at all.

Three questions on its own help page have no answer

The making-changes page carries its questions as headings with the answers underneath, and three of them are published with the answer missing. One is “Do you charge for ferry booking cancellations?” Another is “FAQ - Do you charge for ferry booking amendments?”, where the answer field simply repeats the question. The third is the one that matters most: “Where can I find more information on my rights to refunds and compensation in the event of ferry cancellations and delays?”

Both fee questions are answered properly elsewhere on the site, so this is a maintenance fault rather than concealment. The passenger-rights question is different, because the answer does not exist anywhere else on the site either. A reader whose sailing has just been cancelled is sent to a heading with nothing under it, on the only page that promises to tell them what they are owed.

Two other absences are worth naming because they are not faults of this company so much as facts about the trade. There is no ratings body anywhere that scores a ferry retailer: the Financial Conduct Authority does not regulate ticket agency, Defaqto does not rate it, the tourist boards assess beds rather than bookings, and no regulator publishes an uphold rate for any of these sites. And because the company files small-company accounts, no revenue, booking value or margin figure exists in public for it at all — the only scale numbers available are the ones it prints about itself, which is why the four different route counts above are a problem rather than a curiosity.

Finally, a detail that is genuinely helpful and easy to miss. Timetables for most routes only appear three to six months before departure, and arrival at the port is required “between 30 minutes and 2 hours” before sailing depending on the operator, with the actual deadline given in the confirmation email rather than at the point of sale. Some bookings are confirmed provisionally and firmed up within 24 to 48 hours, and the card is not charged until the operator has confirmed.

Common questions

Is it cheaper to book with Direct Ferries or direct with the ferry company?

On a route with one operator it is hard to be cheaper than the carrier, because Direct Ferries adds a service fee “calculated based on the total cost of your reservation” that no carrier charges. On a route with several operators, or a journey that needs two of them, the comparison is the value — Dover–Calais alone is sold here across three carriers from £21. The fee is only shown at the final checkout step, so price the same crossing on the carrier's own site before you confirm.

What does Direct Ferries charge to change or cancel a booking?

Through My Account the fee is £10 / €12 / $14 per booking to amend and from £10 / €12 / $14 to cancel. Through the messenger or contact form an amendment is £20 on bookings under £100 and £35 above that, and a cancellation starts at £20 / €25 / $27. Changes made in the first 24 hours escape the £10 online fee. The ferry operator's own charges and any increase in the fare apply on top.

Who is responsible if my sailing is cancelled — Direct Ferries or the operator?

The operator. Clause 2 of the booking conditions says Direct Ferries acts “as an agent for the Operators” and that you contract with the carrier, and clause 17 states it is not liable for loss caused by the operator's failure to perform the journey. Care and compensation under assimilated Regulation (EU) No 1177/2010 are the carrier's duty, and the Maritime and Coastguard Agency is the body that enforces those rules.

Is a Direct Ferries booking financially protected?

Not by a bond. There is no ABTA, ABTOT or ATOL cover on a crossing, because a ferry ticket bought on its own is a single travel service rather than a package and ATOL only applies where a flight is involved. The booking conditions say Direct Ferries “would recommend that you take out travel insurance which includes cover for End Supplier Failure”. Its own liability is capped at twice the amount you paid.

How long do refunds take?

Credit and debit card refunds are stated at 3 to 5 working days and PayPal or iDEAL at up to 10 working days once the cancellation is confirmed. A Cancel For Any Reason refund of 80% of the fare is paid within 5 working days on the last-minute version. A Best Price Promise refund goes to the original card in approximately 10 to 14 business days. Cancellations cannot be started by email.

Can I add cancellation cover to a Direct Ferries booking afterwards?

No. The help centre states that flexible fares and cancellation cover “can only be selected when you're making your booking and can't be added afterwards”. Cancel For Any Reason has the same rule and must be bought at the time of booking, requested at least 48 hours before sailing, and is limited to two uses per person per year.

How many ferry operators and routes does Direct Ferries actually cover?

Its about page is the fullest answer: 314 ferry companies plus Eurotunnel, 4,387 routes, around 930 ports and over 276 countries and islands. Other pages on the same site say 4,412 routes and 901 ports, “over 3000 ferry connections” and “almost 4,000 routes and over 270 ferry operators”. The figures are template-driven and refreshed on different cycles, so treat any single one as approximate.

How far ahead can I book, and when do I need to arrive at the port?

Timetables for most routes appear three to six months before departure, varying by operator and season. Check-in requires arrival “between 30 minutes and 2 hours” before sailing depending on the carrier, and the exact deadline is given in the confirmation email rather than at the point of sale. Some bookings are confirmed provisionally and firmed up within 24 to 48 hours.

Our verdict

If you know which ferry you want, book it from the company that owns the ship. If you do not — and on the Aegean, the Adriatic or the Irish Sea almost nobody does — this is the search that saves the afternoon, because 314 operators and 4,387 routes in one timetable is something no carrier will ever show you. The Channel is the weakest case for using it, since three operators run Dover–Calais and all three publish their own lead-in fares, and the strongest case is anywhere a journey needs two carriers who have never heard of each other. What to go in knowing: the service fee is a percentage of your basket and is not published until checkout, the amendment and cancellation charges sit on top of the ferry company's own, and the complaint window is 14 days from the booking. Buy the flexible fare at the moment you book if there is any doubt about the dates, because it cannot be added afterwards, and read the operator's conditions as well as these — in any conflict between the two, the operator's win.
