Dragonpass Airport Lounge

Rates and terms checked 27 October 2026 · Airport Lounge · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Dragonpass sells airport lounge entry by the pass, and the cheapest way in is £59 a year: a Solo Travel membership with two passes included, each one getting a person into any of more than 1,400 lounges across more than 130 countries. Prices read off Dragonpass's own membership page on 27 October 2026, and the contract from its terms and conditions dated 1 October 2026.

The company has been selling airport access since 2005. It says it now has over 40 million members, more than 400 corporate clients, offers at over 650 dining locations and premium retailers, and more than 200 Fast Track security lanes on the same membership. The UK business, Dragonpass International Ltd, has been filing at Companies House since 2013; its group accounts to 31 December 2025 went in on 25 September 2026.

Two things shape everything below, and neither is a criticism. A pass is not only a lounge visit — the same pass can be spent on £26 of airport dining credit or on a Fast Track lane that Dragonpass values at £11 to £19 — and the lounges belong to other companies, so the stay length, the capacity and the refund are theirs to set rather than Dragonpass's.

What £59 a year buys, and what £329 buys

There are three plans and they are priced per pass, not per month. Solo Travel is £59 a year and carries two passes; the page is explicit that “Passes are for your personal use each trip”, so there is no guest entitlement on it at all. Better Travel is £229 a year for eight passes and lets one accompanying guest share them. Travel Together is £329 a year for twelve passes, shareable with up to five guests. Extra passes beyond the allowance cost £29, £28 and £27 respectively, so the larger the plan the cheaper the top-up.

Divide the fee by the passes and the included ones cost £29.50 on Solo Travel, £28.63 on Better Travel and £27.42 on Travel Together. That is the whole price list in three numbers, and it is unusually flat: the £329 plan buys a pass only £2.08 cheaper than the £59 plan does.

The guest rules are where the plans really differ, because Dragonpass is clear that sharing is not free capacity: “Each guest visit uses a separate pass from your annual allowance.” Twelve passes on Travel Together is therefore six trips for a couple, or two trips for a party of six, not twelve visits plus guests. A family of four taking a return flight spends eight of the twelve passes on one holiday.

Set against that, the £59 entry price is genuinely the cheapest published route onto a network this size, and it is cheaper than the plans Dragonpass itself was selling in the spring. It includes two passes rather than none, which is not true of every lounge programme.

A pass is the unit of account, and the three things it buys are not worth the same

The membership page offers three uses for one pass. A lounge visit. “One pass for $32£26€31 towards your bill at participating airport restaurants”, with anything above the credit paid separately. Or “one pass for airport Fast Track security at participating airports”, described on the same page as “a service that costs $15£11€13–$25£19€22 if booked separately”.

Those three uses have three different values, and Dragonpass publishes all the numbers needed to see it. A pass costs between £27.42 and £29.50. Spend it on the dining credit and you have paid £27.42 to £29.50 for £26, losing £1.42 to £3.50. Spend it on Fast Track and you have paid the same for something the seller itself values at £11 to £19, losing £8.42 to £18.50. Only the lounge visit returns more than the pass cost — which makes the flexibility a safety valve for an airport with nothing you can use, rather than three equal options.

The dining credit has its own rules and they are worth reading before relying on it. Clause 2.1.2 allows “only 1 (one) Dining Voucher per purchase”. Clause 7.3.6 says “No refund, cashback or credit will be offered in the event that the Dining Voucher is applied to a bill”, so a £20 lunch does not leave £6 behind. Clause 7.3.7 makes the vouchers “not exchangeable for cash, credit or other goods and services, and non-transferable”, and clause 7.3.8 subjects them to “certain local restrictions such as taxes, tips and alcohol”. Fast Track is “valid for 1 (one) use only” under clause 7.5.10, needs a separate booking for every guest, and is not refunded if cancelled “less than 72 (seventy-two) hours in advance”.

One oddity: the word the sales page is built on does not appear in the contract. The terms never define a Pass. They define a Dragonpass Membership at clause 2.1.5, Dining Vouchers at 2.1.2 and single-use Lounge passes at 7.15.5, each with its own expiry and its own rules. The single currency on the shop front is three separate products once you are inside.

The rooms belong to somebody else, and the booking fee is theirs too

Dragonpass says this twice and plainly. Clause 2.1.6: Participating Outlets “are not subcontractors, agents or representatives of Dragonpass”. Clause 7.7.17: the company is “in no way responsible for the management” of the lounges. Clause 7.7.19 follows from both — “access to a particular Lounge is not guaranteed and is subject to availability” — and clause 7.15.10 adds that a lounge may be unavailable when delays push traffic through it. The network itself moves: clauses 7.1.12 and 7.15.16 allow outlets to be “added or removed at any time without notice”, and clause 7.15.8 lets Dragonpass “amend the Lounge visit charges from time to time in its sole discretion”.

That third-party relationship is also where a cost appears that the price list does not carry. The comparison table on the membership page lists “Additional fee” against lounge pre-booking on all three plans and names no amount, and no figure for it appears anywhere else on the site. The operator publishes it instead: No1 Lounges, which runs the No1, Clubrooms and My Lounge rooms at Birmingham, Gatwick, Heathrow, Jersey and Luton, invites scheme cardholders to “Reserve your place at the lounge from just £6 using your Dragonpass”, and explains why in one sentence — “Without a pre-booking, we cannot guarantee you will gain access.”

Add that £6 to a £29.50 pass and a Solo Travel lounge visit costs about £35.50 in practice; on Travel Together it is about £33.42. Both are still below the £42 average entry price Which? measured in May 2026, but the gap is roughly £6 to £8 a visit rather than the half-price saving a membership implies.

The same operator page is useful for two other reasons. A pre-booking for the Clubrooms lounges at Gatwick “also includes complimentary access to the Fast track security lanes”, which is a pass saved; and Fast Track “is not available at Birmingham, Heathrow, and Luton Airports”, which is a pass that cannot be spent that way at three of the five airports it runs. It is also candid about the limits of a three-party arrangement: “No1 Lounges does not have access to any systems outside of its own”, so a membership question has to go back to Dragonpass or to the card issuer.

Two hours, one hour late, and forty-eight hours' notice

The timings are all in clause 7.15 and they are tighter than a lounge day out suggests. The stay is “usually 2 (two) hours”, and once it “has lapsed, you will be required to re-register your entry” — so a long connection is not one visit. A pre-booking or a cancellation “must be made at a minimum of 48 (forty-eight) hours in advance”, which rules out booking a lounge the night before an early flight. Arrive late and the lounge “will allow you entry to the Lounge for up to 1 (one) hour” past the booked time, after which the slot is gone.

Children are handled sensibly: under clause 7.15.18(g), “Children who are subject to free entry at the Lounge do not need to be included in the pre-booking”, so a free infant does not consume a pass. The free-entry age is the lounge's rule rather than Dragonpass's, which is another number to check on the room you are actually booking.

A bank reselling the same product spells out a rule Dragonpass does not. Revolut's own lounge pass terms, which name Dragonpass as the provider, say a pass is “valid for one visit per person”, that you “may not access more than one lounge within a two-hour period”, and that a valid boarding pass and ID or passport have to be shown at the door. On the Revolut route the pass also stays valid “as long as your Revolut account remains open”, where a Dragonpass single-use lounge pass bought direct is “valid for 1 (one) year from the date of purchase” under clause 7.15.5.

Where you buy it decides whether you can get your money back

Buy direct and the money is spent. Clause 7.1.2 makes memberships bought through Dragonpass channels non-refundable. Clause 4.8 lets you delete the membership in the app or on the website but gives “no refunds or compensation”. Clause 5.1 is the sharpest of the three: a membership “will need to be activated within 1 (one) year of the day on which you purchased” it, and if it is not, it is “terminated without any refund, extension of duration, or compensation of any kind”. Once activated it runs for one year under clause 5.2, and clause 5.3 ends the entitlement on expiry. Clause 4.3 allows suspension or termination without refund where the information given was “false, incorrect or incomplete”, and clause 12.1 lets Dragonpass end the services at any time by notice.

Buy the same lounge access through a reseller and the answer can be different. Revolut's terms say an unused pass “can be refunded if you request it within 14 days of purchase” through in-app chat. Same lounges, same provider named in the document, opposite refund position — and the only variable is where the purchase was made.

There is a question here we could not settle and it is worth asking before paying. Regulation 30(2) of the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 provides that for a service contract “the cancellation period ends at the end of 14 days after the day on which the contract is entered into”. The Dragonpass terms say nothing about any cancellation period, and clause 7.1.2 says the opposite. We are not able to reconcile the two from the published documents, so ask customer services to confirm the position in writing before you buy an annual plan you might not use.

Six directors, two countries, and the filings that are public

The company behind the brand is DRAGONPASS INTERNATIONAL LTD., number 08643888, registered in England and Wales. Its registered office moved to Floor 4, Charter House, 2 Woodlands Road, Altrincham WA14 1HF on 4 September 2025, and that is the address printed in the terms and on the about page.

The board has six serving directors and the dates tell you how the UK arm grew. Mark Ian Koch was appointed on 8 August 2013; Kehui Cai and Jiangnan Zhu followed on 23 October 2013, both recorded as resident in China; Andrew Harrison-Chinn joined on 20 October 2015; Peter John Hill on 7 February 2025; and Ming Sun on 31 December 2025, the same day Yik Long Ronald Tsang's appointment was terminated. Four of the ten officers ever listed have resigned.

The filing record is unbroken and current. Group accounts to 31 December 2025 were filed on 25 September 2026 and run to 32 pages; the previous year's ran to 30 and went in on 30 September 2025. A confirmation statement made on 22 December 2025 was filed with no updates. Group accounts are themselves informative: this is the parent of a group rather than a single trading company.

Scale has an outside witness too. Plaza Premium Group's own announcement of a network partnership names Mark Koch as chief executive of Dragonpass International, puts Dragonpass at 1,200 lounges against Plaza Premium's own 200-plus, and states a combined ambition of over 1,700 lounges for corporate clients. Read beside today's “1,400+”, that is a network that has grown by about 200 rooms since.

One check we could not finish: the persons-with-significant-control register at Companies House refused to load on three attempts, so the ultimate ownership of the UK company is not established on this page. The directors' recorded countries of residence point at a group with Chinese and British halves, and the company's own pages say nothing at all about who owns it.

What Which? measured, and the sum it stops short of

Which? published research on lounge memberships on 7 May 2026, written by senior researcher Amy Axworthy. Two of its figures matter more than the rest: the average UK lounge entry price is now £42 per adult, against £25 in 2017, and Dragonpass is accepted at roughly 65 UK lounges across 25 airports. It also worked out the mid-tier plan at about £21 a visit and made the same point about dining credit losing money, pricing a credit worth about £18 at £26 to redeem.

Be careful reading any write-up of this product from before the autumn, though, including that one. On 7 May 2026 Which? recorded Dragonpass selling a Classic plan at £68 with one visit included and a Preferential plan at £168 with eight. Neither name nor price is on sale now. The three plans above replaced them at some point between 7 May and 27 October 2026, and we could not date the change from Dragonpass's own pages.

The arithmetic nobody publishes is the break-even, and on today's prices it is unforgiving at the bottom of the range. Solo Travel costs £59 and the two passes still need reserving at about £6 each, so two lounge visits cost £71 against £84 at the £42 average — a £13 saving. Take only one visit in the year and you have paid £65 for £42 of lounge, which is £23 worse than paying at the door. The plan pays at two visits and not at one.

Higher up the range the sums are better and more demanding. Travel Together at £329 plus twelve £6 reservations is £401 for twelve visits, £33.42 each, worth about £103 against the average — but it needs twelve lounge visits inside one year from activation, with every guest visit eating a pass. Which?'s own worked example makes the opposite case for light users: three Manchester visits bought at £30.39 a person came to £91.17 against £132 on the Classic plan then on sale. The honest summary is that this product rewards people who already fly often and punishes optimism.

Where it wins

  • £59 a year is the cheapest published route onto a 1,400-lounge network, and it includes two passes rather than none
  • More than 1,400 lounges in over 130 countries, plus 200-plus Fast Track lanes and offers at over 650 dining and retail locations on the same membership
  • One pass can be spent on a lounge, £26 of dining credit or a Fast Track lane, so a membership is not dead weight at an airport with no lounge you can use
  • Passes can be shared — one guest on Better Travel, up to five on Travel Together — which few lounge programmes allow at all
  • Top-up passes are priced below the included ones: £27 on Travel Together against the £27.42 an included pass works out at
  • The full terms are published and dated 1 October 2026, with clause-level detail on lounges, dining, Fast Track, delays and cancellations
  • Flight Delay registration gives lounge access by email when a tracked flight is delayed more than 60 minutes, provided the flight is registered at least 24 hours before departure
  • Dragonpass International Ltd has filed accounts every year since 2013, with group accounts to 31 December 2025 filed on 25 September 2026
  • The contract is governed by the laws of England and Wales, with the courts of England and Wales named for any dispute

Common questions

How much does one Dragonpass lounge visit cost?

An included pass works out at £29.50 on the £59 Solo Travel plan, £28.63 on the £229 Better Travel plan and £27.42 on the £329 Travel Together plan. Extra passes are £29, £28 and £27. On top of that the lounge charges its own reservation fee — No1 Lounges publishes it as “from just £6 per person” — so a real visit lands between about £33.42 and £35.50.

Can a pass be used for something other than a lounge?

Yes, and the membership page gives the values. One pass buys £26 of credit towards a bill at a participating airport restaurant, with anything above that paid separately, or one Fast Track security lane that Dragonpass says “costs $15£11€13–$25£19€22 if booked separately”. Both are worth less than a pass costs, so they are best treated as a fallback where no lounge is usable rather than as equivalent choices.

Can I bring someone with me?

Not on Solo Travel, where “Passes are for your personal use each trip”. Better Travel allows one accompanying guest and Travel Together up to five, but every guest visit “uses a separate pass from your annual allowance”, so twelve passes is six trips for two people. A child who gets free entry at the lounge itself does not need to be in the booking at all under clause 7.15.18(g).

How long can I stay, and how far ahead do I have to book?

Clause 7.15.9 puts the stay at “usually 2 (two) hours”, after which you have to re-register your entry. Pre-bookings and cancellations “must be made at a minimum of 48 (forty-eight) hours in advance” under clause 7.15.18(i), and if you turn up late the lounge will admit you for up to one hour past the booked time. Entry is never guaranteed: clause 7.7.19 makes it “subject to availability”.

Can I get a refund if I do not use the membership?

Not if you bought it from Dragonpass. Clause 7.1.2 makes direct memberships non-refundable, deleting one in the app brings “no refunds or compensation”, and a membership not activated within a year of purchase is terminated with nothing back. A pass bought through a reseller can be different — Revolut refunds an unused one within 14 days. Regulation 30(2) of the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013 gives 14 days to cancel a service contract, the terms do not mention it, and we could not reconcile the two, so get the position in writing before buying a plan on spec.

Who actually runs the lounges, and who do I complain to?

Third-party operators the terms call Participating Outlets, which clause 2.1.6 says are “not subcontractors, agents or representatives of Dragonpass”. Under clause 11.1 a dispute about what an outlet served or did goes to that outlet; anything within Dragonpass's own control has to be raised in writing within 90 days of the event. Total liability is capped at USD 100 by clause 10.3, and there is no financial regulator or ombudsman sitting behind a lounge membership.

Our verdict

Dragonpass is a real network at a real price, and £59 a year for two passes is the cheapest published way onto 1,400-plus lounges that we can find. The catch is not hidden, it is just not added up: an included pass costs £27.42 to £29.50, the lounge adds its own reservation fee of about £6, and two of the three things a pass buys are worth less than the pass. At two or more lounge visits a year the Solo Travel plan beats the £42 average door price; at one it costs £23 more. Travel Together is the better deal per visit at about £33.42, but only if twelve passes genuinely get used inside twelve months by you and your guests. Read clause 5.1 before you buy ahead for a trip you have not booked, and ask in writing about cancellation rights if you are buying an annual plan on spec.
