E.ON Next Dual Fuel

Rates and terms checked 27 October 2026 · Dual Fuel · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

E.ON Next's Next Pledge tariff is priced £50 a year below the Ofgem cap, and the supplier splits that saving evenly: £25 off each fuel, taken off the unit rates rather than the standing charge. That is the only quantified reward it publishes for taking gas and electricity together. Figures checked 27 October 2026 against E.ON Next's own Next Flex price sheet effective 1 October 2026, its charges list, its residential terms of 24 June 2025 and Companies House.

It is a big supplier with an unusually good service record for its size. Citizens Advice scored E.ON Next 3.71 out of 5 for April to June 2026, placing it third of the sixteen domestic suppliers it rates and ahead of every other large one — Octopus at 3.67, Ovo at 3.65, EDF at 2.96, ScottishPower at 2.76 and British Gas at 2.61. Its own quarterly report for the same three months counts 59,276 complaints opened at 760 per 100,000 customers, which puts roughly 7.8 million customer accounts behind the brand.

On Next Flex, the variable tariff a household lands on by default, there is no dual-fuel discount of any kind. The two fuels are priced from separate tables, the two standing charges simply add together, and nothing is taken off either for holding both. What does move a two-fuel bill at E.ON Next is the region the meters sit in and the way the bill is paid: the first is worth £89.87 a year in standing charges alone, and the second as much as £141.35.

Where the meters sit is worth more than any discount in this market

E.ON Next publishes its variable prices as a single PDF covering all fourteen distribution regions, effective 1 October 2026. Reading the two standing charges together is the quickest way to understand what a dual-fuel account costs before a kettle is switched on.

A London household on Direct Debit pays 42.915p a day for electricity and 30.199p for gas: 73.114p combined, or £266.87 over a year. The same account on Manweb, which covers north Wales and Merseyside, pays 67.657p and 30.078p — 97.735p a day, or £356.73. The gap between the two is 24.621p a day, which is £89.87 a year, and every penny of it is decided by geography rather than by anything the household does.

Almost all of that gap is on the electricity side. Gas standing charges on Direct Debit move only from 29.157p in the Southern region to 30.199p in London, a span of 1.042p a day. Electricity runs from London's 42.915p to Manweb's 67.657p, a span of 24.742p. So the regional lottery on a dual-fuel account is really an electricity lottery with a gas bill attached, and a reader comparing suppliers on a single national figure will not see it at all.

Put the unit rates on top and the whole picture emerges. Using Ofgem's revised typical consumption of 2,500 kWh of electricity and 9,500 kWh of gas, in force since 1 July 2026, a Next Flex Direct Debit account costs about £1,672.22 a year in the East Midlands and £1,804.16 on Manweb — a £131.94 spread on identical usage. The headline cap of £1,723 for 1 October to 31 December 2026 is a Great Britain average across those same regions, so a regional figure sitting above it is arithmetic rather than a breach.

The price of not paying by Direct Debit is charged once per fuel

E.ON Next's price sheet carries three tables: Direct Debit, Other Payment Methods, and Prepayment and Smart Pay As You Go. The middle one is where a dual-fuel household quietly loses the most money, because every penny of the difference lands twice.

Take the Eastern region. On Direct Debit the standing charges are 51.835p and 29.339p; on other payment methods they are 60.621p and 37.498p. That is 16.945p more a day, or £61.85 a year, for nothing but the method of payment. The unit rates move as well — electricity from 26.613p to 28.109p and gas from 7.911p to 8.336p — and at typical consumption the full gap comes to £139.62 a year.

That pattern holds everywhere. Across the fourteen regions the standing-charge penalty alone runs from £54.32 in London to £62.58 on Sweb, and the total annual difference from £133.28 to £141.35. A single-fuel customer pays roughly half of it. The structure is entirely normal in this market and E.ON Next publishes it openly, which is more than some do — but it means the real dual-fuel decision at E.ON Next is not whether to consolidate, it is whether to set up a direct debit, and that is worth about three times what the Next Pledge discount is worth.

Prepayment carries the lowest unit rates on the sheet

The third table upends the usual expectation. On Prepayment and Smart Pay As You Go, E.ON Next's unit rates are the cheapest of the three — not the dearest. In the Eastern region prepayment electricity is 25.800p against Direct Debit's 26.613p, and prepayment gas is 7.679p against 7.911p. Across all fourteen regions prepayment electricity comes in between 0.775p and 0.852p below the Direct Debit rate, and gas between 0.228p and 0.298p below.

The standing charges are, to all intents, identical: the largest difference anywhere between the Direct Debit and prepayment figures is 0.009p a day, which is three pence a year. So the whole advantage sits in the unit rates, and on a dual-fuel account at typical consumption it is worth between £42.08 and £48.49 a year depending on region — £42.39 in the Eastern region, £48.23 on Manweb.

This is a consequence of the price cap's own structure rather than generosity, and it does not make prepayment a good idea for a household that could pay monthly instead: a meter that stops supplying energy when the credit runs out is a different product from one that does not. It is worth knowing because the folk assumption runs the other way, and because an E.ON Next customer already on a prepayment meter is not on the supplier's worst terms.

One gas standing charge for the whole country, unless you pay monthly

Here is a detail the price sheet gives away that nothing on E.ON Next's website explains. On Direct Debit the gas standing charge varies by region, from 29.157p to 30.199p a day. On Other Payment Methods it does not: the fourteen regional figures run from 37.483p to 37.501p, a total span of 0.018p a day across the whole of Great Britain. Four regions — Eastern, Manweb, Midlands and Scottish Hydro — are priced at exactly 37.498p.

In other words the regional gas differences that E.ON Next charges a Direct Debit household disappear entirely for a household that pays on receipt of a bill, which is instead charged a flat national figure about 7.3p to 8.3p a day higher than the Direct Debit rate for the same postcode. Nothing in the price sheet, the help pages or the residential terms sets out why, and no footnote marks it. It is not an error and it is not hidden — it is simply unexplained, and on a dual-fuel account the unexplained part is the larger of the two standing charges.

E.ON Next's help pages are candid about the one thing a standing-charge-averse reader will ask next: We don't currently offer a tariff with zero standing charge, so you'll need to pay it for any meters you have with us. Ofgem consulted on requiring every major supplier to offer at least one lower standing charge tariff, saying in a release of 24 September 2025 that if implemented, the tariffs will be offered by suppliers by the end of January 2026. Whether that requirement was made binding could not be established from Ofgem's own pages on 27 October 2026, so the honest statement is the narrow one: E.ON Next offers no zero-standing-charge tariff and names no lower-standing-charge one either.

Next Pledge: a £50 discount and a £50 door

Next Pledge is where the dual-fuel reward actually lives. It is described as a 12-month fixed term tracker tariff with variable rates that are always lower in comparison to the Ofgem price cap, and the discount is stated plainly in its footnote: £50 saving is applied as a reduction on unit rates split across both electricity and gas (£25 saving per fuel) based on Ofgem average dual fuel customer. Direct Debit is required and a smart meter is installed where possible.

Two things follow, and the first is in the customer's favour. Because the saving is applied to unit rates rather than the standing charge, a household that uses more than the Ofgem average gets more than £50, and the rates move down with the cap each quarter instead of being locked at today's level. Set against a cap of 26.32p for electricity and 7.97p for gas, a £25-a-fuel reduction at typical consumption works out at roughly 1.0p off each kWh of electricity and 0.26p off each kWh of gas.

The second is the arithmetic the page does not put side by side. Leaving Next Pledge for another supplier costs only a £25 exit fee per fuel — which on a dual-fuel account is £50. A full year's dual-fuel discount and the cost of walking away are the same number. Moving to a different E.ON Next tariff is free, so the fee is specifically a charge for leaving the company, and it is the mechanism that converts a tracker discount into a twelve-month commitment.

The fixed tariffs charge more to leave. Next Fixed 12m is £50 per fuel and Next Fixed 24m is £100 per fuel, so a dual-fuel household faces £100 and £200 respectively. Next Flex, Next Smart Saver, Next Optimise, Next Drive Smart and Next Pumped carry no exit fee at all. The EV tariffs are the outliers on price: Next Drive advertises lower-priced electricity 8.5p/KWh when you charge between 12am and 6am and Next Drive Smart a 6.5p/kWh off-peak rate for your whole home every night from midnight-6am, both against a national average standard variable rate E.ON Next gives as 26.322p from 1 October 2026.

The only charge with the words dual fuel beside it is £470

E.ON Next keeps a single page listing its additional charges, and it is worth reading before signing up for both fuels, because the one line that names dual fuel is a cost rather than a saving. Removing both smart meters is priced at £470; removing one, gas or electricity, is £235. The dual-fuel figure is exactly double, so there is no reduction for doing both in one visit.

The rest of the list is a tariff of its own. A residential late payment fee is £15, a debt collection fee £25, and a debt resolution visit — formerly pre-disconnection fee, in E.ON Next's own words — £55. A warrant costs £90 with a £60 application fee on top. Missing a booked meter appointment is £30. A standard load test is £103.93 and fitting an isolator £98.07. The open-ended one is the debt collection agency admin fee, given as up to 31% of the outstanding balance, which on a £600 dual-fuel winter arrear would be £186 before the £25 and £55 charges are added.

Credit should be given where the list is honest: these charges are published in one place, in plain figures, on a page a customer can find from the policies index. Several suppliers scatter the same information across separate downloads or do not publish it at all.

Two fuels, and the terms treat them as two accounts

E.ON Next's own guide to dual fuel states that you'll receive one combined bill showing your electricity and gas use. Its residential terms, last updated 24 June 2025, describe something closer to two ledgers under one roof. Where a customer is in credit, the terms say E.ON Next may use it to pay off a debt on any other account you have with us (for example for a different fuel or property) — a sentence that only makes sense if each fuel can carry its own account and its own balance.

The switching clause reads the same way: a customer paying in advance can move supplier provided they owe £500 or less for each fuel, which is a per-fuel threshold and therefore a £1,000 ceiling across a dual-fuel account. And where a customer has more than one account, the terms let E.ON Next decide which one a payment is applied to unless a payment plan says otherwise.

None of that is unfair and some of it helps — an electricity credit wiping out a gas debt is usually what a household would want. It is worth knowing because it is the opposite of how the marketing describes the product, and because it means the two fuels are not as consolidated as one bill implies.

The terms are thinner on the point a reader would most want pinned down. On a price rise they say only that we'll contact you before we put the prices up, with no number of days attached; the single 30-day notice period in the document applies to changes in how renewable electricity is sourced. The cooling-off period is 14 days from agreeing the contract, and an exit fee can be charged for switching more than six weeks before a tariff ends.

A good service record, and an electricity mix that is not

The Citizens Advice assessment for April to June 2026 is the strongest independent thing on this page and it breaks down into four parts. Fewer complaints received scored 3.0 out of 5 on a measurement of 27.1 complaints per 10,000 customers. Contact waiting time scored 3.6, on an average call-centre wait of 1 minute 57 seconds, 91.4% of emails answered inside two days and 100.0% of WhatsApp messages. Billing and metering scored 4.3, on bill accuracy of 98.51% for smart meters and 93.58% for traditional ones and 94.82% of smart meters working correctly. Customer commitments scored a full 5.

E.ON Next's own quarterly report fills in the volume behind that. For April to June 2026 it opened 59,276 complaints and resolved 59,138, settling 49.61% by the end of the next working day and 89.69% within eight weeks. The commonest causes were customer service at 32.28%, payments at 12.16%, general billing and meter reading at 11.19% and debt at 10.40%. Over the twelve months to 30 September 2025, 114,511 complaints were not resolved by the end of the next working day.

Half of a dual-fuel account is electricity, and that is where the company looks weakest. Its fuel mix disclosure for 1 April 2025 to 31 March 2026 gives 27.3% renewable, 53.2% natural gas, 9.3% coal, 5.8% nuclear and 4.4% other, at 322g of carbon dioxide per kWh. Coal at nearly a tenth of supply is unusual among large suppliers in 2026. The disclosure is an electricity figure only — E.ON Next publishes no carbon number for its gas, which arrives through the same network as everybody else's.

If a complaint stalls, the route out is the Energy Ombudsman, free to use, reachable on 0330 440 1624 or at PO Box 966, Warrington WA4 9DF, once eight weeks have passed or a final offer letter has arrived. E.ON Next's own complaints line is 0808 501 5200, open 9am to 5pm Monday to Thursday and 9am to 4pm on Friday, with email and WhatsApp staffed 8am to 10pm every day.

Where it wins

  • Next Pledge is priced below the Ofgem cap by £50 a year, split £25 per fuel, and tracks the cap down each quarter rather than fixing at today's level
  • Third of sixteen suppliers in the Citizens Advice ratings for April to June 2026 at 3.71 out of 5, and the best-scoring of the large suppliers
  • A full regional price sheet is published as a dated PDF, so every unit rate and standing charge can be checked before signing up
  • Billing and metering scored 4.3 out of 5, on 98.51% smart bill accuracy and 94.82% of smart meters working correctly
  • Additional charges are listed in one place with plain figures, including the £15 late payment fee and the £55 debt resolution visit
  • No exit fee at all on Next Flex, Next Smart Saver, Next Optimise, Next Drive Smart or Next Pumped, and no fee for moving between E.ON Next tariffs
  • Prepayment and Smart Pay As You Go carry the cheapest unit rates on the sheet, between 0.775p and 0.852p below Direct Debit on electricity
  • An average call-centre wait of 1 minute 57 seconds, with 100.0% of WhatsApp messages answered within two days

Where it falls short

  • There is no dual-fuel discount on Next Flex, the default variable tariff — the two fuels are priced from separate tables and nothing is taken off either for holding both
  • A full year of the Next Pledge dual-fuel discount is £50, and leaving for another supplier costs £25 per fuel, which is also £50
  • Two standing charges on Direct Debit range from 73.114p a day in London to 97.735p on Manweb, an £89.87 annual gap decided by postcode alone
  • Paying by anything other than Direct Debit costs between £133.28 and £141.35 a year more at typical consumption, because every element of the difference is charged twice
  • The gas standing charge is flat nationally at about 37.49p a day on Other Payment Methods but regional on Direct Debit, and nothing E.ON Next publishes explains why
  • No zero-standing-charge tariff is offered and no lower-standing-charge tariff is named, which the help pages state outright
  • Its own dual-fuel guide still quotes an Ofgem average annual bill of £1,849 from February 2025, measured on consumption values Ofgem replaced on 1 July 2026, against a current cap of £1,723
  • The fixed-tariffs page claims the dual-fuel fixes are “certainly cheaper than taking out a single fixed one just for your electricity or gas” without naming a figure, a basis or a date
  • The electricity fuel mix for 1 April 2025 to 31 March 2026 is 9.3% coal and only 27.3% renewable, at 322g of CO2 per kWh, and no carbon figure is published for the gas at all
  • The residential terms promise only that “we'll contact you before we put the prices up”, with no notice period in days anywhere in the document
  • Removing both smart meters costs £470, exactly twice the £235 single-meter charge, so there is no saving for doing both in one visit

Common questions

Does E.ON Next give a dual fuel discount?

On Next Flex, the variable tariff, no — gas and electricity are priced from separate regional tables and nothing is deducted for taking both. On Next Pledge there is £50 a year, applied as £25 saving per fuel off the unit rates and based on Ofgem's average dual fuel customer. Direct Debit is required for it. E.ON Next's own guide to dual fuel says only that some suppliers give you a discount for choosing a dual fuel tariff and names no amount of its own.

What will two standing charges cost me a year?

Between £266.87 and £356.73 on Direct Debit, depending entirely on region. London is cheapest at 42.915p a day for electricity and 30.199p for gas; Manweb, covering north Wales and Merseyside, is dearest at 67.657p and 30.078p. Paying by any other method except prepayment adds a further £54.32 to £62.58 a year to those two figures. All of this is on the price sheet effective 1 October 2026.

Is it cheaper to pay by Direct Debit?

Substantially, and twice over, because both the standing charge and the unit rate differ on each fuel. In the Eastern region a Direct Debit account pays 26.613p for electricity and 7.911p for gas against 28.109p and 8.336p on other payment methods, with 16.945p a day more in standing charges on top. At Ofgem's typical consumption of 2,500 kWh and 9,500 kWh that is £139.62 a year. Across all fourteen regions the gap runs from £133.28 to £141.35.

What does it cost to leave E.ON Next?

It depends which tariff, and on a dual-fuel account every figure doubles because the fee is charged per fuel. Next Pledge is £25 a fuel, so £50. Next Fixed 12m is £50 a fuel, so £100. Next Fixed 24m is £100 a fuel, so £200. Next Flex, Next Smart Saver, Next Optimise, Next Drive Smart and Next Pumped carry none. Moving between E.ON Next tariffs is free on all of them, and the residential terms allow a fee only where the switch happens more than six weeks before the tariff ends.

Is E.ON Next the same company as npower?

It uses npower's supply licence. E.ON Next Energy Limited is company 03782443, incorporated on 4 June 1999 as Hackremco (No.1496) Limited, renamed Nitrogen Two Limited that November, then npower Direct Limited on 18 September 2000, and finally E.ON Next Energy Limited on 3 June 2020. E.ON acquired npower in November 2019 as part of the Innogy purchase and began migrating customers to E.ON Next in July 2020. The registered office is Trinity House, 2 Burton Street, Nottingham NG1 4BX.

How good is E.ON Next's customer service?

Better than most large suppliers on the only independent measure that covers the whole market. Citizens Advice scored it 3.71 out of 5 for April to June 2026, third of the sixteen suppliers rated, behind Outfox Energy at 4.03 and 100Green at 3.90 and ahead of Octopus at 3.67, EDF at 2.96 and British Gas at 2.61. Its weakest component was complaints volume at 3.0 out of 5 on 27.1 complaints per 10,000 customers; its strongest was customer commitments at 5. Its own report for the same quarter shows 89.69% of complaints resolved within eight weeks.

Can I get a tariff with no standing charge?

Not from E.ON Next. Its help pages say plainly: We don't currently offer a tariff with zero standing charge, so you'll need to pay it for any meters you have with us. On a dual-fuel account that means paying two of them, every day, whether or not any energy is used. Ofgem said in a release of 24 September 2025 that it wanted to require all major suppliers to offer at least one tariff with a lower standing charge, adding that the change is unlikely to reduce bills, as tariffs with a lower standing charge will have a higher unit rate.

Our verdict

Judged as a dual-fuel proposition, E.ON Next pays £50 a year for the business and charges £50 to end it, which is a modest offer honestly described. Judged as a supplier, it is a better one than that makes it sound: third of sixteen on the Citizens Advice ratings and ahead of every other large name, with a published regional price sheet, a published charges list and a published complaints report, none of which is universal in this market. Two things decide what a household actually pays, and neither is the dual-fuel question. Pay by Direct Debit, which is worth between £133.28 and £141.35 a year, and understand that the postcode moves the two standing charges by £89.87 whatever anyone does. Then take Next Pledge rather than Next Flex, because £25 a fuel off the cap for a £25-a-fuel exit fee is a fair trade for anyone not planning to move again inside the year. The gaps that remain are real — a coal share of 9.3% in the electricity, a price-rise clause with no notice period in it, and a national gas standing charge on one payment method that nobody has explained — but they are the sort of thing a reader can price in rather than be caught by.
