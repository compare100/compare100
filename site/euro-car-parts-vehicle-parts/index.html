Euro Car Parts Review: What a Pad Costs, and Which Sale You Landed On

A Pagid brake pad costs £32.79 at Euro Car Parts, down from a shown price of £40.99, and standard delivery is free on anything over £25. Collect it instead and the shop charges nothing at all: click-and-collect is free from more than 250 branches and the company says an order is ready in 15 minutes. Every figure on this page was read from Euro Car Parts' own pages, documents and filings on 3 November 2026.

There is a lot here that other parts sellers do not offer. Returns run to 60 days rather than the 14 the law requires, and the terms say so in those words — the company calls it a goodwill guarantee that is “more generous than your legal rights under the Consumer Contracts Regulations”. Every product carries “a minimum of 12 months warranty (unless otherwise stated)”. A Price Lock scheme holds the price of “1000s of products” for “at least four weeks”, and the company undertakes that a locked price “will not go above the price locked in at the start of that cycle” while it “may go down during each cycle”. If you would rather not do the job yourself, Fit It For Me books the part into a local garage and delivers it there before your appointment.

Two things are worth knowing before you click. The company you buy from is not called Euro Car Parts: it is LKQ Group (UK) Limited, number 02680212, which carried the name Euro Car Parts Limited until 29 February 2024. The same company also runs carparts4less.co.uk off the same terms document and the same branch network — and the two sites do not price delivery the same way, so the cheaper basket depends on which of its shopfronts you open. And on the afternoon this page was written, six of those two sites' own pages were advertising six different sales, with five different discount codes and four different closing dates. Which one you get is decided by the page you arrive on, so it is worth two minutes to check before you pay.

£32.79 for the pad, and eight ways to get it to your door

Euro Car Parts publishes its whole carriage tariff on one page, which is more than most parts sellers manage, and it runs to eight named services. Standard costs £3.95 and arrives “Within 3-4 working days (07:00 - 19:30hrs)” if you order before 4pm, and it is free above £25 — so the £32.79 pad lands at £32.79. Below that line the maths turns: a £19 wiper blade becomes £22.95 delivered, a 21% premium on the part.

Above Standard there is Express at £5.95 for the next working day between 08:00 and 18:00, with cut-offs of 8pm Monday to Thursday, 3pm on Friday and 2pm on Saturday and no Sunday deliveries; a second Express variant at the same £5.95 that takes orders “before midnight Monday-Thursday” on eligible products only; Premium at £6.95, also next working day but across the longer 07:00 to 19:30 window; and Same Day Delivery at £7.95, which needs an order before 12:00 midday Monday to Friday or 11:00 AM on Saturday. Weekend slots cost £7.95 each and both have to be booked by 2pm on the Friday. Anything bulky goes by Large Parcel Delivery at £14.95, quoted at “Within 3-5 working days”.

The cheapest route is not a delivery at all. Click and collect is “FREE from 250+ Branches Nationwide”, items are “available in 15 minutes for collection”, and a branch big enough to hold the part is usually the difference between fitting the car this afternoon and fitting it on Thursday. Two small catches sit in the small print rather than on the banner: orders placed after 5pm are processed the next day, and the delivery page says the part is held “until the end of the next working day” while clause 13.7 of the terms says the company “will hold your order in your selected branch for three (3) days or as may be stated on your original order”. The free and Express services are also switched off for international orders, “some out-of-area UK postcodes”, oversized parcels and “specialist or dangerous goods” — a category that, on the returns page, explicitly takes in brake cleaner, brake fluid, aerosols and shock absorbers, which is a fair slice of what people actually buy.

Six banners, five codes, and a help page saying you need none

Here is the single most useful thing a shopper can know about this retailer, read in one sitting on 3 November 2026. Six of its own pages were advertising six different offers, and they do not agree with each other.

The brake pads category page carried “UP TO 10% OFF OVER 16,000 PARTS!” with a counter reading “ENDS IN 14 Days” and no code at all. A second page at the singular address, brake-pad rather than brake-pads, carried “SAVE UP TO 30% ON PARTS” with “USE CODE: SAVE30”, a counter reading “Ends in: 6 days”, and a published ladder — 30% off a £100 spend with SAVE30, 25% off £50 with SAVE25, 20% off £20 with SAVE20. The product page for the £37.69 Pagid pad carried “SPEND £100 & SAVE 20%” and “USE CODE: SAVE20”, with SAVE15 and SAVE10 offered underneath. The product page for the £32.79 pad carried “Up to 30% off over 40,000 parts, hurry ends tomorrow!” plus “FREE mystery gift when you click & collect with code MYSTERY”. The brake fitting page carried only “Use Code: BRAND”. And over on the company's other site, the regulated credit disclosure page of all places carried “End of year sale! Save 24% on Car Parts with code SALE24”, counting down 9 days.

Read the two ladders side by side and the arithmetic is the point. A £100 order is worth 20% off on one page and 30% off on another — £10 of the same basket, decided by nothing except which page the shopper happened to open. The same sale is described as covering 16,000 parts on one page and 40,000 on another, and as ending in 14 days, in 6 days, in 9 days and “tomorrow”. None of this is concealed and none of it is dishonest; it reads like several campaigns running on several templates at once. But the practical consequence is real, and it is the reason to open two or three pages before you check out.

Meanwhile the company's own help centre answers the question “Do I need a Promo Code?” with the words “Not with Great Value. Always.” It then lists what it means by that — free same-day collection from 250+ UK branches, free standard delivery over £25, and 60 days to return unused products — and describes Price Lock, which covers “1000s of products” for “at least four weeks” and promises that a locked price “will not go above the price locked in at the start of that cycle” although it “may go down during each cycle”. That is a genuinely good scheme and it is the right answer to a shopper who distrusts permanent sales. It is also flatly at odds with five live codes on five of the shop's own pages, and the help article carries no date, so there is no telling which position is the newer one.

One company, two websites, two different free-delivery lines

carparts4less.co.uk looks like a competitor and is not. Its terms and conditions name the same operator in the same clauses as the Euro Car Parts terms — “LKQ Group (UK) Limited”, “Our company number is 2680212”, the same Tamworth registered office — with the same clause 20.9 goodwill table, the same clause 23.5 liability cap and the same WS Goods carve-outs. Its complaints document gives an @eurocarparts.com email address. Its click-and-collect runs off the same “250+ collection points”.

What differs is the part a shopper actually pays. Standard delivery on Car Parts 4 Less is the same £3.95 but free only above £30, against £25 on Euro Car Parts, and the cut-off is 6pm rather than 4pm. Next-working-day is £5.95 by DHL with a 4pm cut-off, where Euro Car Parts takes Express orders to 8pm; a DPD option is quoted at “from £6.95”. Oversized items are £14.95 on both. Car Parts 4 Less warns of an “out-of-area surcharge” of £5.95, which the Euro Car Parts page mentions only as “some out-of-area UK postcodes”. The biggest gap is collection speed: Euro Car Parts says 15 minutes, while Car Parts 4 Less says items “will be available to collect from the next working day if ordered Mon - Fri before 5:30 pm”.

So a £27 basket is delivered free from one of this company's websites and costs £30.95 from the other, and a part needed within the hour is available from one and not the other. Neither site mentions the existence of the other on the pages read for this review, which is the only criticism worth making of an arrangement that is otherwise simply two brands on one warehouse. Check both before you pay; on a small order it is the single biggest saving available here, and it needs no code.

Sixty days to change your mind, unless it is a WS Good

The returns promise is the warmest thing in the paperwork and it deserves saying plainly: “We are happy to offer a refund on unused items returned within 60 days of purchase.” Clause 20.9 sets that against the statutory position in a table — 14 days by law, 60 days by the company's own choice for website and app orders — and describes it as a “goodwill guarantee offered by LKQ Group (UK) Limited … which is more generous than your legal rights under the Consumer Contracts Regulations”. Taking a part back into a branch is free. If the part is faulty, “We will arrange a pick-up service free of charge”. Refunds are “processed within 14 days of us receiving the product back”.

Three qualifications sit further down. A courier collection of something you simply do not want costs £3.45, and if you post it yourself then “You (the customer) are responsible for all postage costs”. The Car Parts 4 Less help centre adds that on an unsuitable item “we will refund the cost of the product only and not the original delivery cost”, and that returns are processed in 5 to 7 working days before the 14-day refund clock is confirmed. So the 60 days described as free in the help article is free in one of the four ways of using it.

The third qualification is the one worth flagging hardest, because it reaches the statutory right as well as the goodwill one. Clause 20.8 removes the 14-day cancellation right entirely for “WS Goods”, and clause 20.9 removes the 60 days for “WS Goods, Performance Goods or Special Order goods”. Performance Goods are defined at clause 18.1 as items “supplied for specialist use” that “usually alter the vehicle from manufacturers' specification”, which is clear enough. A Special Order is defined at clause 4.17 as “any Goods that are not held in stock by us (a non-stock item which may include some WS Goods)”. But WS itself is defined at clause 3.1 only as “Goods and/or Services provided by WS only” — the abbreviation is never expanded anywhere in the terms read for this review. A shopper cannot tell from the contract whether the thing in their basket is a WS Good, and therefore cannot tell whether they have 60 days, 14 days or no cancellation right at all. That is a drafting gap rather than a trap, and asking before you order a non-stock item is the sensible response.

Twelve months as a floor, and a lifetime warranty that will not load

The warranty position is clearer than the terms make it look. Clause 19.1 says only that warranty details “will be provided to you with the Goods or otherwise on request”, and clause 19.2 refers to a “Warranty Period” without defining it — but the help centre fills the gap: “All the products we sell have a minimum of 12 months warranty (unless otherwise stated)”, and both Pagid pad pages print “12 months” on the product itself. That is the figure to hold.

What it covers is narrower than the length suggests. The warranty reaches “parts which are found faulty through manufacturing defects only”, and a claim needs the part “proved to be faulty by the manufacturer during its respective warranty period” — so the decision sits with the brand, not the retailer. The published exclusions are “wear and tear, willful damage, negligence, tampering of goods, abnormal working conditions, incorrect fitment or ordering of goods, or failure to follow the manufacturer's instructions”. Neither the article nor the terms mention labour, and neither mentions consequential loss. Clause 23.5 does the rest of that work: total liability “will not exceed the price paid for the Goods and/or Services in a single Contract”. On the pad in this review that is a ceiling of £32.79, against a garage bill for fitting it twice. Consumer law sits above a clause like that for a consumer buyer, and the terms say so elsewhere, but the clause is what the document leads with.

There is a better warranty in the group and it is hard to read. The trade portal at omnipart.eurocarparts.com publishes a page titled “Euro Car Parts Lifetime Warranty Terms and Conditions”, which on two attempts on 3 November 2026 returned only a loading spinner and no text at all — so which ranges a lifetime warranty covers, what “lifetime” means, and whether labour is in it could not be established and are not claimed here. A shopper who buys from the consumer site is quoted 12 months. If a lifetime warranty applies to the same part, the retail pages do not say so.

Fit It For Me: the shop sells the part, a stranger turns the spanner

Fit It For Me is a neat piece of plumbing. Put a registration in, pick compatible parts, and at the basket the site offers a list of local workshops with AM or PM slots; bookings open up to 14 days ahead and no sooner than 48 hours after ordering; checkout has to be completed inside a 30-minute reservation window; the workshop then confirms, which it “should confirm within 24 hours”, with customer service chasing it if nothing has come back in 3 hours. The parts are delivered to the garage before the appointment. On the day you arrive 15 minutes before opening for a morning slot or at 11:45AM for an afternoon one, and you can leave the car and agree a collection time.

Who you are dealing with changes at the garage door, and the terms are candid about it. Clause 13.8: the garages “are not affiliated, owned, controlled or managed by us” and “Each Garage is its own independent business, separate from LKQ Group (UK) Limited”. You pay the website for the parts and the garage separately for the labour, on the garage's invoice, and “you will be bound by your selected Garage's terms and conditions”. The booking page says each workshop sets its own labour rate and shows it on its listing before booking, with “All prices displayed online … including VAT. This includes prices shown for the labour cost” — so the rate is visible, which is better than most parts sellers that offer fitting at all.

What is not published anywhere is the standard the garages are held to. The service page calls them “trusted garages”, “our selected workshops” and, in the section about things going wrong, “independent garages”; it does not state a vetting process, a qualification requirement, an accreditation, a minimum guarantee on the work, or how many workshops are in the network. For comparison, the Institute of the Motor Industry runs a public Professional Register that “lists individual members and their place of work”, open to members “at associate grade and above and individuals that hold a valid achievement”, and requires a listed member to “give evidence of continued learning and development activities, with a minimum of 90 CPD credits over a 3 year period”. That register has searches by member, business and location, and it is free. Euro Car Parts could point at it and does not — so a buyer who wants to know whether the person fitting their brakes is on it has to check for themselves, which takes about a minute and is worth doing.

The complaints procedure you can find is the one for finance

This company holds a credit permission, and its disclosure document is unusually readable: “LKQ GROUP (UK) LIMITED trading as Euro Car Parts”, FCA registration number 738734, “authorised and regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority for regulated consumer credit activities”. It states its role in six words — “We act a credit broker not a lender” — says it “can introduce you to a limited number of lenders”, discloses that “we receive a commission per finance agreement entered into” calculated as a share of the amount borrowed and that the commission does not change what the customer pays, and confirms that “You will not make any payment to us for introducing you to a finance provider”. Volunteering the commission basis is good practice; the document carries no date and no version number, which is the only mark against it.

Behind that permission sits a full complaints machine. The published procedure runs an informal stage with a Summary Resolution Communication inside three business days; failing that, an initial response letter “within five business days of receiving your complaint” and a final response “no later than eight weeks”; a named Complaints Officer, Thomas Robinson, on 0203 788 7842; and a referral to the Financial Ombudsman Service at Exchange Tower, London E14 9SR, which must be made “within six months of the date of this letter”. It sets out who qualifies — consumers, certain microenterprises, charities with income under £6,500,000, trustees of trusts with assets under £5,000,000, small businesses for conduct after 1 April 2019, and guarantors — and the company undertakes to cooperate with the Ombudsman and be bound by its awards. A complaint is treated as closed if no confirmation arrives within four weeks of the final response.

Read the first line of that document and the asymmetry appears. The procedure covers finance complaints only; for anything else, including a part that fails or a delivery that does not arrive, it gives an email address, custservice@eurocarparts.com, and nothing further. There is no stage, no acknowledgement period, no response deadline, no named officer and no external body. The terms acknowledge the point obliquely at clause 27.2, which tells the customer they “have rights pursuant to section 19(2) of the Alternative Dispute Resolution for Consumer Disputes Regulations 2015” — a duty to inform a consumer about alternative dispute resolution, satisfied without naming a scheme the company belongs to. So if the credit agreement goes wrong there is an ombudsman with a statutory award limit behind it, and if the brake pad goes wrong there is an inbox. That is true of goods retailing in Britain generally rather than peculiar to this shop, but a reader who has just read three paragraphs of Ombudsman detail should know which of their two contracts it applies to.

The parent is listed, which means the only audited numbers anywhere near this business are published four times a year. LKQ Corporation's third-quarter results of 29 October 2026 — five days before this page was written — report Europe third-party revenue of $1,451m against $1,615m, a fall of 10.2%, of which organic parts and services accounted for −9.8%, exchange rates −1.2% and acquisitions +0.8%. Two sentences in that release matter to a customer rather than a shareholder. LKQ attributes the quarter to “operational challenges resulting from the ERP implementation in Germany” and to “volume declines in the United Kingdom and Benelux” — the British business is named. And the company has brought in Alvarez & Marsal “to advise the Company on its European operations on an interim basis”, withdrawn its full-year 2026 guidance, and set out three priorities, the second of which is “Restoring service levels across the region”. A parts distributor's own board saying that service levels need restoring is the most useful warning available about a next-day promise, it is dated, and it is the company's own language rather than anyone's opinion. It should not stop anyone ordering a brake pad for collection from a branch 15 minutes away. It is worth a thought before paying £7.95 for a part that has to arrive today.

Common questions

Is Euro Car Parts the same company as Car Parts 4 Less?

Yes. Both websites are operated by LKQ Group (UK) Limited, company number 02680212, from T2 Birch Coppice Business Park in Tamworth, and both run the same terms and conditions with the same clause numbering. The prices and the carriage differ, though: standard delivery is free above £25 on eurocarparts.com and above £30 on carparts4less.co.uk, click-and-collect is quoted at 15 minutes on one and the next working day on the other, and Car Parts 4 Less names DHL and DPD where Euro Car Parts does not name a carrier. Neither site mentions the other, so it is worth pricing a basket on both.

Do I need a discount code, and which one is the best?

The company's help centre says you do not — it answers “Do I need a Promo Code?” with “Not with Great Value. Always.” — but five codes were live on its own pages on 3 November 2026: SAVE30, SAVE25 and SAVE20 on a ladder of £100, £50 and £20 spends; SAVE20, SAVE15 and SAVE10 on a different ladder; MYSTERY for a free gift with click-and-collect; BRAND on the fitting pages; and SALE24 for 24% off on Car Parts 4 Less. Because the £100 threshold is worth 20% on one page and 30% on another, the only reliable approach is to open two or three category pages, note the codes, and try the best one at the basket.

How long is the warranty on a part from Euro Car Parts?

At least a year. The company states that “All the products we sell have a minimum of 12 months warranty (unless otherwise stated)”, and both Pagid brake pad pages read for this review printed “12 months” on the product itself. The cover is for “parts which are found faulty through manufacturing defects only” and a claim needs the part proved faulty by the manufacturer, with wear and tear, incorrect fitment, negligence, tampering and abnormal working conditions all excluded. Labour is not mentioned, and clause 23.5 caps the company's total liability at the price paid for the part. A separate lifetime warranty document exists on the group's trade portal but would not load on 3 November 2026, so nothing is claimed for it here.

Can I return a part I ordered by mistake?

Usually, and for longer than the law requires: 60 days on unused items bought through the website or app, against the statutory 14 days. Taking it into a branch is free; a courier collection of an unwanted item costs £3.45; posting it yourself is at your own cost; and a faulty part is collected free. Refunds are processed within 14 days of the goods coming back. The 60 days does not apply to WS Goods, Performance Goods or Special Order goods, and the 14-day cancellation right is removed for WS Goods altogether — the terms define a Special Order as a non-stock item, so if the part has to be ordered in, ask what return rights come with it before you pay.

Who actually fits the part if I use Fit It For Me?

An independent garage, not Euro Car Parts. Clause 13.8 states that the garages “are not affiliated, owned, controlled or managed by us” and that each one “is its own independent business”. You pay the website for the parts and the garage separately for the labour on its own invoice, bound by its terms. Bookings run up to 14 days ahead and no sooner than 48 hours after ordering, the workshop confirms within about 24 hours, and the part is delivered to the garage before your slot. Each workshop's labour rate is shown, including VAT, before you book. No vetting standard, qualification requirement or guarantee on the work is published, so it is worth checking the technician against the Institute of the Motor Industry's free Professional Register, which requires listed members to show at least 90 CPD credits over three years.

Is the branch network big enough to collect from?

The company gives the same figure on three separate pages — “250+ Branches Nationwide” on the delivery page, “250+ collection points” on Car Parts 4 Less and “250+ UK branches” in the help centre — which is a consistency worth noting. Collection is free, the delivery page says an order is “available in 15 minutes”, and orders placed after 5pm are processed the next day. One caution: the delivery page says the part is held “until the end of the next working day”, while clause 13.7 of the terms says the branch holds it “for three (3) days or as may be stated on your original order”. Go by the shorter of the two.

Our verdict

On price and on logistics this is the strongest parts proposition in the category. A Pagid brake pad at £32.79 with free delivery over £25, or free collection in 15 minutes from one of more than 250 branches, beats waiting four days for a cheaper part from a warehouse abroad — and the 60-day returns window and the published 12-month minimum warranty are both better than the law requires. Price Lock is a genuinely good idea. The thing to do before paying is open more than one page: on the afternoon this was checked the shop was running six different sales, a £100 basket was worth 20% off on one page and 30% on another, and the same company's other website, carparts4less.co.uk, sets its free-delivery line at £30 instead of £25 with next-day collection instead of 15 minutes.
