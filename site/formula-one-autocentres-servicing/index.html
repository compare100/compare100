Formula One Autocentres Servicing: The Prices, the 40-Point List and the Ombudsman Nobody Mentions

Rates and terms checked 14 November 2026 · Servicing · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

A full service at Formula One Autocentres is priced at £135 for an engine up to 1000cc and £175 at 2500cc, with the interim service running from £70 to £110. On the same 12,000-mile schedule that is £111.95 below the cheapest full service at Kwik Fit and £144.99 below National Tyres, on the figures this site last checked at both chains on 8 September 2026.

The chain says it was established in 1969, that it is still family owned, and that it now runs “over 130 state-of-the-art branches” and “over 800 highly trained staff”, open seven days a week. Its own about page places those branches “across England”, so anyone booking from Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland should check the centre finder before planning the trip.

The better thing in its favour is one it never advertises. Its branches sit on The Motor Ombudsman’s public register under the Service and Repair Code, which the Chartered Trading Standards Institute approves, and clause 6.7 of that code commits an accredited garage to “a legally binding final decision of the Ombudsman”. Nothing on the company’s website says so.

What £65 buys, whatever is under the bonnet

The booking page prints the whole grid rather than a “from” price, which is more than most of the trade does. A Short Car Service is £70 up to 1000cc, £80 to 1300cc, £90 to 1600cc, £100 to 2000cc and £110 to 2500cc. A Master Car Service is £135, £145, £155, £165 and £175 across the same five bands.

Two patterns fall straight out of it. Each step up in engine size adds exactly £10, in both tiers. And the gap between the two tiers is exactly £65 at every band — so the extra checks, the air filter, the spark plugs and the pollen filter are charged at a flat £65 on a one-litre city car and on a 2.5-litre estate alike. A grid that regular is a priced product rather than a quoted job, and it is the reason the figures can be compared with anybody.

What the grid does not reach is anything bigger or more complicated: “For hybrid vehicles and vehicles above 2500cc call your local centre with details”. For a large share of cars sold in the past decade that means the published price list stops before the car does, and there is no indication of what the phone will quote.

The service intervals are the trade standard — the interim “every 6,000 miles or every 6 months” and the full “every 12,000 miles or every 12 months (whichever comes first)”. Kwik Fit and National Tyres run their interim and full services to the same two clocks, which is what makes the price comparison a like-for-like one rather than a cheaper schedule dressed up as a cheaper service.

Twenty checks, then eighteen more

Both checklists are published in full before you book, item by item, which is unusually open. The interim list is a road test, six things looked at with the car on the floor — exterior lights, wipers, the front washer, the rear washer and wiper, the fan or auxiliary belt and the instrument panel warning lights — nine with it on the ramp, including wheel alignment and geometry, wheel bearings, tyres, shock absorbers and springs, road wheel balance, tyre pressures, brake pads or shoes, brake discs or drums and the exhaust system, and four service tasks: engine oil and filter, coolant level, brake fluid level and anti-freeze strength.

Getting a wheel alignment check and a road wheel balance inside a £70 service is more than the price suggests. It is worth reading the service tasks closely, though: apart from the engine oil and filter, every one of them is a level or a strength rather than a change. Brake fluid is checked, not replaced; anti-freeze is tested, not renewed.

The full service adds seat belts and the power steering reservoir on the floor; steering gaiters and joints, propshaft and suspension joints, driveshaft gaiters, the clutch, handbrake linkages and customer-reported items on the ramp; the air filter, battery and terminals, door hinges and the bonnet catch among the service tasks; and four scheduled tasks — timing belt interval, pollen filter interval, spark plugs and a service light reset.

Note the word “interval”. The timing belt line checks whether the belt is due, not that it is changed, and a belt replacement is the most expensive single job on a service schedule and the most expensive one to miss. The pollen filter is written the same way. So of the four scheduled tasks, two are diary entries and two are work. The company states plainly that “we won’t carry out any extra work without your approval”, which is what clauses 3.2 and 4.4 of the code it is accredited to require of it.

A 40-point service with 38 points in it

The full-service checklist is published under the heading “40-Point Full Service”. Counted line by line it holds 39 entries, and one of them — brake discs and drums — appears twice inside the on-ramp block. That leaves 38 distinct checks against a number the product is named after, and a second page on the same site repeats the claim, describing “a 40-point check compared to the 20-point Short Service”.

The interim list, headed “20-Point Interim Service”, comes to exactly 20. So the counting is right on one list and two out on the other, which makes it look like a drafting slip rather than a sales decision. It is still the number on the tin, it is the difference the £65 is sold on, and it is the sort of thing a reader can check in two minutes before handing over the keys.

The ombudsman that will bind the garage, and the website that never mentions it

Formula One Autocentres branches appear on The Motor Ombudsman’s business register, accredited to its Service and Repair Code, and each site carries its own accreditation number: Bolton, at 113–115 Manchester Road, is #109970, and Welwyn Garden City, at 1 South Mundells, is #134999. That is worth knowing in itself — accreditation is held branch by branch, not by the chain, so the register has to be checked for the actual centre you are booking rather than for the name over the door.

The code is approved by the Chartered Trading Standards Institute and it is specific. An estimate “will be supplied as a breakdown of costs to be provided in writing” (2.8) and a quotation “in writing as a breakdown of the firm agreed price” (2.9). Additional work needs the customer contacted “for authorisation prior to commencement” (3.2 and 4.4). Replaced parts “will be made available for you to view and examine until collection of the vehicle” (2.5 and 3.6). Final invoices must list the work done with parts, labour, environmental disposal charges and VAT shown separately (4.5). A complaint should be acknowledged within ten working days and answered finally within eight weeks, and a consumer then has one year to take it onward.

Clause 6.7 is the one that matters: “the accredited business will submit to a legally binding final decision of the Ombudsman”. The decision binds all parties once the consumer accepts it, so the customer keeps the option of rejecting it and going to court instead, and the service costs the consumer nothing. More than 7,500 retailers and repairers are accredited across the Ombudsman’s four codes.

The scheme is not a formality. It opened on 1 November 2016 on roughly 20,000 contacts and 1,000 cases in its first year, and it expects “in excess of 300,000 contacts” and “more than 55,000 cases by year-end” in 2026 — although the summary bullet on that same announcement says 50,000, so even the Ombudsman has two figures for it. Under the Service and Repair Code alone it logged 1,604 new cases in the first quarter of 2024, 35% up on 1,189 a year earlier and past a previous high of 1,372; 1,657 in the second quarter; 1,586 in the third; and 4,847 across the first nine months, 29% up on 3,763. In that third quarter, drivetrain complaints made up just under 40% of submissions, down from 51%, while the standard of a repairer’s customer service jumped from 17% to 32%; chassis took 9%, electrical systems 8%, exterior fixtures 8% and interiors 4%. The average amount in dispute was £3,689, down 13% from £4,258, and the remedy customers asked for most often was a free-of-charge repair, at 31%, ahead of compensation at 23% and a full refund at 19%.

None of this appears on the company’s own pages. The customer careline page invites complaints about “any aspect of the work we carry out” and then gives a phone number, 01438 746633, and hours of 9am to 5pm Monday to Friday — no stages, no timescales, no body named. The MOT terms, the payments and cancellations terms and the contracts terms name no ombudsman or dispute scheme either, and the payments page carries a “Motor Industry Code of Practice” badge with no name or web address beside it; the complaints policy linked from the MOT terms returned an error and could not be read. Regulation 19(1) of the Alternative Dispute Resolution for Consumer Disputes (Competent Authorities and Information) Regulations 2015 asks a trader that is obliged to use an ADR body to give “the name and website address of the ADR entity” on its website and in its general terms; accreditation here is voluntary rather than obliged, so this is not a breach. It is simply a free remedy that a customer reading only the trader’s website would never find.

Over 80, over 100, over 130

Three of the company’s own pages give three different sizes for the same business. The about page claims “over 130 state-of-the-art branches across England” and “over 800 highly trained staff”. The MOT booking page says the company has “over 80” testing stations “across the UK”. The MOT information page says “over 100 state-of-the-art MOT testing stations situated across the UK”.

Not every branch has to be an MOT station, so 80 or 100 against 130 is no contradiction on its own. But 80 and 100 cannot both be the number of test stations, and England and the UK cannot both be the territory. The reader-facing point survives either reading: somewhere between 30 and 50 branches do not test, so a service and an MOT booked without checking will be two journeys rather than one. The centre finder that would settle both questions returned an error throughout 14 November 2026 and could not be read.

No price for the test, two prices for getting it wrong

Across the MOT booking page, the MOT information page and the MOT terms and conditions, the company publishes no price for an MOT test at all — only a direction to the price promise or to “your nearest MOT test centre for further information on prices”. The single money figure it puts beside an MOT test anywhere is on its FAQ page, and it is not its own: “the maximum charge you would receive for an MOT test is £54.85”. That is the statutory ceiling for a class 4 car with up to eight passenger seats, which also sets £57.30 for a nine-to-twelve-seat class 4, £64 where a seat belt installation check is included, £58.60 for a class 7 goods vehicle between 3,000kg and 3,500kg and £29.65 for a motorcycle. Quoting the cap tells a customer what the garage may not exceed, not what it charges.

The two MOT prices the company does print are both for things going wrong. A partial retest costs £14.75 if the car comes back within ten working days of failing, and cancelling an MOT booking inside 24 hours of the appointment costs £19.95.

The free retest is described twice, and the two descriptions disagree. The MOT terms say a partial retest carries no fee where the vehicle is left at the centre for repair and “retested before the end of 7 working days following the day it originally failed”, and no fee where it is brought back to the same centre “before the end of the next working day” for a limited list of items that includes access panels, the battery, doors, mirrors, seatbelts, windscreen and glass, and wheels and tyres. The MOT information page says the opposite: “a retest fee will apply if your vehicle is removed from the test centre for repair”. Whichever page a branch has in front of it is the one that decides the bill.

The classes are muddled too. The MOT terms say “we currently only offer MOT testing for cars and light commercial vehicles” and then, lower down the same page, that “MOTs for vans and bikes are not available”. A van is a light commercial vehicle. The MOT information page mentions only cars. Phone the branch before driving a van to one.

Match the price, and 10% of the difference

The price promise is the boldest claim on the site and, on the face of it, the most generous of the three chains: “Find any competitor’s substantiated, comparable quote* cheaper and we’ll match the price PLUS give you 10% of the difference.” The servicing and MOT pages carry the shorter version, “we simply WON’T be beaten on price!”

The asterisk leads to “Terms & conditions apply”, and the two addresses the site’s own index gives for those terms both returned errors on 14 November 2026, so the conditions could not be read. Nothing that can be read defines “substantiated” or “comparable”, sets a distance within which a competitor counts, puts a time limit on a claim, or says whether servicing and MOTs are covered as well as tyres. A promise that pays out 10% of the difference is a real commitment and it deserves readable terms; as things stand it is the only claim on the site whose small print a customer cannot check before booking.

One in five cars fails even after the garage has had a go

The scale of the work is public. In the 2025 to 2026 financial year, 33,310,534 MOT tests were carried out on classes 3 and 4 — cars, vans and passenger vehicles with up to 12 seats — out of 35,480,950 across every class. The initial failure rate for those classes was 27.51%, and the final failure rate, after items put right at the station and after retests, was still 22.30%. Rather more than one car in five that is presented for a test ends the process having failed it.

The defect tables for the latest quarter, April to June 2026, put the initial failure rate at 26.75% and show where the quieter problems sit: body, chassis and structure on 3.08% of all tests, noise, emissions and leaks on 3.04%, steering on 1.81%, seat belts and supplementary restraint systems on 0.90%, road wheels on 0.39% and the identification of the vehicle itself on 0.32%.

Read those against the two checklists and the £65 starts to look like a straightforward trade rather than an upsell. The interim service looks at tyres, brakes, shock absorbers and springs and the exhaust. Seat belts, steering gaiters and joints, propshaft and suspension joints and the handbrake linkage appear only on the full one — three of the defect categories above among them.

Leaside Tyre & Exhaust, and the decade before it

The company is on the register as Formula One Auto Centres Limited, number 01437311, at Aston House, Boulton Road, Stevenage SG1 4QX, active, under SIC 45200 for the maintenance and repair of motor vehicles. It was incorporated on 16 July 1979 and traded as Leaside Tyre & Exhaust Centre Limited until 9 November 1987. Accounts were last made up to 31 July 2025, the next are due for the year to 31 July 2026 by 30 April 2027, and the next confirmation statement is dated 29 December 2026 for filing by 12 January 2027. There is no strike-off, administration or liquidation filing on the record. Two further companies sit at the same registered office: Formula Autocentres Limited (09109003) and Formula Plus Autocentres Limited (09108967).

The about page says the business was “established in 1969”, a decade before this company existed. Trading under earlier ownership or a different structure is perfectly ordinary and the register cannot confirm or deny it, so the honest reading is that “over fifty years” belongs to the business and 1979 belongs to the company. It is worth a moment of a reader’s time before taking either number as the age of the firm their money goes to.

One narrower point follows from the same records. Regulation 25 of the Company, Limited Liability Partnership and Business (Names and Trading Disclosures) Regulations 2015 requires a company to give the part of the United Kingdom in which it is registered, its registered number and the address of its registered office on its websites and order forms, and the contracts page does exactly that, naming the number 1437311 and the Stevenage address. Regulation 24(2) asks for something slightly different — “every company shall disclose its registered name on its websites” — and the name used throughout the terms is Formula One Autocentres Limited, which is not a name on the register at all. The number settles who the contract is with; the name on the contract does not.

A last practical note from the front page, carrying no date: “our website Tyre ordering service is temporarily suspended”, with customers asked to “place a Tyre order” by calling the local centre. The servicing and MOT booking journeys were working normally.

Common questions

How much is a full service at Formula One Autocentres?

£135 for an engine up to 1000cc, then £145 to 1300cc, £155 to 1600cc, £165 to 2000cc and £175 to 2500cc, as published on the company’s own booking page on 14 November 2026. Hybrids and engines above 2500cc are not priced online; the site asks you to call the branch. The interim service runs £70, £80, £90, £100 and £110 across the same bands, so the gap between the two tiers is a flat £65 whatever the engine.

What is the difference between the Short and Master services?

The interim covers 20 published checks — a road test, six items with the car on the floor, nine on the ramp and four service tasks, of which only the engine oil and filter is a replacement. The full service adds seat belts, the power steering reservoir, steering gaiters and joints, propshaft and suspension joints, driveshaft gaiters, the clutch, handbrake linkages, the air filter, battery and terminals, door hinges and the bonnet catch, plus spark plugs, a service light reset and interval checks on the timing belt and pollen filter. Note that the belt and filter lines are interval checks, not replacements.

What does an MOT cost there?

The company does not publish a price. Its MOT booking page, MOT information page and MOT terms all refer you to the price promise or to your nearest centre instead, and the only figure printed beside an MOT test anywhere on the site is the £54.85 statutory maximum for a class 4 car, which is a ceiling rather than a price. It does publish £14.75 for a partial retest within ten working days and £19.95 for cancelling inside 24 hours. Ask for the price on the phone, and ask whether your branch tests at all — the company claims over 130 branches but only 80 or 100 test stations, depending on which of its pages you read.

What happens if the work is wrong?

More than you would guess from the website. Branches are accredited to The Motor Ombudsman’s Service and Repair Code, approved by the Chartered Trading Standards Institute, under which a complaint should be acknowledged within ten working days and answered finally within eight weeks, after which you have a year to refer it onward. Clause 6.7 commits the accredited business to “a legally binding final decision of the Ombudsman”, and the service is free to you; you can reject the decision and go to court instead. Accreditation sits with each branch rather than the chain, so check the register for your own centre — Bolton is #109970 and Welwyn Garden City #134999 — because nothing on the company’s own site mentions the scheme at all.

Will servicing here affect a manufacturer’s warranty?

Having a car serviced away from a franchised dealer does not of itself void a manufacturer warranty, provided the schedule is followed and parts of matching quality are used. The practical question is the paperwork, and on that the servicing pages that could be read say nothing about whether a manufacturer digital service record can be updated — the nearest thing on the full-service checklist is a service light reset. For a car with no paper logbook, ask the branch directly how the service will be recorded before you book.

Is the price promise worth anything?

On its wording it is the most generous of the three chains: match a cheaper “substantiated, comparable quote” and pay 10% of the difference on top. The problem is evidence. The asterisk leads to “Terms & conditions apply” and both addresses the site’s own index gives for those terms returned errors on 14 November 2026, so nothing readable says what counts as substantiated or comparable, how far away a competitor may be, or how long you have to claim. Take a written quote from the other garage with you and settle the conditions at the counter before the work starts.

Our verdict

On price this is the clearest result in the category. A full service at £135 to £175 and an interim at £70 to £110, on the same six and twelve-month schedules the dearer chains use, undercuts Kwik Fit’s cheapest full service by £111.95 and National Tyres’ by £144.99, and the entire grid is published rather than hidden behind a “from” price. The checklists are published too, so the £65 between the two tiers can be judged before booking rather than after. What makes the chain more interesting than its prices is the thing it does not advertise: its branches are accredited to a Chartered Trading Standards Institute-approved code whose clause 6.7 binds the garage to an Ombudsman’s final decision, free to the customer, and that is a stronger backstop than either of its larger rivals could be shown to have. The paperwork, though, is some way behind the counter. The MOT price is nowhere, the retest rule is given two opposite ways on two of its own pages, three pages give three sizes for the estate, the 40-point service lists 38 checks, and the price promise asterisk leads to terms that cannot be read. None of that makes the work worse, and none of it is expensive to fix. Book the service, take a written estimate under clause 2.8 before anything is touched, confirm the MOT price and whether your branch tests at all by telephone, and check your own centre’s accreditation number on the Ombudsman’s register while you are at it.
