What £39.99 to £109.99 gets you, measured

Game Over publishes a dimension for every seat it sells, which sounds unremarkable until you try to find the same figures on a marketplace listing. The Classic Gaming Bean Bag is L83cm x W60cm x H83cm and costs £74.99. The 8-Bit compact chair is L73cm x W49cm x H73cm at £54.99. The Lounger stretches to L120cm and stands 83cm high at £109.99, and the footstool is W60cm x H42cm x D40cm at £44.99. The printed cushions are L56cm x H27cm and £22.99 each.

The Mini Gaming Bean Bag is the outlier at 51 x 47 x 46cm and £39.99, sold as a saddle-shaped seat for children aged six to thirteen. It is the cheapest way into the range and the only product on the site aimed at a specific age band.

Construction is the same story across the range: a water-resistant polyester outer, double-stitched, filled with fire-retardant EPS polystyrene beads, with pockets, a velcro headphone hook and metal air vents. The fabric is water-resistant rather than waterproof — the shop says so itself — and the cleaning instruction is a damp cloth, not a washing machine. On a £74.99 chair that a teenager will eat crisps in, that is worth knowing before rather than after.

The beads are the part the law actually tests

A bean bag has no financial regulator, no ombudsman and no approved dispute scheme behind it. It does have one set of mandatory rules, and they are about fire. The Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988 cover bean bags and floor cushions by name, and what they regulate is the filling: a non-foam filling material has to be tested to BS 5852: Part 2 using ignition source 2, and the finished item has to carry a durable permanent label securely attached to an outside surface.

Those regulations changed recently. The Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) (Amendment) Regulations 2025 were made on 28 April 2025 and came into force on 30 October 2025. They removed the separate display-label requirement at the point of sale, narrowed the definition of furniture so that products intended for babies and very young children fall outside it, and doubled the window for bringing a prosecution from six months to twelve. The permanent label on the item survived all of that; it is still the thing to look for when the parcel arrives.

The reason this is not a technicality is in the Office for Product Safety and Standards research report of April 2023. Upholstered items were the material first ignited in 12% of domestic fire incidents but accounted for 29% of the deaths, and were the main material driving fire development in 16% of fires but 43% of the deaths. The same report classifies bean bags as upholstered items, usually larger than 60cm x 60cm, filled with expanded polystyrene beads. Fire deaths across Great Britain fell from a peak of about 1,100 in 1980 to roughly 300 in 2019, and tighter furniture rules are part of why.

Game Over describes its beads as fire-retardant on every product page. It does not publish a test reference, a standard number or the wording of the permanent label anywhere on the site. Asking for it costs an email, and a seller who cannot produce it has a problem: an offence under section 12 of the Consumer Protection Act 1987 carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine at level 5 on the standard scale, or both.

Refilling one, and what doing it properly costs

Every bean bag flattens. Game Over is straightforward about it — its own answers say the filling will compress with use and a top-up will be needed, and that a heavy-duty zip at the base is there for the purpose. The Classic chair also has an inner liner, so the beads are held in a bag inside the cover rather than loose against the zip.

The shop sells beads by volume and publishes a capacity guide: 12 cubic feet fills a bean bag, 15 fills a Lounger, 3.5 fills a footstool. A 5 cubic foot bag is £19.99, reduced from £21.99, and 10 cubic feet is £29.99, reduced from £32.99. There is a 2.5 cubic foot option listed at the same £19.99 as the 5 cubic foot bag, which is odd pricing; it was showing as sold out on 18 September 2026.

Run the arithmetic before you assume refills are cheap. Filling a Classic chair from empty needs 12 cubic feet, which means the 10 and the 5 together at £49.98 — two thirds of what the chair itself costs. A Lounger takes exactly 15 cubic feet, the same £49.98, against a £109.99 purchase price. A genuine top-up after a couple of years is a £19.99 job and that is the realistic case, but anyone planning to strip and refill a bag completely should price it first.

Game Over Ltd on paper

The register tells a short, consistent story. Game Over Ltd was incorporated on 17 February 2020 and filed dormant accounts for the years to 28 February 2023 and 29 February 2024. On 26 February 2025 it shortened its accounting period to 31 December 2024, and on 29 September 2025 it filed micro-entity accounts for that year. Micro-entity accounts do not include a turnover figure, so nobody outside the company knows what it sells in a year — that is the filing option a small company is entitled to use, not evidence of anything being hidden. Its latest confirmation statement was made on 10 January 2026 and the next set of accounts is due by 30 September 2026. A director was appointed on 3 January 2024 and another terminated six days later.

One loose thread is worth flagging rather than asserting. The shop's contact page lists social accounts under two different names, gameover_uk on some platforms and LOFT25Gaming on others. Loft 25 is a separate Birmingham bean bag and soft-furnishings brand; Loft 25 Ltd, company 06170952, was incorporated on 20 March 2007 as Changing Sofas Limited, renamed on 4 October 2019 and dissolved on 1 January 2026, while Loft 25 Group Ltd, company 12931054, remains active as a holding company at a different Birmingham address. No connection between the two businesses is published on either site, and the shared handle is suggestive rather than conclusive. It needs confirming with the company before anyone treats it as fact.

Where the price tags and the refund policy contradict each other

On 18 September 2026 every one of the 28 items in the shop's all-products collection carried a struck-through comparison price. Cushions were £22.99 from £25.29, the Classic £74.99 from £84.97, the Lounger £109.99 from £150.00, the blanket £29.99 from £32.99. The footstool was £44.99 from £45.99, which is a reduction of one pound presented as a sale.

The Chartered Trading Standards Institute's Guidance for Traders on Pricing Practices, free at businesscompanion.info and updated on 14 August 2025 to take in the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, sets out the test. A price comparison should run for a period no longer than the higher price was actually charged, and a recommended retail price must not be false and must not be created purely to present the appearance of a discount. That is a test a shopper can run without any help: note today's prices, come back in a fortnight, and see whether the sale ever ends.

The sharper problem is what the refund policy does with those price tags. It says, in terms, that only regular priced items may be refunded and that sale items cannot be. If every product in the shop is displayed as a sale item, that sentence refuses refunds on the entire catalogue.

It also claims more than the law allows. Under the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, a consumer buying at distance has 14 days from the day the goods arrive to cancel, and regulation 28 — the list of exceptions to that right — contains nothing at all about sale, clearance or discounted goods. Made-to-measure items are excepted, perishables are excepted, sealed goods unsealed after delivery are excepted. A discounted bean bag is not. To be fair to the shop, its 30-day window is more generous than the statutory 14 days; the carve-out inside it is the part that does not stand up.

Two smaller things sit in the same small print. Return postage is the buyer's responsibility and is deducted from the refund, which is lawful if you are told in advance but expensive on an item the size of a Lounger. And the terms of service say the agreement is "governed by and construed in accordance with the laws of 15a Alexandra Trading Estate, Alexandra Road, Birmingham, B210PG" — an address where a jurisdiction should be, which is store-platform boilerplate nobody finished filling in.

That same address is worth a second look. The site prints the returns postcode as B210PG. Companies House records the registered office as B21 0PD. One letter separates them, and it is the address a customer posts a £74.99 chair back to.

A bean bag for a six-year-old, and a warning Britain does not require

The Mini Gaming Bean Bag at £39.99 is sold for children aged six to thirteen, and the shop's own answers describe the range as suitable for all ages. That makes the safety question concrete rather than theoretical, because loose polystyrene beads are a choking and suffocation risk to small children and a bean bag is a container a child can climb into.

Australia has had a mandatory rule about this since 2014. Its Consumer Goods (Bean Bags) Safety Standard requires a child-resistant slide fastener on every opening through which filling can be added or removed — in practice a zip with the tag removed that locks shut — and a warning in fixed words on the bag, on any cover and on the packaging of refill beads: "WARNING: Children can suffocate if bean bag filling is swallowed or inhaled. Do not let children climb inside this bean bag. A bean bag is not a safe sleeping surface for an infant under 12 months of age."

Britain has no bean-bag-specific standard of that kind. What reaches a bean bag here is the 1988 fire regulations and the general duty on any consumer product to be safe. Game Over's design helps on the main risk — the inner liner keeps beads away from the outer zip — but its own answers describe a heavy-duty zip at the base for refilling, nothing on the product pages says whether that fastener is child-resistant, and no suffocation warning appears anywhere on the site. Neither point is an allegation, and both are worth an email before buying the Mini for a young child.

Common questions

How much do Game Over bean bags cost?

On 18 September 2026 the Mini for children aged six to thirteen was £39.99, the compact 8-Bit chair £54.99, the Classic £74.99, the Cloudrunner limited edition £89.99 and the Gaming Lounger £109.99. Footstools were £44.99 and printed cushions £22.99. Delivery to mainland Britain is free on all of them.

What is inside them, and are they fire-safe?

They are filled with EPS polystyrene beads that Game Over describes as fire-retardant, inside a water-resistant, double-stitched polyester cover. Bean bags are named in the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988, which require the filling to pass BS 5852: Part 2 at ignition source 2 and the finished item to carry a permanent label. Game Over does not publish that test reference, so ask for it and check the label on the item when it arrives.

How much filling does one take, and what do refills cost?

Game Over's capacity guide gives 12 cubic feet for a bean bag, 15 for the Lounger and 3.5 for a footstool. Beads are £19.99 for 5 cubic feet and £29.99 for 10. A routine top-up is therefore about £20; filling a Classic chair from empty needs both bags at £49.98, which is two thirds of the price of the chair.

Can I send one back if I change my mind?

The shop advertises 30 days, which is longer than the law requires, but its policy also says sale items cannot be refunded — and every item in the shop was showing a sale price when this page was checked. Under the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013 you have 14 days from delivery to cancel a distance purchase, and regulation 28 lists no exception for discounted goods. Return postage is the buyer's and comes out of the refund.

Is a bean bag safe for a young child?

The risk is loose beads being swallowed or inhaled, and a child climbing inside. Australia has required a child-resistant zip and a fixed suffocation warning on bean bags since 2014; Britain has no equivalent standard, and no such warning appears on Game Over's pages. The Classic chair's inner liner reduces the exposure. If the Mini is for a six-year-old, ask the company directly whether the base fastener is child-resistant before ordering.

Our verdict

Game Over is a small Birmingham manufacturer that publishes more hard detail than most shops its size: prices, dimensions, filling volumes, dispatch times and a 24-month guarantee on its main chair. At £74.99 for the Classic and £39.99 for the children's Mini, with free mainland delivery and beads from £19.99 to keep them going, the product side of the proposition is honest and easy to check. The paperwork is where it slips. A refund policy that excludes sale items, in a shop where everything is a sale item, is both self-defeating and wider than the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013 allow; the missing company number, the one-letter postcode difference and the unfinished governing-law clause all point the same way, at small-print nobody has read since the store was set up. None of that makes the bean bags worse, and all of it is fixable in an afternoon. Ask for the fire-safety label and the returns address in writing before you order, and buy on the product rather than the terms.
