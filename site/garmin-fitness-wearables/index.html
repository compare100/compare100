Garmin Fitness Wearables: Cover, ECG Accuracy and the Aviation Standard Behind the Brand

Rates and terms checked 14 November 2026 · Fitness & Wearables · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Garmin's third quarter to 26 September 2026 was a record: consolidated revenue of $1.99 billion, up 12%, at a 63.3% gross margin, with the Fitness division that builds its watches contributing $694.3 million of it at a 33% operating margin. That is roughly 35% of the company by sales, on our own division of its published figures, and it sits inside a business that also makes certified aircraft instruments and marine electronics.

That mix is the reason to take Garmin seriously on a wrist. Battery life is measured in days rather than hours — the CIRQA Smart Band is sold on “up to 10 days battery”, “24/7 health & sleep tracking” and “automatic activity detection”, and Garmin markets it in plain terms as a “subscription-free fitness tracker”. Delivery is free on UK orders over £30, Klarna is accepted, and the current promotion extends returns until 15/01/2027. On the one health feature a regulator has actually assessed, Garmin publishes its own validation numbers, including the unflattering one.

One thing to know before you shop: Garmin's own UK site does not print a price. Its product pages serve the currency code GBP with no value against it, and the category, warranty, terms of sale and subscription pages all return a notice that they do not work without JavaScript enabled. So the price you pay, and the paperwork you can read before paying it, are both set by whoever sells you the watch rather than by Garmin.

One year on your wrist, five on a garden speaker

Garmin writes its own warranties, and it writes several. The leaflet that ships with a fitness watch — the Important Safety and Product Information booklet, the regulated disclosure a buyer is actually handed — says this: “Non-aviation products are warranted to be free from defects in materials or workmanship for one year from the date of purchase.” That is the whole period. The booklet names no separate term for the United Kingdom or the European Economic Area; the only 24-month figure in it belongs to Italian law, which gives a consumer 24 months from delivery whatever the manufacturer offers.

Now set that against the documents Garmin publishes for its other divisions, which are UK-specific, carry revision numbers and run to numbered clauses. The marine and audio warranty policy marked EMEA Rev. B (02/2025) gives five years on Pavilion outdoor speakers, three years on marine audio, powersports audio and home audio subwoofers and electronics, and two years on car amplifiers, enclosed subwoofers and connection products — extended to three where the amplifier is paired with a qualifying JL Audio power connection system and fitted by an authorised installer. The trolling motor policy, EMEA Rev. B (03/2025), gives three years for recreational, non-commercial use, with composite shafts covered for the original customer's lifetime, dropping to one year for any commercial use.

Both of those documents also say, at clause 1.1.4, that the warranty “does not affect the customer's statutory rights under applicable laws”, and the marine policy states in capitals that Garmin “DOES NOT EXCLUDE, LIMIT OR SUSPEND OTHER LEGAL RIGHTS YOU MAY HAVE”. The watch leaflet carries no equivalent clause. So the ladder across one manufacturer's own paperwork runs five years, three years, two years, one year — and the device strapped to you all day sits level with a car speaker at the bottom of it. Nothing about that is unlawful: the Consumer Rights Act 2015 stands behind the sale whatever the guarantee says. It is simply a choice Garmin has made and published, in its own words, in four different documents.

What Garmin has to prove to the Civil Aviation Authority

The comparison worth making is not with another watch brand. It is with Garmin's own aviation business, because the same company sells into both and the standard of proof is not remotely the same.

To get a stand-alone approval for a piece of cockpit equipment in the UK, a manufacturer applies for a UK Technical Standard Order Authorisation. The CAA's own description of what that takes: the applicant must “demonstrate to the CAA the equipment meets a prescribed” Minimum Operational Performance Specification; the authorisation is granted under UK Regulation (EU) No 748/2012, Part 21 Subpart O; the acceptable specifications “include those within or referenced from CS-ETSO”; any deviation has to be justified as giving an equivalent level of safety; and the applicant needs both a production approval and “a design organisation approval (DOA or ADOA) that are acceptable to CAA”. Even then the approval does not permit installation on its own — the installer has to obtain a separate installation approval for the aircraft. EASA puts the same point about its European equivalent plainly: an ETSO “ensures that a part or appliance complies with a minimum performance standard”, and it is “one way (and not the only way) to have parts and appliances approved”.

Garmin has been through that process repeatedly on the European side — its own release of 22 March 2017 announcing EASA approval of the GTX 345 and GTX 335 all-in-one ADS-B transponders for fixed-wing general aviation aircraft and helicopters is one of many. Worth being precise about the UK, though: the CAA's Register of UK Technical Standard Order Authorisations, updated 2 November 2026, names 29 current holders and five surrendered ones — Acro Aircraft Seating, AmSafe Bridport, Collins Aerospace, Chelton, Dunlop Aircraft Tyres, Eaton, GE Aviation Systems, HeartSine, Honeywell, Martin-Baker, Meggitt, Thales and the rest — and Garmin is not among them. Several register entries carry EASA.21O reference numbers, which suggests the CAA is recognising European authorisations rather than duplicating them, but the register does not explain itself and we are not going to assert a route it does not state.

The contrast stands either way. In the cockpit, a number has to be shown against a prescribed specification, with an approved design organisation behind it. On the wrist, the same company's booklet says the optical heart rate monitor and pulse oximeter are “intended to be used only for recreational purposes, not medical purposes”, that “the heart rate and blood oxygen saturation readings are for reference only”, and that “there are inherent limitations with the technologies that may cause some of the readings to be inaccurate”. No specification, no authorisation, no register. That is the honest position for a fitness feature, and it is worth knowing that the company telling you so holds itself to the other standard three divisions over.

Eleven and a half per cent of readings came back inconclusive

There is one exception on a Garmin watch, and Garmin documents it properly. The ECG app is a medical device, and its Instructions for Use — GPN 190-02816-92, Revision AM, dated 23 September 2026 — reads like one.

The intended purpose is to “create, record, store, transfer, and display a single-channel electrocardiograph similar to a Lead I ECG”, for home use by people aged 22 and over, with no clinical condition required. The validation study ran on about 590 subjects. In recordings the app could classify, it identified atrial fibrillation correctly 99.5% of the time and sinus rhythm 100% of the time, and it agreed with board-certified cardiologists reading 12-lead traces 96% of the time.

Then comes the figure that matters and that no competitor page quotes. 11.5% of recordings were inconclusive — roughly one in nine, on our arithmetic. Count those in, as a user holding a watch has to, and AFib detection falls to 86.5% and sinus rhythm detection to 91.1%. Garmin prints both pairs of numbers itself, which is more than most makers of anything do.

The contraindications are longer than most people expect from a watch feature, and they are stated as flat prohibitions. “DO NOT use if you are younger than 22 years old.” “DO NOT use if you have a known arrythmia other than AFib.” “DO NOT use if you have a cardiac pacemaker, implanted cardiac defibrillator, or other implanted electronic devices.” “DO NOT use if you are pregnant.” “DO NOT use for clinical diagnosis of heart-related conditions. ECG app results are NOT a diagnosis.” “DO NOT use for continuous, real-time monitoring of heart rhythm.” The second of those is the one to sit with: a reader who already knows they have an arrhythmia that is not AFib — the group most likely to buy a watch for their heart — is told not to use the feature. The warnings add that the app “CANNOT check for signs of a heart attack” and cannot detect blood clots, stroke, high blood pressure, congestive heart failure, high cholesterol or other arrhythmias, and that results “are only potential findings”. It will not work below 10 °C or above 45 °C, or outside 10% to 95% humidity, and you need a bare thumb and index finger free of bruises, scars or cuts.

A device cleared in five countries, and a UK address that is not there

The same document is where the United Kingdom goes missing. Its intended-purpose section names the European Union, Switzerland, South Africa, the United Arab Emirates and the United Kingdom. Its regulatory block then names an authorised representative for the European Union (Emergo Europe, Westervoortsedijk 60, Arnhem), for Switzerland (MedEnvoy Switzerland, Zug), for Australia (Emergo Australia, Sydney), for Israel (I.L. Emergo Israel Ltd, Haifa) and for Brazil (Emergo Brazil Import, São Paulo). The manufacturer is Garmin International, Inc., 1200 E. 151st St, Olathe, Kansas. The document carries the CE mark and no UKCA mark, and it names no UK Responsible Person at all.

That matters because Great Britain runs its own regime: a non-UK manufacturer placing a medical device on the GB market has to appoint a UK Responsible Person who holds the declaration of conformity and the technical file. Whether Garmin has one could not be established on this run — garmin.com/en-GB/legal/compliance/ would not open, returning only its title and footer, and the page Garmin's in-box booklet points at for the full declaration behaves the same way. The booklet itself says only, for the UK, that “Garmin declares that this product is in compliance with the relevant statutory requirements”, against a specific citation of Directive 2014/53/EU for the EU. So the position is a gap in the published paperwork rather than a proven breach, and that is exactly how it should be read — but a buyer in Southampton cannot look up who is answerable for the regulated feature on their own watch, and a buyer in Arnhem can.

The superseded version of the same document is worth opening too, because it dates the product's direction of travel. Revision AB, dated 19 May 2025, carries the identical part number and the identical 11.5% inconclusive rate, and lists eleven device families — including the Venu 2 Plus, marked “United States only”. Revision AM lists more than twenty, taking in the Approach S72, D2 Mach 2 and Mach 2 Pro, Enduro 4, fēnix 8 Pro, fēnix 9 and 9 Pro, quatix 8 Pro, Venu 4 and Forerunner 970 — and the Venu 2 Plus has gone from the list. In seventeen months the feature spread across nearly the whole premium range, and the one model that had it first was dropped. If you are buying for the ECG app specifically, check the current revision against the exact model on the box rather than the range name.

EUR 8.99 a month, and no sterling figure anywhere

Garmin held out on subscriptions longer than its rivals and then launched one. Garmin Connect+ was announced on 27 March 2025 at EUR 8.99 a month or EUR 89.99 a year, with a 30-day free trial, bringing Active Intelligence, a performance dashboard, live activity, extra coaching support, advanced LiveTrack and more personalisation behind a paywall. The commitment that came with it is the part worth crediting: Garmin's own announcement states that all existing Garmin Connect features and data remain free, and the free app has not been stripped since.

Two observations on the money. Twelve monthly payments come to EUR 107.88 against the annual EUR 89.99, so paying monthly costs about 16.6% more — our arithmetic on Garmin's two published figures. And the prices are in euros because the only Garmin-published price we could find is in euros: the UK subscription page returns the JavaScript notice rather than a plan list, so there is no sterling figure for a British buyer to plan against before they sign up. Against that, the CIRQA Smart Band is advertised in Garmin's own words as a “subscription-free fitness tracker” with “up to 10 days battery”, which is a genuinely different proposition from the rest of the category and the single warmest thing on Garmin's UK shelf.

A Southampton wholesaler with a shop in Westquay

The company a British buyer contracts with is Garmin (Europe) Limited, company number 02724437, incorporated on 19 June 1992 and registered at Liberty House, Hounsdown Business Park, Southampton SO40 9LR. Status active. Its registered activity is SIC 46520, wholesale of electronic and telecommunications equipment and parts — which is a reasonable description of most of what it does and an odd one for a business that now sells to the public directly and runs a high-street shop. Companies House showed its last accounts made up to 28 December 2024, with the next due for the year to 31 December 2025 by 30 September 2026; the filing history page would not open on this run, so whether that set has since been filed could not be confirmed.

The UK footprint is real and long-standing. Garmin's own release of 13 November 2024 records that the European headquarters was set up in Romsey “more than 30 years ago” and is now in Totton, and that its first UK retail store opened to the public on 6 November 2024 at Westquay Shopping Centre in Southampton — nearly 1,500 square feet on the Upper Shopping Level, in a centre with over 17 million visitors a year, and the only UK location showing the full consumer range. Jon Oliver, Managing Director of Garmin UK & Ireland, is quoted saying the company was “thrilled to bring Garmin's extensive product range to one location on the high street in the UK for the first time”.

Where the UK operation is thin is publishing. Garmin's UK press release index holds ten items. Nine of them are dated between March 2019 and January 2022. The tenth, and the only one from the last four years, is a sailing sponsorship dated 31 July 2026. For a company announcing record quarters, that is a newsroom nobody is maintaining.

The division that pays for the one on your wrist

Garmin is listed in the United States, so the numbers behind the watch are audited and published quarterly rather than left to a marketing page. For the quarter ended 26 September 2026: consolidated net sales of $1,986.1 million against $1,770.9 million a year earlier, a 12% increase and a record; gross margin 63.3% from 59.1%; operating margin 30.8% from 25.8%; GAAP diluted earnings per share $2.80 from $2.08.

The segment table is where it gets interesting for anyone buying a watch. Fitness took $694.3 million at a 63% gross margin and a 33% operating margin, giving $230.6 million of operating income. Outdoor took $597.3 million at 68% and 39%. Aviation took $252.7 million — barely a third of Fitness by sales — at a 78% gross margin, the richest of the five, and 26% operating. Marine took $309.9 million at 64% and 30%. Auto OEM took $131.9 million at a 16% gross margin and lost $11.9 million at the operating line.

Read it as a buyer and two things follow. Garmin's consumer wearables are its biggest business by revenue, so the watches are not a sideline funded by the cockpit; and the cockpit is nonetheless where the margins are, which is why the company has every reason to keep certifying things properly. Full-year 2026 guidance was raised to around $8.15 billion of revenue, pro forma earnings of $10.80 a share, a 61.0% gross margin and a 29.0% operating margin at a 16.5% tax rate, with a $4.20 annual dividend paid in four instalments of $1.05 — the next on 24 December 2026 to holders of record on 11 December 2026, the last on 26 March 2027.

Clean registers, and a website you cannot read

Two public checks came back well. The ASA's rulings index returns nothing at all for “garmin” — 0 rulings, 0 informally resolved cases, 0 mail order cases and 0 database cases — which, for a brand making health claims in a category where the regulator has upheld complaints against others, is a genuinely good record and not a common one. And Garmin publishes safety information properly: the in-box booklet tells anyone with a pacemaker or other implanted electronic device to consult a physician before using a heart rate monitor, warns that the optical sensor and pulse oximeter “emit lights and flash occasionally” and that anyone with epilepsy or sensitivity to bright or flashing lights should check with a doctor, and carries the blunt battery warning that severe or fatal injuries can occur within two hours of swallowing one.

We could not read the Office for Product Safety and Standards register on this run: productsafety.gov.uk refused the request under its own robots file, and we do not work around that, so no statement is made here about recalls or safety reports either way.

The gap that will actually affect a shopper is Garmin's own UK website. Its product pages serve the currency code GBP and no price value; its category pages, its consumer limited warranty page, its terms and conditions of sale, its subscription plans page and its compliance page all return a notice that the page does not work without JavaScript enabled. So the three documents a careful buyer would want before paying — the guarantee, the sale terms and the UK declaration of conformity — are not readable on the manufacturer's own site. What is readable there is the promotional banner: free shipping over £30, Klarna accepted, and returns extended to 15/01/2027. One more wrinkle, for completeness: Garmin's marine audio policy disagrees with itself, Appendix 2 being headed as a two-year parts-only warranty while clause 5.1.0 gives car speakers and subwoofers one year. Minor, but it is the kind of thing that makes you read the rest twice.

Common questions

How long is a Garmin watch guaranteed for in the UK?

One year. Garmin's own safety and product information booklet says that “non-aviation products are warranted to be free from defects in materials or workmanship for one year from the date of purchase”, and it states no separate period for the UK or the European Economic Area. The only 24-month figure in the booklet is Italy's statutory consumer guarantee. Your rights under the Consumer Rights Act 2015 are separate from, and additional to, whatever the manufacturer's guarantee says. For comparison, Garmin's UK-specific policies give three years on marine audio and a trolling motor in recreational use and five on Pavilion outdoor speakers.

Does the Garmin ECG app work in the United Kingdom?

The intended-purpose section of Garmin's ECG Instructions for Use, Revision AM of 23 September 2026, names the United Kingdom alongside the European Union, Switzerland, South Africa and the United Arab Emirates. The document itself says the app is “only available in select regions” and points readers to garmin.com/ECG for current availability, and that page would not load its content on 14 November 2026 — so confirm availability for your specific model with the seller rather than relying on a region list. More than twenty models are listed as compatible on firmware 1.1.4, including the Forerunner 970, Venu 3 and Venu 4, fēnix 8, 8 Pro, 9 and 9 Pro, and the Enduro 3 and 4.

How accurate is Garmin's ECG app?

On Garmin's own validation study of about 590 subjects, the app identified atrial fibrillation correctly 99.5% of the time and sinus rhythm 100% of the time — but only in recordings it could classify. It judged 11.5% of recordings inconclusive, which is roughly one in nine, and once those are counted the figures fall to 86.5% for AFib and 91.1% for sinus rhythm. It agreed with board-certified cardiologists reading 12-lead traces 96% of the time. Garmin states in the same document that results “are only potential findings”, that the app cannot check for signs of a heart attack, and that it cannot detect blood clots, stroke, high blood pressure, congestive heart failure, high cholesterol or other arrhythmias.

Do I have to pay for Garmin Connect+?

No. When Connect+ launched on 27 March 2025, Garmin stated that all existing Garmin Connect features and data remain free, and that has held. The subscription adds Active Intelligence, a performance dashboard, live activity, extra coaching support, advanced LiveTrack and more personalisation, at EUR 8.99 a month or EUR 89.99 a year with a 30-day free trial. Garmin publishes no sterling price that we could find, so check the figure in the app before confirming. Twelve monthly payments come to EUR 107.88 against EUR 89.99 annually, about 16.6% more. The CIRQA Smart Band is marketed as a subscription-free tracker.

Why does Garmin's own website not show prices?

Garmin's UK pages build their content in the browser rather than serving it as text, so a product page returns the currency code GBP with no price value against it, and the category, warranty, terms of sale, subscription and compliance pages all return a notice that they do not work without JavaScript enabled. It is a technical choice rather than a policy, but the practical effect is the same: the price, the guarantee and the UK declaration of conformity are not readable on the manufacturer's own site, so you are relying on the retailer for all three. Delivery is free on UK orders over £30 and the current promotion extends returns to 15 January 2027.

Our verdict

Buy a Garmin for the engineering and the battery life, not for the paperwork. This is a company that knows exactly how to prove a number when a regulator makes it — its aviation equipment is approved against prescribed minimum performance specifications with an approved design organisation behind it, and its ECG app comes with a dated Instructions for Use that publishes a 99.5% detection rate and an 11.5% inconclusive rate side by side. That candour is rare and it is the best reason to trust the brand. The weak side is everything Garmin chooses not to publish in Britain: a one-year guarantee where its own boats, cars and garden speakers get two, three or five, no UK-specific warranty document for wearables, no UK responsible person named on the one feature that is a medical device, no sterling price for Connect+, and a UK website whose warranty, terms and compliance pages will not open. If the ECG app is why you are buying, check the current document revision against the exact model number before you pay — the list changed substantially between 19 May 2025 and 23 September 2026, and one model was dropped from it. For everyone else the watch is the strong part and the small print is the part to get in writing from the retailer.
