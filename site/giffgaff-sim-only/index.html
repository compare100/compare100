giffgaff SIM Only: the three commitment levels, priced

Rates and terms checked 14 November 2026 · SIM Only · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

giffgaff sells SIM-only plans from £6 a month, and it sells the same money three different ways. That £6 buys 1GB on pay as you go, 2GB on a monthly rolling plan, and nothing at all on an 18-month contract, where the cheapest rung is £8 for 40GB. All 22 plans on the shop page carry unlimited UK calls and texts, 5G at no extra cost and up to 5GB of roaming across 37 EU Zone destinations. Figures checked 14 November 2026 against giffgaff's own plan pages, terms and regulated contract summaries.

The brand has 4 million members and advertises 99% UK coverage, because it runs on O2's network and is owned by the company that owns O2. The independent measure that matters most here is flattering: Which? named giffgaff a Recommended Provider in its mobile survey published 28 July 2026, with a customer score of 79% from a sample of 360 members — ahead of EE on 74%, Vodafone on 72%, O2 itself on 67% and Three on 65%. The SIM is free, it arrives the next day if ordered before 5pm, there is no credit check to connect, and giffgaff recertified as a B Corp in March 2026 with a score of 97.6, up from 85.8.

One thing has changed and a reader should know it before clicking. giffgaff built its name on having no contract at all, and the plan page now leads with 18-month contracts: clause 6.20 of the terms states that “An 18 month commitment (“Term”) is required in relation to our monthly contracts”, and clause 6.25 sets an early termination charge of 50% of the monthly plan value for every month left to run. The no-contract plans are still there, on the same page, lower down the tabs.

What each price buys, on each of the three levels

Almost every mobile seller offers one plan on two or three terms and lets the reader work out what the commitment is worth. giffgaff does it across eight price points, so the trade is unusually legible. Read down a column and the same money buys very different amounts of data.

Two numbers in that table are worth stopping on. At £8 the 18-month contract carries 40GB against 5GB on the rolling plan — eight times the data for the same money, which is the largest reward for commitment anywhere on the page. At £10 the same commitment buys 25GB against 20GB, so a year and a half of lock-in is worth 5GB. The reward is not graded; it depends entirely on which rung you land on.

The £8 rung also breaks the ladder outright. Its 40GB is more than the £10 contract's 25GB on the rung directly above it, so on the day this page was written the cheapest 18-month plan on the site was also a better data buy than the next one up. giffgaff explains that with a badge rather than a sentence: the tile reads “Was 6 GB” with “34 GB extra free” under a “Data boost” label, and nothing on the page says how long the boost lasts or what the plan reverts to. The honest reading is that £8 for 40GB is a promotion wearing a price tag, and a reader should not assume month 19 looks like month one.

Pay as you go is the level most people overlook and it is the one with the clean arithmetic. It is the same unlimited UK calls and texts, bought a month at a time with no auto-renewal obligation, and it costs data rather than money: 1GB instead of 2GB at £6, 30GB instead of 50GB at £15, 120GB instead of 200GB at £25. Buy three plans in a row and giffgaff adds 1GB to every subsequent £10, £12, £15, £20 or £35 purchase, which its own contract summaries call a “reserve tank”.

The no-price-rise promise, and the date written into its footnote

The plan page carries the heading “No mid-contract price rises*.” in large type. That is a real advantage and giffgaff deserves credit for making it a headline rather than a footnote, because an annual stepped increase of £2 to £4 is now standard across the big networks and it is the single thing that makes a two-year quote unreliable.

The asterisk resolves to this, quoted in full: “No mid-contract price rises on 18 month contracts purchased before end of 2026. Fair usage policy, terms and exclusions apply, see giffgaff.com/terms”. So the promise is attached to a purchase window rather than to the product. A reader buying on 14 November 2026 has until 31 December 2026 to buy under it; a reader arriving in January 2027 sees the same heading with nothing behind it unless giffgaff renews the commitment.

Follow the footnote to the terms it points at and the picture changes again. Clause 4.5 reads “We may increase or decrease the Charges at any time”, with a promise to notify by email or text before a significant increase to a service you use, and clause 13.3 gives 30 days' notice and a right to cancel where a change is not to your exclusive benefit. The £12 plan page takes a third position, under the heading “We'd rather increase your data, not your prices”: “Sometimes we like to add more data to our plans, just to give you a little extra.” Three giffgaff documents, three different strengths of undertaking, and only the footnote carries a date.

The Black Friday plan has its own version of the same problem. The £12 tile advertises 200GB, promising “Get an extra 150 GB of data for 18 months when you sign up before 7/12/26”, while the promotion footnote on the same page reads “Offer on £12 50GB 18 month plan” and the home page describes it as “£12/mo, 200GB data for 18 months,” then 50GB/mo. Read together those say the plan is a 50GB plan with 150GB lent to it for the length of the term, which is a fair deal and a long way from 200GB for £12 a month. Nothing on the plan page itself reconciles the two numbers.

Leaving an 18-month plan costs half of what is left

The exit terms are the part of giffgaff's offer that has changed most and they are set out plainly in the contract. Clause 6.25 charges “an early termination charge consisting of 50% of the value of your monthly Plan”, multiplied by the number of months remaining in the term. giffgaff works the sum itself: on a £25 plan cancelled in month 5, “Your early termination charge is £12.50 x 13 = £162.50”.

Run that across the range and the exposure is easy to see. Walk away from the £8 contract after six months and the charge is £4 for each of 12 remaining months, £48. From the £12 plan at the same point it is £72, and from the £25 unlimited plan £150. There is a 14-day cooling-off period under clause 6.20, and a monthly rolling plan or a pay-as-you-go goodybag carries no termination charge at all, because the commitment ends when you stop buying the next one.

Switching away is governed by Ofcom's rules rather than giffgaff's, and the mechanics are worth knowing before the charge is incurred. Text PAC to 65075 to move and keep your number, STAC to 75075 to leave without keeping it, and INFO to 85075 to be told your switching position, including whether you can leave without charge. A code is valid for 30 days and the number should normally be working on the new service within one working day.

Roaming: 5GB in 37 destinations, then 10p a megabyte

Roaming is included on every plan and the terms are specific enough to budget against. The EU Zone is 37 named destinations — the 27 EU states plus Gibraltar, Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, San Marino and the French overseas departments of French Guiana, Guadeloupe, Martinique, Mayotte and Reunion — with unlimited calls and texts and up to 5GB of data at no extra cost.

The 5GB figure is a cap rather than an allowance, and it bites hardest on the plans a heavy user would buy. The Fair Usage Policy caps EU data below the UK allowance on any unlimited plan or any plan with more than 5GB, and past the cap “you will be charged 10p/MB for the remainder of your plan”. That is £102.40 a gigabyte, so a reader on the £25 unlimited contract who treats roaming as unlimited can spend more in a fortnight abroad than the plan costs in a year. Calls and texts outside the bundle abroad are cheap by comparison at 3.2p a minute and 1p a text.

Beyond the EU Zone there are two routes. Travel data add-ons cover 61 countries — the 37 EU Zone destinations plus 24 others including the USA, Canada, Australia, Japan, India, Switzerland and Türkiye — in 1GB, 5GB or 10GB sizes, lasting 30 days, from £4, with calls and texts not included and each add-on valid only in the country it was bought for. Otherwise pay-as-you-go roaming runs from 20p a megabyte, £1 a minute and 30p a text. On a plane, a ferry or a cruise ship the rate is £1 a minute, 30p a text and £5 a megabyte, which is fifty times the EU excess rate.

There is a residency rule as well, and it is the one that catches people working abroad: using the service in the EU Zone for “63 or more days in any four-month period” is likely to be treated as unfair use unless an EU Zone Travel add-on is active, and giffgaff undertakes to contact the member at least 14 days before any charges apply.

Who you are buying from, and nobody to telephone

The company is older than the brand. Companies House records giffgaff Limited, number 04196996, as incorporated on 9 April 2001 under the name O2 Communications Limited, renamed O2 Ash Limited on 10 November 2008 and giffgaff Limited on 8 September 2009. It is active, registered at 500 Brook Drive, Reading RG2 6UU under SIC code 61900, and filed full accounts for the year to 31 December 2025 on 23 July 2026 along with a confirmation statement on 16 April 2026. The complaints address is a different building: giffgaff Ltd, Belmont House, Belmont Road, Uxbridge UB8 1HE.

Ownership explains the network. giffgaff sits inside Virgin Media O2, which its own Q3 2026 earnings release of 3 November 2026 describes as “a 50:50 joint venture between Liberty Global Ltd. (Liberty Global) and Telefónica, S.A. (Telefónica)”. That release reports 46,348,800 total mobile connections at 30 September 2026, of which 24,440,300 are contract and 7,914,800 prepaid, quarterly revenue of £2,426.7m, contract mobile ARPU of £18.12 and 5G+ outdoor population coverage of 86%. giffgaff is named once in the whole document, in the glossary, where prepaid connections are said to include “retail O2 and giffgaff brands” — so no separate giffgaff subscriber count is published anywhere, and the 4 million members on the marketing pages cannot be checked against an audited figure.

Support is the structural fact a reader should understand before buying, because it is deliberate and it is not like the rest of the market. giffgaff publishes no customer service telephone number at all. The contact page offers a self-service help hub, an “AI-powered giffgaff HelpBot” that hands over to an agent if it cannot resolve the question, and two community forums where other members answer — giffgaff's own stated typical community reply time is under 2 minutes, which is faster than any call centre. The one number on the site, 0330 041 4802, is a complaints voicemail: you leave your name, number and email and “We'll reply by email within 5 working days”.

The escalation route is current and correctly stated on the complaints page: six weeks, or a deadlock letter, then the Communications Ombudsman at PO Box 730, Warrington WA4 6WU, on 0330 440 1614 or textphone 0330 440 1600. That six-week figure reflects Ofcom's change of 8 April 2026, which cut the wait from eight weeks after finding that around 700,000 consumers between January 2022 and 2024 still had an unresolved complaint at six weeks and only about 19% could get it resolved or referred before the eight-week threshold.

What giffgaff pays its own members

Payback is the oldest part of the proposition and it is still running, with published rates. One Payback point is worth £0.01, points are earned for recruiting members, helping on the forums and suggesting improvements, and a cash payout needs “1,000 Payback points or more” — £10 at that rate. Points can instead be taken as account credit or given to charity with no minimum. giffgaff and its members have donated £1,097,557.32 since 2010, including almost £100,000 in 2022.

The recruiting side has grown into a graded scheme. A member becomes a Super Recruiter after 15 activations, and the tier is set on the average over the previous three months: Apprentice, up to 14 members recruited, pays £5 on the recruit's second qualifying spend and £5 on the third, so £10; Expert, 15 to 119 recruits, pays £8 then £12, so £20; and Master, over 120 recruits, pays £10 then £12, so £22. The recruit has to spend at least £10 on credit or a plan, twice, within 120 days of activating, and only the second and third spends count.

Two timing details decide whether any of this is worth having. Payback cash is paid “twice a year, in June and December”, in the second and third weeks, so a point earned in July waits five months; Super Recruiter payments are monthly, around the middle of the month, and “We pay our Super Recruiters exclusively via PayPal”, with points credited to the Payback account instead for anyone without one.

Students get the sharpest plan on the site. The student exclusive is 100GB for £10 a month on a monthly rolling plan with unlimited UK calls and texts — five times the data of the ordinary £10 rolling plan and four times the £10 contract's 25GB. It needs an email address ending .ac.uk, it has been available since 4/8/26, activation has to happen by 4/8/27, and it lasts 12 months from activation, after which nothing on the page says what the plan becomes.

Where giffgaff's own documents contradict each other

Seven pairs of giffgaff pages disagree, and most of them are small. Two are not.

The first is traffic management. The Traffic Management Key Facts Indicator, dated 2 October 2026 and headed “All giffgaff mobile plans”, states that traffic management is not used during peak hours and is not used to manage congestion in particular locations. The separate Traffic Flow Policy, by the same help centre, describes a policy that applies “During certain hours of the day”, refers to “our busiest hours” and “peak hours”, and justifies itself with the finding that “as few as 1% of members were using around 30% of the total network resource”, triggering on members “using double the average monthly network resource within a single day” and expected to affect “less than 1% of our active members”. Those two documents cannot both be right about whether peak-hour management happens. Neither carries a speed figure in Mbit/s, so there is no published floor for what a throttled connection delivers.

The second is the complaints deadline. The complaints code of practice says six weeks, which is correct. The Fair Usage Policy inside the main terms still sends unresolved complaints to “the Ombudsman Services after 8 weeks” — the pre-April 2026 rule and the old name of the scheme. A member who reads only the terms waits two weeks longer than the rules allow.

The smaller four. The founding date is 2008 on the about-us page (“In 2008, our founder, Gav Thompson, decided that there was a better way to do mobile”) and 2009 on the B Corp page (“back in 2009, forging a better way to do mobile”), so no founding year is printed here — only the register's fact that the company took the name on 8 September 2009. The free-SIM page says in one sentence that “This 18 month contract gives you the most data for your money and auto-renews each month” and in another that “You're free to leave when you want”, which clause 6.25 contradicts for £162.50. The plan grid itself prints no minutes or texts figure against any of the 22 plans, so the single most basic fact about them only appears once you open an individual plan or the help article of 30 September 2026. And the roaming page says “61 countries” in its table and “60+ destinations available” in its body text, which is a harmless slip but tells you how carefully these pages are kept. The plan page's own counter is a seventh: it announces “24 plans” above a grid holding 22 distinct ones, the extra two being 18-month contracts repeated as Top picks.

One absence is worth more than any of those. The European Electronic Communications Code requires a contract information summary before purchase, and giffgaff publishes them: the £8 goodybag summary, published 11/04/2023, gives 4GB and says “This plan will last one calendar month from the day of purchase”, and the £25 goodybag summary, published 14/02/2025, gives 120GB on the same one-month basis. Both match today's pay-as-you-go tiers. No equivalent summary could be found for any of the six 18-month contracts — which are the only plans that have a term, a termination charge and a time-limited price promise, and so the only ones where the regulated summary would tell a reader something they cannot get from the tile.

The independent measures, and the network underneath

Nobody rates a mobile brand the way Defaqto rates an insurer, so the evidence comes from three places and it points two ways.

Which? is the warmest of the three and the most directly about giffgaff. Its survey of 5,137 mobile customers, conducted in January 2026 and published 28 July 2026, made giffgaff a Recommended Provider alongside Talkmobile, Smarty, 1p Mobile and Lebara, on a customer score of 79% from 360 giffgaff members. The number that matters is the gap to its own host: O2 scored 67% on a sample of 936, so giffgaff beats the network it sells by 12 points. giffgaff also cites Uswitch Network of the Year 2025 and a Best Mobile Handset Contract Network 2026 award, both of which are customer votes rather than measurements.

Ofcom's complaints data is the second, and it is kind to giffgaff. In the release published 23 July 2026, covering January to March 2026, Ofcom named Sky Mobile as generating the most pay-monthly mobile complaints and listed EE, giffgaff, Tesco Mobile, Three and Vodafone among those generating the fewest. The per-100,000 figures live in charts rather than text and the summary CSV would not open on the day this page was written, so no number is printed here; the Q2 2026 release page also returned a client error on two attempts. Ofcom's own caution is worth carrying with these tables in any case: differences of less than one complaint per 100,000 customers should be considered comparable.

Ofcom's Mobile Matters study is the third and the least comfortable, because it measures the masts rather than the brand. The edition published 17 July 2025, crowdsourced by Opensignal from October 2024 to March 2025, put O2 last or near last on most measures: the lowest share of connections on 4G at 68%, the highest share still on 3G at 3%, only 33% of 5G connections reaching 100 Mbit/s or more against 4% on 4G, and latency slightly higher than the other three networks on both generations. EE had the highest 5G share at 32% and Vodafone the lowest at 24%; Three had the fastest 5G downloads and the lowest 5G latency. Overall connection success was 96% on 5G standalone and 98% on non-standalone. The 2026 edition of that report exists but its PDF returned a client error on two attempts, so these are the most recent per-operator figures that could be read — they are more than a year old and should be treated as such.

Set against that, giffgaff's own coverage claim is “99% UK coverage from an award-winning network”, and Virgin Media O2's 5G+ outdoor population coverage of 86% at 30 September 2026 is the owner's own figure. Both are about reach rather than speed, and reach has never been O2's problem.

Common questions

Which network does giffgaff use?

O2. giffgaff's own footer states that “giffgaff runs on O2's mobile and Virgin Media's broadband network”, and giffgaff Limited sits inside Virgin Media O2, a 50:50 joint venture between Liberty Global and Telefónica. giffgaff advertises 99% UK coverage and 5G at no extra cost; Virgin Media O2 reported 5G+ outdoor population coverage of 86% at 30 September 2026.

Does giffgaff still do plans with no contract?

Yes. Eight monthly rolling plans run from £6 for 2GB to £35 for unlimited data, and eight pay-as-you-go goodybags run from £6 for 1GB to £35 for 200GB. Neither carries a termination charge, because the commitment ends when you stop buying the next plan. The six 18-month contracts, from £8 for 40GB to £25 for unlimited, are the ones with a term.

What does it cost to leave a giffgaff 18-month contract early?

Half the monthly plan value for every month left to run. Clause 6.25 of the terms gives the example of a £25 plan ended in month 5: “Your early termination charge is £12.50 x 13 = £162.50”. On the £8 plan at the same point it would be £48. There is a 14-day cooling-off period under clause 6.20.

How much roaming is included, and what happens if I go over?

Up to 5GB of data in 37 EU Zone destinations with unlimited calls and texts, on every plan. Past the cap you pay 10p per MB, which is £102.40 a gigabyte. Calls and texts outside the bundle abroad are 3.2p a minute and 1p a text. Travel data add-ons cover 61 countries in 1GB, 5GB or 10GB sizes for 30 days from £4, and using the service in the EU Zone for 63 or more days in any four-month period may be treated as unfair use.

Can I phone giffgaff if something goes wrong?

Not for service. giffgaff publishes no customer service telephone number: help comes from the self-service hub, an AI HelpBot that hands over to an agent, and member community forums where giffgaff's stated typical reply time is under 2 minutes. For a formal complaint there is a voicemail on 0330 041 4802 or a web form, answered by email within 5 working days, and the Communications Ombudsman can take the case after 6 weeks or on a deadlock letter.

Is the giffgaff student plan worth it?

On data, substantially. The student exclusive is 100GB for £10 a month on a monthly rolling plan with unlimited UK calls and texts — five times the 20GB the ordinary £10 rolling plan carries. It needs an email address ending .ac.uk, has been available since 4/8/26, must be activated by 4/8/27 and lasts 12 months from activation. Nothing on the page says what the plan becomes afterwards.

Our verdict

giffgaff is the cheapest well-rated way onto O2's network, and the £6 2GB rolling plan with unlimited UK calls and texts remains the thing it does better than anyone: no contract, no credit check, free SIM, and a Which? customer score of 79% that beats O2's own 67%. The £8 40GB contract and the student plan at 100GB for £10 are genuinely strong prices rather than loss leaders with conditions attached. What a reader should go in knowing is that the brand now sells two quite different products under one name. The rolling and pay-as-you-go goodybags are what giffgaff has always been, with a regulated contract summary behind each and nothing to leave. The 18-month contracts carry the best data, a termination charge of half the plan for every month left, no published contract summary, and a no-price-rise promise whose footnote expires at the end of 2026. Pick the level deliberately rather than taking the biggest number on the page, and if you want the 18-month data, buy it before the promise does.
