Go.Compare Phone Insurance: Who Runs the Quote, and What Cover Costs

Rates and terms checked 15 November 2026 · Phone Insurance · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Go.Compare has been comparing insurance since 2006, and the business turned over £191.8m in the year to 30 September 2025. It charges the person doing the comparing nothing. Its own notes to editors say it carries no advertising and no sponsored listings, and that it is paid a fee by the product provider only when a customer buys — which is why a gadget or mobile phone quote costs you nothing to run and commits you to nothing at the end of it.

Two things set it apart from the other large comparison sites. It pays the ratings agency Defaqto to add policy detail to its comparisons, so a results page shows up to 30 extra features of cover rather than price alone — it was the first comparison site to show policy detail at all. And it joined the British Insurance Brokers’ Association in 2008 and says it is still the only comparison website in it.

The gadget and mobile phone comparison itself is built and run by somebody else, and both companies say so in public. It is a white-label service from Comparison Creator Limited, firm reference 832239, a software house that launched the first gadget insurance comparison in the UK and now supplies Go.Compare’s breakdown, gadget, wedding and home emergency comparisons as well. For a reader that is reassurance rather than a warning: the form behind the Go.Compare badge is the work of a specialist that runs this one product for several of the big sites.

One thing to know before you start. Go.Compare’s own mobile expert said on 9 September 2026 that if a home contents policy already includes personal possessions cover, “you likely won’t need to worry about getting gadget insurance”, because otherwise “you’d just be paying for two policies for the same sort of cover”. A comparison site willing to tell you to check what you already have, before it earns anything from you, is one worth running the quote on.

Free to use because the insurer pays on the sale

The money works the same way it did when the business floated. The Gocompare.com Group plc prospectus of 11 October 2016 says the group “primarily generates revenue from fees paid by Partners each time a Customer clicks through to the Partner’s website” and does not charge customers, and its risk factors describe a “cost-per-acquisition (‘CPA’) pricing model” under which the partner pays when a consumer clicks through and completes an application. The same document reports “over 600 Partner brands” across more than 40 products and measures the business on “income per sale”.

The scale of it is published every year by its parent. Future plc reported on 4 December 2025 that Go.Compare took £191.8m of revenue in the year to 30 September 2025, down 5% from a restated £202.7m, on adjusted operating profit of £77.6m against £81.6m and adjusted EBITDA of £80.4m against £84.0m. The division sits inside group revenue of £739.2m, itself down 6% from £788.2m. Car insurance did the damage: £117.6m, down 10% on what Future called strong comparators, which it put down to lower quote volumes partly offset by better conversion. Everything else — home, travel, energy, broadband, pet and gadget — came to £74.2m, up 2% as reported and 3% organic, and now makes 39% of the division, three percentage points more than a year earlier. Over two years the division has still compounded at 10% a year, and in March 2025 it bought the renewals business RNWL for £2.8m up front plus an earn-out.

Gadget cover is somewhere inside that £74.2m and is never broken out. There is no published figure for how many phone policies are bought through the site, how many providers sit on the gadget panel, or what the average premium paid through it is. For a company that publishes median home insurance premiums down to the pound, that is a conspicuous gap.

The quote engine belongs to Comparison Creator, and both firms say so

On 11 September 2023 at 12:58 Go.Compare announced a temporary car insurance comparison under the subheading “The new service is powered by long-term partner Comparison Creator”, and stated plainly that “The comparison service is offered via a white label service with Comparison Creator”. Comparison Creator’s own release about the same launch describes itself as the firm “who already supply breakdown, gadget, wedding and home emergency comparison for Go.Compare”. Steve Jones, its chief executive, said “It is great to see another one of our white label services launched with Go.Compare”, and Ceri McMillan, managing director of Go.Compare, called it “our long-term partner”. Two companies, two websites, the same account of who does what.

That launch is also a useful warning about how thin a new comparison panel can be. The short-term car service went live “with two insurance panel members, with others due to join in the coming weeks”. Nobody has published an equivalent number for the gadget panel at any point.

Comparison Creator Limited is company 07336373, incorporated on 5 August 2010, authorised in its own right under firm reference 832239, and describes itself as “a software development company specialising in providing outsourced solutions” with “14+ years of heritage in comparison product technology”. Across everything it runs it claims “over 75 Service and Insurance providers” and “over 2.5m quotations per annum”, and it names its consumer-facing arm: “Cover Engine is our partnership brand.” Beyond the white label it also says Go.Compare “uses our API service to cross-sell breakdown cover when customers compare car insurance”.

The client list on its own site is the part worth reading twice. It states that “MoneySuperMarket uses our white label service”, and the logos under its “Who we work with” heading include Confused, Uswitch, MoneySuperMarket, Money Saving Expert, Forbes Advisor, Quidco and Go.Compare. Shopping around for gadget cover across four of those names can mean filling in one company’s form four times.

Its own experts will argue you out of buying

On 9 September 2026 at 19:50, after Apple launched a range starting at £1,999 for the Duo — “the highest ever price for an iPhone device” — Catherine Hiley, Go.Compare’s mobile expert, wrote that “With the new devices being of such high value, you might be tempted to get gadget insurance to cover it. But, if your home contents insurance includes personal possessions cover, it may already be covered by this.” If it is, she said, “you likely won’t need to worry about getting gadget insurance”, because “you’d just be paying for two policies for the same sort of cover”. Where the contents policy does not include it, her advice was to ask the home insurer what adding it costs and buy the gadget policy only “If it’s less than a gadget insurance policy”. The same release told readers that older handsets typically drop about £100 on the night pre-orders open and hold that for roughly 10 days, and that the 256GB iPhone 16 Pro Max fell by around £130 when the iPhone 17 opened for pre-order.

She was equally unprotective of the retail channel a year earlier. Commenting on the iPhone 17 range on 9 September 2025 at 19:56, with prices from £799, she wrote that “You can get gadget insurance for a brand new phone from any gadget insurer” and “you don’t have to get it from Apple or from the retailer you bought it from”.

Now do the sum the releases invite. The only monthly gadget premiums Go.Compare has published are £6.97 for a 128GB iPhone 15 and £7.65 for a 128GB Samsung Galaxy S23, in its release of 8 August 2024 at 09:55 — and it sourced both to quotes run in November 2023 on ProtectYourFamily.co.uk, a rival comparison site, rather than to its own panel. At £6.97 a month a single phone costs £83.64 a year and the Samsung £91.80. Against that, Go.Compare’s own sales data puts the median contents-only premium at £65 a year paid annually, and its October 2025 research put the median contents premium at £63, with some customers paying as little as £43 for cover on roughly £30,000 of belongings. Insuring one handset on its own costs more per year than a whole contents policy at the site’s own median.

The comparison that would settle it is contents plus personal possessions cover against a gadget policy, and that is the one number Go.Compare does not publish. Its expert tells readers to ring their home insurer and ask. That is honest and it is also an admission that the site cannot answer the question it has raised.

Where a contents policy stops, in the site’s own research

On 13 March 2026 at 10:42 Nathan Blackler, home insurance expert at Go.Compare, set out the limits. “Cover for personal possessions outside the home is usually an optional extra, not a standard feature,” and “Most policies have single-item limits (often around £1,000) unless you list higher-value items separately.” The research behind it, by Censuswide among 2,000 UK nationally representative consumers between 10 and 12 February 2026, found people carry £533.81 of belongings on an average day out and £510.83 on a night out, that 12% carry more than £1,000, that 37% have no personal possessions cover at all and a further 11% do not know, and that “your phone alone can be worth £800 or more”.

Students get it worse. On 13 August 2026 at 08:00 Tamzin Metcalfe, home and pet insurance expert at Go.Compare, cited Defaqto data showing 22% of contents insurance policies “include no cover at all for student belongings”, and a separate Go.Compare release putting one in four (26%) parents’ policies at zero cover for theft of student possessions at university. Single-item limits in that market run “between £500 and £1,500”. Standard student contents cover protects “items inside your accommodation - not when out and about on campus”, so a laptop taken from a library or a phone lifted on a walk home needs the personal possessions add-on; and “Claims for theft can be rejected if there are no signs of forced entry”. Student contents cover itself “could cost as little as £69 per year”.

The hardest number in this product is in the 2024 release. Go.Compare analysed 305 contents insurance policies on the Defaqto Matrix as at 1 August 2024 and found 56 included mobile phone cover away from home as standard, with a further 242 offering it as an add-on to personal possessions cover. That is 18% of the market — so the advice to check the contents policy first will come back negative more than four times in five.

The limit is the other half of it. A £1,999 iPhone 18 Duo is roughly double the £1,000 single-item limit Go.Compare itself calls typical, and above the top of the £500 to £1,500 band it quotes for student policies. Its Christmas release of 2 January 2026 at 09:55 makes the same point with a worked example of a £2,000 camera against a £1,000 single article limit, and reports that 67% of people do not plan to update a policy after buying presents, with 24% saying it “isn’t worth the hassle” — from Censuswide fieldwork among 2,001 adults between 4 and 7 November 2025. Relying on contents cover is sound advice for a mid-range phone and no help at all for the handset its own newsroom was writing about in September.

For the risk itself, go to the Office for National Statistics rather than to a comparison site. Crime in England and Wales for the year ending June 2026, released on 22 October 2026, records police theft from the person down 20% to 115,891 offences and “at similar levels to YE March 2020 (114,117 offences)”, robbery of personal property down 7% to 57,193, and total robbery up 10% to 88,282 largely because the Home Office Counting Rules for business property were clarified. The previous bulletin, for the year ending March 2026 and released on 23 July 2026, had theft from the person at 121,161 against 49,666 in the year to March 2021 — a near-trebling over five years before this year’s fall. Neither bulletin publishes a mobile-phone-specific count; ONS points instead at its property crime tables for the year ending March 2025, so the phone-theft series runs more than a year behind the headline crime figures. Go.Compare’s own 2024 release reached for “over 300,000” phone thefts taken from newspaper freedom-of-information requests, with no year attached to it.

Paying by the month is the expensive way, on its own figures

Gadget cover is sold monthly almost without exception, and Go.Compare has published what monthly payment costs on the one product it has the data for. Its release of 3 September 2025 at 09:25 took the median premiums actually paid on home insurance sold through the site between 1 June 2024 and 31 May 2025: buildings and contents £230 paid annually against £280 paid monthly, 22% more and £50 a year; buildings only £206 against £236, 15% more; and contents only £65 against £87, 34% more. Its headline figure was that Britons could be spending 33% more a year by paying monthly.

Nathan Blackler’s line on it was that paying annually could cut a premium “by more than a third in the case of contents only cover”. The same release found that buying about 28 days before renewal pays, and that leaving it to the due date lifts the premium 24%, from £200 to £247.

Note which product is punished hardest: contents only, the cheapest and the closest in size to a gadget policy. A £6.97 monthly gadget premium is already a monthly premium, and nobody — not Go.Compare, not the panel — publishes the annual-pay equivalent or the credit charge inside it. The site has measured this penalty precisely for home cover and not at all for the product this page is about.

One shareholder behind the quote engine and the star ratings

This is all on the public register and it was read on 15 November 2026. Comparison Creator Limited (07336373), the firm that builds the gadget comparison, is 75% or more owned by Jump Topco Limited (10611968), notified on 2 March 2017 — the date its founders, Stephanie Louise Murdoch and Stephen Idris Jones, each ceased to hold between 25% and 50% of it. Jump Topco is in turn owned between 50% and 75% by Regulus Bidco Limited (09467963), notified on 9 February 2017, with Stephen Idris Jones holding between 25% and 50% alongside.

Regulus Bidco Limited also holds 75% or more of Defaqto Ltd (02870220), notified on 6 April 2016. Defaqto is the ratings agency whose stars and whose “up to 30 extra features of cover” Go.Compare pays to display on the very comparison Comparison Creator builds. Above both sits Fintel Group Holdings Limited (15059686) of Fintel House, St Andrews Road, Huddersfield HD1 6NA, which has held 75% or more of Regulus Bidco since 16 August 2023, taking over from Fintel plc (09619906) which had held it from 22 April 2021. Comparison Creator, Jump Topco, Regulus Bidco and Defaqto Group Limited (05442706) all give the same registered office: the Financial Research Centre, Haddenham Business Park, Pegasus Way, Haddenham, Aylesbury HP17 8LJ.

Nothing in that makes a Defaqto star wrong. Defaqto’s independence is independence from the insurers it rates, and that is intact. But Go.Compare’s notes to editors describe Defaqto simply as “an independent financial researcher”, and a reader choosing between two gadget policies on their star ratings is not told that the ratings agency and the company that built the comparison answer to the same shareholder. Neither Go.Compare’s boilerplate nor Comparison Creator’s website mentions it. The facts are free to look up and take about four minutes; the disclosure is simply absent.

One housekeeping note for anyone checking: Defaqto Group Limited’s last accounts were made up to 31 July 2018 and Companies House shows them overdue, so the live entity is Defaqto Ltd rather than the group company that shares its name.

The 2017 ground rules, and the pages a reader cannot open

The Competition and Markets Authority published the final report of its digital comparison tools market study on 26 September 2017, after a year’s work begun in September 2016 and an update on 28 March 2017. Its ground rules were that sites be “Clear, Accurate, Responsible and Easy to use”, that they “should be clear about how they make money; how many deals they’re displaying and how they are ordering the results”, that they be clear on how they protect personal information, and that “It should be made as easy as possible for people to make effective comparisons or use different sites”. It also found that more than 90% of the people it surveyed were very or fairly satisfied with the sites they used, and that nearly two-thirds of comparison users visited more than one site when shopping around.

Hold Go.Compare to that in three parts. On how it makes money it is clear, and says so in every press release. On how many deals it shows for gadget cover it publishes nothing — no panel count, no provider list, no statement of market coverage. And on using different sites, the CMA’s two-thirds sits awkwardly beside Comparison Creator’s client list.

The site itself could not be read on 15 November 2026, which is worth stating plainly. www.gocompare.com returned a bot check on /gadget-insurance/ and /gadget-insurance/guide/ — “As you were browsing something about your browser made us think you were a bot” — and /gadget-insurance/mobile-phone-insurance/ returned a client error, while robots.txt and sitemap.xml were served normally. The robots file disallows /results/, /quoteprocess/, /api/, /account/, /health/, /mobile-phone/go/, /broadband/go/, /ps/ and /e/, and names /_Incapsula_Resource. Closing a live results page to crawlers is correct and every serious comparison site does it. But all 12 gadget-insurance pages listed in Go.Compare’s own sitemap are pages it asks search engines to index, and on this date none of them could be opened by anything that was not a full browser. So no price, excess, claim limit, device age rule or panel member from Go.Compare’s own gadget pages appears above; everything here comes from its newsroom, its parent’s accounts, its prospectus, its supplier and the public registers.

The firm reference number is the other gap. The press boilerplate says only that Go.Compare is “authorised and regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority” and gives no number, no company number and no registered office. The FCA Register’s firm pages are a JavaScript application that returns a CSS error to a fetch, and the FCA’s warnings pages returned server errors on the same date, so the number could not be confirmed and is not stated here. Comparison Creator, the supplier, prints 832239 on its own home page and in its own press releases.

Worth knowing before you search for the brand. The FCA carries a long run of clone-firm warnings against operations trading as Go Compare Online, Go Compare Investments, Go Compare the Bond Market, Compare the Bonds, Gocomparethebonds.com, Gocomparethebondmarket.com, Gocomparefixedrates.com, GoCompare Bonds, GoCompare Bond Rates, bonds.go-compare.info and goocomparebonds.com. The real Gocompare.com Limited is registered for insurance mediation under SIC 66220 and does not sell bonds, fixed-rate accounts or investments. Anything using the name to sell a bond is not this company — and the genuine site is reached by typing the address, not by following a search advert.

For the record, the newsroom is a genuinely useful thing and most comparison sites do not run one as well. On 15 November 2026 it held 737 items across 62 pages, the newest dated 13 November 2026, named its experts by role — Catherine Hiley on mobile, Nathan Blackler and Tamzin Metcalfe on home, Tom Banks on gadget insurance — listed Lynsey Walden, Kath Chadwick, Rubie Barker and Rosie Johns as contacts, gave a phone number of 02920 020360, and dated and sourced its survey work down to the fieldwork days and the research agency. Go.Compare also publishes the uncomfortable half of its own findings: its October 2025 release reported that 32% of households have no contents policy at all, up from 26% in 2022, rising to 44% among 16 to 24-year-olds, against 61% insured and 8% unsure, from Censuswide fieldwork among 2,000 adults between 22 and 24 September 2025.

Where it wins

  • Free to use and no obligation at the end — Go.Compare takes a fee from the provider on a completed sale and says in its own notes to editors that it carries no advertising and no sponsored listings.
  • Defaqto ratings are shown alongside price, adding up to 30 extra features of cover, so a results page can be read on what the policy does rather than on the premium alone.
  • Its own experts tell readers to check their contents policy first and to buy the cheaper of the add-on or the gadget policy — advice that costs the company money.
  • The newsroom dates and sources its research properly, naming Censuswide and Sago, the fieldwork days, sample sizes and, in one case, a margin of error of plus or minus 2.5%, 19 times out of 20.
  • The quote journey is built by a dedicated specialist, Comparison Creator Limited (FRN 832239), which launched the first gadget insurance comparison in the UK and handles over 2.5m quotations a year.
  • A member of the British Insurance Brokers’ Association since 2008, which it says makes it the only comparison website in the trade body.
  • Backed by a listed parent that publishes the division’s numbers twice a year: £191.8m of revenue and £77.6m of adjusted operating profit in the year to 30 September 2025.

Where it falls short

  • No gadget panel is published anywhere. There is no provider count, no insurer list and no statement of market coverage for phone or gadget cover — and the one panel size Go.Compare has ever disclosed, two members at the launch of its short-term car comparison in September 2023, is not encouraging.
  • The only monthly gadget premiums the company has ever published, £6.97 for a 128GB iPhone 15 and £7.65 for a 128GB Galaxy S23, come from its release of 8 August 2024 and were priced in November 2023 — on a rival comparison site, not on its own panel. They are three years old.
  • Its central piece of advice fails for most readers on its own evidence. Go.Compare’s analysis of 305 contents policies on the Defaqto Matrix as at 1 August 2024 found only 56 — 18% — included mobile phone cover away from home as standard, and its own research calls personal possessions cover “usually an optional extra, not a standard feature”.
  • The advice also fails on the handset it was written about: a £1,999 iPhone 18 Duo is about double the £1,000 single-item limit the company itself describes as typical, and above the £500 to £1,500 band it quotes for student policies.
  • The company that builds the gadget comparison and the ratings agency whose stars appear on it share a controlling shareholder — Regulus Bidco Limited (09467963) holds 75% or more of Defaqto Ltd and, through Jump Topco Limited, more than half of Comparison Creator Limited — and neither firm discloses it on its website.
  • Every one of the 12 gadget-insurance pages in Go.Compare’s own sitemap was unreadable on 15 November 2026 behind a bot check or a client error, although robots.txt permits them, so no price, excess, claim limit or device age rule could be taken from the pages a reader is sent to.
  • No firm reference number, company number or registered office appears in its press boilerplate, and the FCA Register could not be read on the same date, so the authorisation a reader would want to check could not be confirmed from either source.
  • Paying monthly is the expensive route and the company has quantified it only for home cover — 34% more for contents only, £65 a year against £87 — while gadget insurance, sold by the month as standard, has no published annual-pay comparison at all.

Common questions

Does Go.Compare sell phone insurance itself?

No. Gocompare.com Limited (company 05799376) is registered under SIC 66220 as an insurance agent and broker, carries no insurance risk and underwrites nothing. It introduces you to providers and is paid a fee by the provider when a sale completes, on the cost-per-acquisition basis its 2016 prospectus describes. The gadget and mobile phone comparison itself is a white-label service supplied by Comparison Creator Limited (company 07336373, FRN 832239), which both companies have confirmed in public since 11 September 2023.

What does gadget insurance cost through Go.Compare?

The company does not publish a price. The only monthly gadget premiums it has ever put in writing are £6.97 for a 128GB iPhone 15 and £7.65 for a 128GB Samsung Galaxy S23, in its release of 8 August 2024 — and it attributed both to quotes run in November 2023 on ProtectYourFamily.co.uk, not to its own panel. At £6.97 a month that is £83.64 a year. Your own figure depends on the handset, its age, whether you add theft and loss, and the excess, and you will only see it by running the quote.

Will my home insurance already cover my phone?

Probably not without an add-on. Go.Compare’s own analysis of 305 contents policies on the Defaqto Matrix as at 1 August 2024 found 56 included mobile phone cover away from home as standard and 242 offered it as an add-on to personal possessions cover. Its March 2026 research calls personal possessions cover “usually an optional extra, not a standard feature” and puts single-item limits “often around £1,000”. Ring your insurer, ask the price of adding personal possessions cover, check the single-item limit against what your phone cost, and compare that with a gadget premium.

How many insurers are on the gadget panel?

Go.Compare has never said. There is no panel count, insurer list or market-coverage statement for gadget cover in its newsroom, its parent’s accounts or its prospectus, and all 12 of its gadget-insurance pages were unreadable to anything but a full browser on 15 November 2026. Comparison Creator, which runs the journey, claims “over 75 Service and Insurance providers” across every product it operates, which is not the same thing as a gadget panel. The CMA asked comparison sites in September 2017 to be clear about “how many deals they’re displaying”; on this product that number is not in public.

Is it cheaper to pay annually?

On home cover, measurably. Median premiums paid through Go.Compare between 1 June 2024 and 31 May 2025 were £230 a year for buildings and contents paid annually against £280 paid monthly, £206 against £236 for buildings only, and £65 against £87 for contents only — 34% more on the cheapest product, and up to 33% more across the board. The same research found that leaving a renewal to the due date rather than buying about 28 days ahead lifted the premium 24%, from £200 to £247. Gadget cover is sold monthly as standard and no annual-pay comparison has been published for it.

Why do searches for the name turn up warnings?

Because the brand is heavily cloned. The FCA publishes warnings against operations trading as Go Compare Online, Go Compare Investments, Go Compare the Bond Market, Compare the Bonds, Gocomparethebonds.com, Gocomparethebondmarket.com, Gocomparefixedrates.com, GoCompare Bonds, GoCompare Bond Rates, bonds.go-compare.info and goocomparebonds.com. The real company does not sell bonds, fixed-rate accounts or investments of any kind. If something using the name is offering a fixed-rate bond, it is not Gocompare.com Limited of Cardiff.

Who owns Go.Compare?

Future plc. Gocompare.com Limited was incorporated on 28 April 2006 and floated as Gocompare.com Group plc, whose prospectus of 11 October 2016 reported “over 600 Partner brands” across more than 40 products. The Go.Compare division took £191.8m of revenue and £77.6m of adjusted operating profit in the year to 30 September 2025, within group revenue of £739.2m, and bought the renewals business RNWL in March 2025 for £2.8m up front. Car insurance is £117.6m of that revenue and everything else, gadget cover included, is £74.2m, which is never broken down further.

Our verdict

Go.Compare is a good place to start on phone cover and a poor place to finish. It costs nothing, it commits you to nothing, it puts Defaqto’s detail next to the price instead of leaving you to guess, and its own mobile expert will tell you to check your contents policy before you buy anything — which is not the behaviour of a site trying to shift a product. Set against that, the gadget comparison is Comparison Creator’s software rather than Go.Compare’s, no panel is published, the only premiums the company has ever printed are £6.97 and £7.65 a month from November 2023 quotes on a rival site, and the ratings agency it leans on shares a controlling shareholder with the firm that built the form. Run the quote, because it is free and it takes a few minutes. Then do the two sums the site will not do for you: £6.97 a month is £83.64 a year against a £63 median contents premium, and a £1,999 handset is double the £1,000 single-item limit most contents policies carry. Ring your home insurer for the price of personal possessions cover and buy whichever is cheaper — which is exactly what Go.Compare told you to do.
