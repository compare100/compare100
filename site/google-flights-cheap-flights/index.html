Google Flights: 300 Partners, No Booking Fee, and Who You Actually Buy From

Rates and terms checked 14 November 2026 · Cheap Flights · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Google Flights searches more than 300 airline, online travel agency and aggregator partners, costs nothing to use, and adds no fee of its own to the price you see. Google’s own help pages are precise about what that figure covers: it is “only for the passenger air transport service itself and includes VAT/GST and airport taxes”, and it is refreshed “approximately once every 24 hours”. Bag charges are kept separate and can be folded back in with a filter that adds either a checked bag or an overhead carry-on to every result at once.

The one thing to understand before you start is that Google is not selling you anything. Its own booking page says so in a single sentence: “Google doesn’t sell plane tickets, and won’t be a party to your booking transaction.” Google “acts only as an intermediary to securely pass your information to the airline or travel agency”, and “as the party responsible for your booking, the relevant airline or online travel agency provides customer support”. So the ticket, the money, the change fee and the complaint all belong to whoever you land on when you click through — which is the number you ring if something goes wrong, not Google.

What Google does build itself, and builds better than anyone selling flights, is the carbon figure beside each result. It comes from the Travel Impact Model, which is published free under a CC BY-SA 4.0 licence, exposed through a public API, overseen by an outside advisory committee, and was on model version 3.0.0 dated 27 July 2026 when this page was checked.

What the number on the screen is made of

A flight search is only as useful as the price it quotes, so it is worth knowing exactly what Google is quoting. The help pages state that the displayed figure covers the passenger air transport service with VAT or GST and airport taxes inside it, and that “additional fees may apply (luggage, credit card, etc.)”, varying by whichever airline or agency you choose. Nothing in that figure is Google’s. There is no membership, no trial and no card required to search.

Where Google goes further than most is the bag filter. Switch it on and every price in the list is recalculated to include the fee for one checked bag or for putting a carry-on in the overhead locker, and Google notes that the filter “shouldn’t remove any flights from the results” — it only changes the numbers. That matters on short-haul Europe, where the headline fare and the fare you actually pay can be two different products. Google is honest about the limits too: it “displays the baggage information that we receive from our partners”, estimated bag fees “may be subject to additional government taxes”, carry-on charges “vary by country”, and what counts as a carry-on “varies by airline”, so the airline’s own baggage page remains the authority on size and weight.

The date tools are the cheapest saving on offer. The calendar shows “the lowest total price to fly for each day”, the price graph plots the same route by week or by month, and the green highlighting marks the most affordable options judged on “price in relation to the lowest-priced option and the total number of options available”. None of it costs anything and none of it requires you to be signed in.

Google is not the seller, and that decides who you ring

Read Google’s booking help and the arrangement is unusually clearly drawn. “All bookings are made directly with the relevant airline or online travel agency.” Google “acts only as an intermediary to securely pass your information”. In most cases “your card will be charged when your booking is confirmed”, and some sellers “may place a temporary authorisation on your card” first. If you then need to change or cancel, Google says to contact the airline or agency directly; it can help you find the booking details but cannot make the change for you, and it tells you to “check the cancellation policy so that you’re aware of any cancellation-related deadlines or penalties”.

That is cleaner than a travel brand that takes your money and then points at a third party, and it is worth saying so plainly. But it has a consequence a lot of readers only meet at the worst moment: every promise attached to your ticket is somebody else’s promise. Your confirmation email, Google notes, carries “the confirmation number and the airline or travel agency’s contact information” — that is the counterparty, and it may be an agency rather than the carrier.

One result type deserves a second look before you click. Google marks some itineraries “Separate tickets booked together” — what the industry calls a virtual interline, meaning “a combination of flights from carriers without a codeshare or interline agreement”. Google says these “generally require travellers to collect and recheck baggage” and to check in for each flight separately, and that you lose the coordination between carriers that a single ticket gives you. A missed connection on a self-transfer fare is not the airline’s problem, and on separate tickets “you might be charged a change fee for each ticket”. The saving can be real; so is the exposure.

For a UK resident, the contract behind the tool itself sits with Google LLC under terms effective 30 July 2026, and those terms say that for a UK resident “these terms and your relationship with Google” are “governed by English law” with disputes filed “in the English courts”. The warranty Google gives is that it provides the services “using reasonable skill and care” — a service standard, not a promise that a price was right.

How the results are ordered, in Google’s own words

Most comparison tools will not tell you how they sort. Google will. The default “Best” tab “shows you the flights that offer the best trade-offs between price, convenience and ease of booking”. Top departing flights are ranked on price and convenience, where convenience means duration, number of stops and whether you change airport; a non-stop itinerary with no price information “always appear[s] in the lowest position”. Everything in the “Other departing flights” list is simply in ascending price order.

The commercial question follows immediately, and Google answers it in one line: its partnerships “have no impact on the ranking of offers suggested to you”. Set that against the thing the Competition and Markets Authority spent 2019 worrying about in this sector — the effect commission has on how results are ordered — and a flat public statement is the strongest form of answer available short of publishing the algorithm.

What is less visible is who is missing. Google states that “not all airlines or available flights are included, as a partnership with Google is required”, and that partners “may have rules that prevent us from linking to it on their website”. There is a second gap on top: a business in the European Economic Area can ask Google not to display its prices and availability on Google Flights to EEA users, and “content currently being displayed on Flights will be removed within 30 days of opting out”, while “your data will still appear on Search”. No list of who has taken that option is published anywhere, and the choice is framed for EEA businesses and EEA users rather than UK ones. Either way, a Google Flights search is a search of Google’s partners, not of the market.

The prediction tools, and the words they carefully use

Google’s price insights are prediction, and the wording is chosen with care. You may be told that “prices are unlikely to drop before you book”, that “prices are lower than usual” — which means “significantly lower than expected based on an analysis of past prices of similar trips” — or that “prices are likely to increase”, sometimes with an approximate amount attached. Tracking a route sends you an email when the price “changes significantly”; tracking “Any dates” sends one when the route’s minimum price “drops significantly over a month”, and a routine list of the best available fares when it does not. There are also emails warning that a current fare is about to expire and the replacement will probably cost more, carrying an estimate of the rise and how confident Google is in it.

Twice in that paragraph the threshold is the word “significantly”, and Google publishes no number behind it. Nor does it publish any accuracy rate for the predictions themselves — no hit rate, no sample, no methodology note of the kind it does publish for emissions. The tools are free and they are better than guessing, but they cannot be audited from outside, and a confident-sounding sentence about next month’s fares is still a forecast.

The newest tool is blunter about its own limits. AI Flight Deals, which takes a plain-English trip idea and works out destinations and dates for it, is labelled “an experimental (beta) feature”, warns that “the destinations and dates that it suggests may not always be a perfect match”, and says outright that “the price that you see may change when you go to book”. It defines a deal against the median of the cheapest prices for that route over the past 12 months, with savings deals at least 20% below typical. It does not support multi-city trips, specific passenger types, “groups larger than four people” or specific stopover requests.

Two of the best features stop at the Atlantic

The Google Price Guarantee is the most generous thing in the product and a UK traveller cannot use it. Its own additional terms say it “is made available by Google LLC and is only available to users based in the U.S. who are 18 years or older”, and that it “is only available for one-way and round-trip itineraries departing from the U.S.”. Where it applies, Google tracks the fare after you book and pays the difference: the gap “must be $5 or more”, payouts are capped at “$500 in the aggregate across all eligible itineraries in a given calendar year”, you are limited to “one Payout per eligible itinerary” and to three confirmed guarantee bookings at a time, the booking must be confirmed with Google “no later than 30 days after the first flight in your itinerary has flown”, and the payout redeemed no more than 45 days after that. The terms were last modified 01/10/2024.

AI Flight Deals is the same story at an earlier stage. Google’s announcement of 14 August 2025 said it would “roll out in the U.S., Canada and India” over the following week, and no UK availability has been announced since. For a reader in Manchester or Glasgow, then, two of the three features Google has built since 2024 are visible in the coverage and absent from the product.

None of that makes the free part worse. It does mean that comparisons written for an American audience — which is most of what is written about Google Flights — describe a tool that is not the one loading in a UK browser.

The emissions estimate is the most carefully built figure here

If you want one reason to use Google Flights over a cheaper-looking alternative, it is this. Since July 2025 Google has shown the EASA flight emissions label where one exists, which is built from an airline’s verified historical data for a specific route. Where it does not, the estimate comes from Google’s own Travel Impact Model, which combines public and licensed datasets and is overseen by an advisory committee formed after Google joined the Travalyst coalition, first meeting in Zurich in the summer of 2023. Google says EASA and the model are being aligned so that estimates can be compared fairly, and that sustainable aviation fuel reductions reported by EASA are kept out of the headline CO2e figure and shown separately in a tooltip.

The detail behind the number is published rather than asserted. The figure is well-to-wake, covering fuel production and transport as well as take-off, cruise and landing, with non-CO2 effects converted to CO2 equivalent on global warming potential. Cabin classes are weighted on the seat area ratios in IATA Recommended Practice 1726 — first 5, business 4, premium economy 1.5, economy 1 — so a business seat on the same aircraft carries roughly four times an economy seat’s share. Load factors come from US Department of Transportation data for US flights and ch-aviation data elsewhere where it exists. The model’s own API, which is public and free under a CC BY-SA 4.0 licence, publishes each estimate with its sources: a Zurich to Boston flight returns a load factor of 0.81, a cargo mass fraction of 0.30 and a 6,119 km distance, with 318,102 grams for economy against 1,590,510 for first.

Each flight is then labelled higher, typical or lower against the median for the route you searched, you can sort by emissions or filter to “Less emissions only”, and the scale is UK-legible: Travalyst gives a typical one-way economy Gatwick to New York JFK flight as 466 kg CO2e per passenger, with a lower-emitting option on the same route 12% or 57 kg below that, against 56 kg for Gatwick to Dublin. Train comparisons use an IEA average of 19 g CO2e per passenger-kilometre.

Google is also unusually frank about what the figure leaves out. Persistent contrails form on a small fraction of flights and, citing Lee (2021), account for about a third of aviation’s total warming impact, so the real climate effect is substantially higher than the fuel-based number — which is why each result carries a contrail band of Low (up to 20% more), Medium (20–100% more) or High (over 100% more), predicted from historical weather and explicitly liable to differ on the day. The estimates still do not account for flight direction or for actual sustainable fuel use, the fallback load factor is a 2019 pre-pandemic industry average, and some aircraft types produce no estimate at all and are labelled “unknown emissions”. The API does not cover charter, private or cargo flights, and reaches only departures within the next 11 months.

Where your protection comes from when the flight is cancelled

Nothing on Google Flights protects your trip, and that is not a criticism of Google so much as a fact about what a search engine is. Your rights come from the airline and from UK law. If a flight from or to the UK is cancelled with less than 14 days’ notice and it was the airline’s fault, the Civil Aviation Authority sets fixed compensation per person: £220 under 1,500 km, £350 between 1,500 and 3,500 km and £520 over 3,500 km, with reduced bands of £110, £175 and £260 where the replacement gets you there inside two, three or four hours depending on distance. You also choose between a refund of every unused part of the ticket and an alternative flight at a time you pick, and where extraordinary circumstances such as extreme weather or a strike remove the compensation the airline must still provide food, drink, a means to communicate, and a hotel and transfers if you are rerouted to the next day — “no matter how long the delay lasts”.

If the airline says no, the complaint goes to its approved dispute scheme, and that is free. AviationADR covers easyJet, Ryanair, Wizz Air, TUI Airways, Virgin Atlantic, Air France, KLM, Delta, Air Canada, Turkish Airlines, SAS, TAP Air Portugal and others; CEDR covers British Airways, BA CityFlyer and Cathay Pacific. The complaint must concern something in the last six years, or five in Scotland, must reach the scheme within 12 months of the airline’s final response, and can be referred early if the airline has not replied within eight weeks — most decisions arrive within three months. Where an airline belongs to no scheme, the CAA’s Passenger Advice and Complaints Team can help but cannot make a binding decision.

ATOL is the other half of the question and it does not reach a flight bought on its own. The CAA describes ATOL as financial protection for a package trip that includes a flight, booked with an ATOL holder, and says plainly that it “is not travel insurance”. Google neither holds nor claims it, mentions neither it nor ABTA anywhere, and could not hold it: it is not selling the trip. So a flight-only ticket bought through a Google Flights link carries whatever the seller carries — which is the single best reason to notice which company you are being handed to.

Google signed the CMA’s booking principles in 2019

One piece of the record is worth having, because nobody writing about Google Flights seems to carry it. When the Competition and Markets Authority finished its work on online booking in 2019 — after formal undertakings from Booking.com, Expedia, Agoda, Hotels.com, ebookers and trivago — it announced on 13 September 2019 that 25 further firms had signed up to its sector-wide principles. Google is on that list, alongside Skyscanner, Kayak, Momondo, Opodo, Lastminute, Trip.com, TripAdvisor, Airbnb and On the Beach. The principles include not giving a false impression of a room’s popularity and always displaying the full cost upfront, and the CMA said it would “continue to actively monitor compliance in the sector”.

The regime behind that has since been rebuilt. The consumer provisions of the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 came into force on 7 April 2025, letting the CMA decide for itself whether consumer law has been broken rather than litigating through the courts, and fine up to 10% of global turnover. Drip pricing — one price on the screen and fees added as you proceed — is prohibited outright. A tool that shows the air fare with taxes in it, keeps bag fees separate and then offers to add them back in is on the right side of that rule by design, and the CAA has said it is reviewing its own dispute-resolution policy in light of the same Act.

Scale, for context, comes from Alphabet’s own filings rather than anyone’s estimate: $127,577 million of revenue in the quarter to 30 September 2026, of which $82,012 million was advertising and $63,350 million was Google Search and other. The same filing records that the European Commission issued non-compliance decisions under the Digital Markets Act in July 2026 including a fine of €460 million relating to Search, and that the UK has issued a statement of objections on Google’s advertising technology. The UK trading company, Google UK Limited, is number 03977902, incorporated 20 April 2000 and registered at 1 St Giles High Street, London WC2H 8AG — though it is Google LLC that a UK user’s terms name as the service provider.

Common questions

Does Google Flights charge a booking fee?

No. There is no subscription, no account requirement and no Google fee inside the fare. Google’s help pages say the displayed price is “only for the passenger air transport service itself and includes VAT/GST and airport taxes”, with luggage and card charges added by the seller. Google is paid through its wider advertising business — $82,012 million of Alphabet’s $127,577 million of revenue in the quarter to 30 September 2026 — not by you.

Who do I contact if my flight is cancelled after booking through Google Flights?

The airline or the online travel agency named on your confirmation email. Google states that it “won’t be a party to your booking transaction”, cannot make changes or cancellations for you, and that the seller “provides customer support”. Your statutory rights are against the operating airline: for a UK cancellation with less than 14 days’ notice that was the airline’s fault, £220, £350 or £520 per person depending on distance, plus care and assistance and a choice of refund or re-routing.

Is a flight booked through Google Flights ATOL protected?

Not by Google. ATOL is financial protection for a package trip that includes a flight, bought from an ATOL holder, and the CAA says plainly that it “is not travel insurance”. Google neither holds nor claims ATOL or ABTA cover, because it is not selling the trip. Whatever protection you have comes from the company that takes your payment, so it is worth reading whose name appears at checkout.

Can I use the Google Flights price guarantee in the UK?

No. Its own terms, last modified 01/10/2024, limit it to “users based in the U.S. who are 18 years or older” and to “one-way and round-trip itineraries departing from the U.S.”. Where it does apply it pays a difference of $5 or more, capped at $500 across a calendar year. AI Flight Deals is similarly restricted, having rolled out in the US, Canada and India from 14 August 2025.

Does Google Flights show every airline?

No, and it says so: “not all airlines or available flights are included, as a partnership with Google is required”, and some partners have rules preventing Google from linking to their site. A business in the European Economic Area can also ask for its prices and availability to be removed from Google Flights for EEA users within 30 days. Treat the results as Google’s partner set rather than the whole market, and check a carrier directly if you do not see it.

How reliable is the carbon figure beside each flight?

It is the best-documented number in flight search, and Google is open about its gaps. Where an EASA flight emissions label exists it is used, built from an airline’s verified route data; otherwise the Travel Impact Model applies, on well-to-wake fuel, IATA RP 1726 seat-area ratios and real load factors where available. It does not yet account for flight direction or actual sustainable fuel use, falls back to a 2019 industry-average load factor, and labels some aircraft “unknown emissions”. Contrails, worth about a third of aviation’s warming, are shown separately as Low, Medium or High bands.

Our verdict

As a free way to find out what a route costs and which days are cheapest, Google Flights is hard to beat: more than 300 partners in one search, VAT and airport taxes already inside the price, bag fees addable at a click, and a published statement that commercial partnerships do not move the running order. The emissions work is better still — version 3.0.0 of the Travel Impact Model dated 27 July 2026, the EASA label where it exists, seat-area weighting from IATA RP 1726, contrail bands and an open API — and no company selling flights publishes anything like it. Use it the way it is built: as a map of the market Google has partnered with, not the whole market, and with the understanding that the deal, the refund and the complaint all belong to whoever takes your card. Check the price on the seller’s own page before you pay, because Google refreshes its copy roughly once a day. And if you came for the Price Guarantee or AI Flight Deals, neither is available in the UK.
