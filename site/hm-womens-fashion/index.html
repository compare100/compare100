H&M Women's Fashion Review: Delivery, Returns and What It Refuses to Sell

Rates and terms checked 14 November 2026 · Women's Fashion · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

H&M charges £3.99 to deliver a UK order with no minimum spend, £2.99 to send something back by post, and nothing at all if you walk it into a store. Dresses on hm.com run from £12.99 to £119.99 across 2,714 styles. Full-price items get 30 days to come back — double the 14 days the law insists on. Figures read on 14 November 2026 from H&M's own UK terms, returns and product pages.

The shop behind those prices has been trading in Britain for longer than the name suggests. H&M Hennes & Mauritz UK Limited was incorporated on 6 February 1979 as Hennes of Oxford Street Limited, became H&M Hennes Limited on 20 November 1984 and took its present name on 12 September 2007; it still files from 1st Floor UK House, 164-182 Oxford Street, London W1D 1NN. The group above it ran 3,579 H&M-brand stores in 82 markets and 62 online markets at 31 August 2026, and turned a gross margin of 54.0 per cent in the quarter to that date.

One thing to know before you fill a basket: hm.com sells other companies' labels alongside its own, and the charges are levied per brand. A two-brand order carries £3.99 of delivery for each of them, and £2.99 of return fee for each parcel that goes back. On a large item the second brand adds £24.99 rather than £3.99. Clause 2,3 of the UK terms sets all three figures out plainly, which is more than many retailers do.

The charge that multiplies, and the one that disappears at a till

H&M's UK terms set a flat £3.99 standard delivery fee with no basket threshold to clear, which is a kinder arrangement than a free-over-£50 rule for anyone buying one £19.99 top. The fee does not scale with the order either: a £12.99 dress and a £300 basket of six both travel for £3.99.

It does scale with the number of labels. Clause 2,3 says that where external brands are in the bag "there is an additional delivery fee of £3.99 per brand", and that "for larger products, there is an additional cost of £24.99 per brand". Three brands and a sofa-sized item is not a theoretical basket on a site that sells homeware next to knitwear, and it is the one piece of arithmetic the checkout will do for you only at the last screen.

Returns run the same way. The returns page says "a £2.99 return fee will be deducted from your refund" and, for mixed baskets, "unless otherwise stated in the online store, a fee of £2.99 per brand". Hand the parcel over a counter in any H&M store instead and the fee is nothing at all, with the money going back to the card that paid. For a two-brand order that is £5.98 saved by walking past a post box, and it is the single most useful sentence on H&M's returns page.

Thirty days, fourteen if it was in the sale, and a Christmas window to 17 January

The headline return window is 30 days from the day the parcel arrives. H&M builds it in two pieces: the terms give "the right to cancel all or part of your purchase for any reason by notifying us within 14 days", then "an additional 16 days to notify us" on full-price goods, "which means that you have a total of 30 days to make a return for any reason". Once notice is given there are a further 14 days to get the goods moving. The statutory floor underneath all of that is 14 days, so the extra 16 are H&M's own.

Two categories do not get them. The terms state that for sale items "the right of return notice period is 14 days", and the returns page extends that to "14 days for sale items and designer collection articles". A £119.99 ELIE SAAB x H&M kaftan therefore has half the return window of a £12.99 full-price dress, which is the opposite of what most shoppers would assume.

Over Christmas the window stretches: from 1 November, full-price items and items bought with a promotional discount "can be returned until 17th of January". Sale stock is not named in that sentence. After that the clocks are honest and slow — up to 14 calendar days for the parcel to be received, a refund due within 14 days of receipt, up to 5 working days for a card to clear, and up to 30 days for PayPal. Worst case on a PayPal order, the money is back 58 days after it was posted.

What British law actually forbids in a garment: cats, dogs and seals

Shoppers tend to assume there is a law behind a fur-free label. There is, and it is narrower than almost anyone expects. The Cat and Dog Fur Regulations 2008 give effect to Regulation (EC) No 1523/2007 of 11 December 2007, which bans placing cat and dog fur on the market and importing or exporting it. The definitions in regulation 1(2) are species-specific: "cat" means "an animal of the species felis silvestris" and "dog" means "an animal of the subspecies canis lupus familiaris".

The penalty has teeth. Regulation 2 makes a contravention an offence punishable "on conviction on indictment, to a fine not exceeding £75,000" and on summary conviction by a fine not exceeding the statutory maximum, which the explanatory note puts at £5,000. Enforcement sits with "every weights and measures authority in Great Britain" and every district council in Northern Ireland, except where goods are under customs supervision, when it is HMRC. Obstructing an officer carries a fine at level 3 on the standard scale.

The Seal Products Regulations 2010 work the same way for seals, with the same £75,000 ceiling on indictment, a definition reaching "all products, either processed or unprocessed, deriving or obtained from seals" — meat, oil, blubber, organs, raw and tanned fur skins, skins assembled in plates and crosses — and enforcement by general customs officials designated under section 3 of the Borders, Citizenship and Immigration Act 2009.

That is the whole of it. Mink, fox, rabbit, chinchilla, raccoon dog, crocodile, python: none of them is banned from a British shop. Every other restriction a fashion retailer observes on animal materials is self-imposed.

The bans H&M puts on itself, and who stands behind each one

H&M Group's animal welfare policy, published on hmgroup.com, goes a long way past the two statutory instruments. It states that the group "does not accept the use of real animal fur" and names mink, fox, rabbit, sable, karakul lamb, chinchilla and raccoon dog among the examples; that it "does not accept the use of exotic skins or skins from wild animals"; that it "does not accept the use of angora rabbit hair or any other hair from animals reared in cages for their hair"; that it "does not accept the use of virgin down or feathers in our products"; and that it "does not accept leather or skin from fetal or newborn lambs".

What is left is tied to named external standards rather than to H&M's own word, and that is the part worth checking. Virgin sheep wool "must be certified in accordance with the Responsible Wool Standard (RWS) or equivalent"; alpaca to the Responsible Alpaca Standard; cashmere to the Good Cashmere Standard; mohair to the Responsible Mohair Standard; yak sourced "exclusively through Shokay". Recycled wool and animal hair, and all down and feathers, must be certified to the Global Recycled Standard, with the Recycled Claim Standard also accepted. Leather is allowed only "from tanneries certified by the Leather Working Group" and only from domesticated animals — cows, buffaloes, sheep, goats, pigs — with buffalo the only horn and bone permitted. Virgin animal fibres must come from farms certified to standards "built on the Five Domains Model".

Six separate certification bodies for six fibres is unusually specific, and it is checkable in a way a slogan is not: a shopper can read the standard rather than the marketing. Two caveats belong with it. The Responsible Down Standard, the scheme most often quoted on a jacket label, does not appear in the document at all — H&M's route to down is recycled content under GRS, not certified virgin down. And the policy itself says its goals "should not be understood as guarantees of specific outcomes, results or timelines".

A certification regime that changes on 31 December 2026

The standards named above are being consolidated. A footnote to the policy records that Textile Exchange's Materials Matter Standard "becomes effective on December 31, 2026" and "will be mandatory from December 31, 2027". In practice that means the RWS, RAS, RMS and GRS marks a shopper learns to look for this winter are the ones being folded into a single scheme over the next thirteen months, and that a garment bought in 2028 will carry a different certificate from one bought in 2026 without anything about the sheep having changed.

The group's material figures give the background. Its annual and sustainability report for 2025, published 26 March 2026, puts 91 per cent of commercial products in recycled or sustainably sourced materials, including 32 per cent recycled materials against a 30 per cent target for 2025, with 100 per cent recycled or sustainably sourced the stated goal for 2030. Those are group-wide figures across 4,101 stores at 30 November 2025 and are not broken out for womenswear, or for the UK.

The second-hand question, and the page the UK site does not have

Every other women's fashion page on this site answers one question: how many ways there are to buy the same clothes second-hand. H&M ought to have the best answer on the market, because H&M Group "has been the majority owner of Sellpy since 2019", and Sellpy — founded in 2014 — is described on hmgroup.com as a broad digital platform for second-hand fashion "currently available in 24 markets, including Sweden and Germany", having most recently launched its selling service in Poland and Czechia.

A British shopper cannot get at it from hm.com. The UK site carries a "Pre-Loved & Garment Care" item in its women's and men's menus, but the two pages behind it — pre-loved in store, and garment collecting — return no readable content at all, so neither the participating stores nor the terms of the take-back scheme can be checked. The French and Danish sites each carry a dedicated H&M x Sellpy page inside their women's sections; the equivalent UK address resolves to the ordinary women's landing page. The 24 markets are not listed anywhere H&M publishes, so whether Britain is one of them could not be established, and Sellpy's own UK site is closed to automated reading.

The one documented sale of pre-owned H&M stock in Britain was a promotion. On 23 October 2024 the group announced limited re-releases of pre-loved pieces to mark 20 years of guest designer collaborations, running as drops in seven stores from 24 to 31 October 2024, with London's Regent Street taking Friday 25 October and hm.com taking 31 October. The pieces spanned 23 collaborators from Karl Lagerfeld in 2004 to Rabanne in autumn 2023 and were, in the group's words, "sourced through partnerships with the online pre-loved marketplace Sellpy and global vintage retailers". No piece count and no prices were published, and it was a week.

So the count is nought, and the reason is a missing page rather than a missing business. The group also owns Looper Textile Co., which the garment collecting page's own metadata names alongside REMONDIS as the partners that "sort, resell, recycle, and repurpose" what shoppers hand in, and it opened the first store of A Retro Tale, a vintage accessories business, in Stockholm in spring 2025. None of that is reachable from a UK basket.

What the company behind the till has actually filed

The seller is a 46-year-old British company, not a branch of a Swedish one. Companies House shows H&M Hennes & Mauritz UK Limited, 01413450, as active, incorporated 6 February 1979, SIC 47710 "retail sale of clothing in specialised stores", with accounts last made up to 30 November 2024 and the next set, to 30 November 2025, due by 30 November 2026. Its confirmation statement was last dated 1 October 2025 with the next due by 15 October 2026. A second entity, H&M Hennes & Mauritz UK Services Limited, 12117130, was incorporated on 23 July 2019, and an old Northern Irish company, NF004044, trading from Rushmere Shopping Centre in Craigavon, was closed on 1 December 2010.

Above it, the nine-month report published on 24 September 2026 shows a business whose profits are rising faster than its sales. Net sales for the quarter to 31 August 2026 were SEK 57,189m against SEK 57,017m, a gross margin of 54.0 per cent against 52.9, and operating profit of SEK 6,037m against SEK 4,914m, lifting the operating margin to 10.6 per cent from 8.6. Across the nine months net sales were SEK 161,624m against SEK 169,064m — a fall — while operating profit rose to SEK 13,462m from SEK 12,031m. Chief executive Daniel Ervér credited purchasing, cost control and more efficient operations, and the report notes around 2 per cent fewer stores at the end of the quarter than a year earlier. For a shopper the relevant part is dull and reassuring: a company earning a 10.6 per cent operating margin can afford to pay a refund.

Two store counts on one company's website

H&M's own pages do not agree on how big it is. The markets and expansion page gives the H&M brand 3,579 stores, 82 store markets and 62 online markets at 31 August 2026, with the regional rows adding to 4,023 stores across the whole group. The investor relations landing page, reporting 2025, says "~3,000 stores in 81 markets", alongside SEK 200 billion of net sales and 125,000 employees. The annual report's key figures table gives 4,101 stores at 30 November 2025 and 61 online markets.

Those are three different numbers for roughly the same thing, and nothing on the pages explains whether the smallest is a rounded brand estimate or a different definition. No store count appears on this page as a result, beyond the dated table figures above, and the UK is not broken out in any of them: the group reports Western Europe, not Britain, so how many of those shops are within reach of a returning parcel is not published.

The safety register has nothing on H&M, and that is worth saying

The Office for Product Safety and Standards publishes every product safety report and recall notified in Britain, and it is the one place a retailer's claims meet an outside record. Searched on 14 November 2026, the clothing listing returns 255 product safety reports and neither "H&M" nor "Hennes" appears among them. The named clothing notices belong to other sellers entirely, including a George at Asda children's two-piece set, 2505-0161, recalled from end users on 23 May 2025.

The register is just as quiet on the subject of fur. A search on that word returns 10 reports and not one concerns animal fur, cat or dog fur, or fur mislabelling; the nearest match is a Cozee Home Luxury Faux Fur Heated Throw, 2202-0286, recalled from end users on 18 March 2022, which is an electrical matter. Two readings follow, and the second is the honest one. A clean register is a real point in H&M's favour. It is also evidence that the £75,000 fur offence is almost never prosecuted in a shop, which is precisely why a brand's self-imposed ban matters more than the law does.

Points at a pound each, and the 24-month clock on them

H&M's UK club is arithmetically simple. The membership page states that "one currency unit in a purchase equals one earned point" and, for Britain, "one pound = one point". Points then "expire 24 months after the month during which they were earned", so a point banked in November 2026 is good until the end of November 2028.

What a point is worth in money is not stated on that page, and nor are the thresholds at which rewards unlock, so a shopper cannot work out the return on a £50 basket before joining. The free delivery the page mentions for members carries no qualifying figure either. By contrast the costs are published to the penny — £3.99, £2.99, £24.99 — which makes the scheme the one part of H&M's UK offer where the price is clear and the benefit is not.

Where it wins

  • £3.99 standard delivery with no minimum order, so a single £12.99 dress is not penalised the way a free-over-£50 threshold would penalise it
  • Returning an online order to any H&M store costs nothing and refunds to the original card, against £2.99 per brand by post
  • 30 days to return full-price goods, 16 days more than the statutory minimum, stretching to 17 January for anything bought from 1 November
  • Real fur, exotic skins, angora, virgin down and fetal or newborn lamb leather are all refused outright by group policy, far beyond the cat, dog and seal bans that are all UK law requires
  • Each permitted animal fibre is pinned to a named external standard - RWS for wool, RAS for alpaca, the Good Cashmere Standard, RMS for mohair, GRS for recycled wool and down, Leather Working Group tanneries for leather - so the claim can be checked against a published scheme
  • 2,714 dresses listed from £12.99 to £119.99, with the ELIE SAAB x H&M collaboration at the top end
  • Prices and fees are all published in the terms rather than discovered at the checkout, down to the £24.99 large-item surcharge
  • Neither H&M nor Hennes appears in the 255 clothing product safety reports on the OPSS register
  • A 10.6 per cent operating margin in the quarter to 31 August 2026 on SEK 57,189m of sales, which is the boring fact that makes a refund likely

Where it falls short

  • The second-hand route does not exist for a UK shopper: the Pre-Loved & Garment Care pages return no readable content, there is no H&M x Sellpy page where the French and Danish sites have one, H&M holds no pre-owned stock on hm.com and runs no buy-back, and the 24 Sellpy markets are nowhere listed so Britain cannot be confirmed as one
  • Delivery and returns are charged per brand, so a three-brand basket costs £11.97 to deliver and £8.97 to send back, and a large item from a second brand adds £24.99 rather than £3.99
  • Sale items and designer collection articles get 14 days rather than 30, so the most expensive thing in the shop has the shortest return window, and sale stock is left out of the Christmas extension to 17 January
  • Every animal-material ban beyond cat, dog and seal fur is the company's own policy rather than law, no independent audit of compliance is named, and the policy says its goals are not to be understood as guarantees of specific outcomes, results or timelines
  • The Responsible Down Standard appears nowhere in the animal welfare policy, so the down in an H&M coat is covered by a recycled-content certificate rather than by the welfare scheme most shoppers look for
  • H&M's own pages give three store counts - ~3,000 for 2025 on the investor page, 3,579 brand stores at 31 August 2026 on the expansion page and 4,101 group stores at 30 November 2025 in the annual report - with no definition offered for the difference
  • A PayPal refund can take 14 days to be received, 14 days to be processed and 30 days to land, which is 58 days in the worst case
  • No UK figures are published for stores, sales or garment collection volumes; the group reports Western Europe and the 91 per cent sustainably sourced and 32 per cent recycled material figures are group-wide
  • The loyalty scheme publishes the earning rate and the 24-month expiry but not what a point is worth or what a reward costs, so the benefit cannot be valued before joining

Common questions

What does H&M charge for delivery and returns in the UK?

Standard delivery is £3.99 with no minimum order, with a further £3.99 for each external brand in the same basket and £24.99 per brand for larger products. A posted return costs £2.99 per brand, taken out of the refund. Returning an online order to an H&M store costs nothing.

How long do I have to return something to H&M?

30 days from receipt on full-price items, made up of 14 days to give notice and a further 16, then 14 days to send the goods once notice is given. Sale items and designer collection articles get 14 days. From 1 November, full-price and promotionally discounted items can be returned until 17 January.

Can I buy second-hand H&M clothes in Britain?

Not through hm.com. H&M Group has majority-owned the second-hand platform Sellpy since 2019 and says it operates in 24 markets, but it does not list them, the UK site has no H&M x Sellpy page where France and Denmark do, and the Pre-Loved & Garment Care pages return no readable content. The only dated British sale of pre-owned H&M stock was a one-week drop at Regent Street from 25 October 2024.

Does H&M use real fur?

Its group animal welfare policy says it "does not accept the use of real animal fur", naming mink, fox, rabbit, sable, karakul lamb, chinchilla and raccoon dog, and it also refuses exotic skins, angora, virgin down and fetal or newborn lamb leather. UK law itself bans only cat fur, dog fur and seal products, with fines to £75,000 on indictment, so the rest is H&M's own rule rather than a legal requirement.

How much do H&M dresses cost?

On 14 November 2026 the women's dresses category listed 2,714 styles from £12.99 for a draped halterneck to £119.99 for an ELIE SAAB x H&M embellished jacquard-weave kaftan, with a £19.99 lace-inset jersey dress, a £24.99 flared-skirt satin dress and a £54.99 bow-detail velour dress in between.

How do H&M loyalty points work?

One pound spent earns one point and points expire 24 months after the month in which they were earned. H&M does not publish what a point is worth in money or the spend at which rewards unlock, so the value of the scheme cannot be calculated in advance.

Our verdict

H&M is the rare large retailer that publishes its charges to the penny and then explains the one way to avoid them: £3.99 to deliver, £2.99 to post a return, nothing to hand it back in a shop, and 30 days to decide on anything bought at full price. On animal materials it goes a great deal further than the law, which forbids only cat, dog and seal, and it does so against six named certification schemes rather than a slogan - that is checkable, and it is the strongest thing on the page. The weaknesses are charges that multiply per brand, a 14-day window on the most expensive stock, and a second-hand offer that the group plainly has and the UK site plainly does not show. Buy from it on price and on the return terms; do not buy from it expecting to find its pre-loved rail.
