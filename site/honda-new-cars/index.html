Honda New Cars: Range Prices, the Undated Lists and the Warranty You Have to Keep Buying

Rates and terms checked 14 November 2026 · New Cars · Compare100 editorial team

The cheapest new Honda in Britain costs £18,995 on the road and it runs on a battery. That is the Super-N Advance, a 29.6kWh electric city car with a 128-mile WLTP range, and the figure comes from Honda's own Super-N EV 26YM price list, which breaks it down as £15,020.83 basic, £3,004.17 of VAT, a £18,930 recommended retail price and £65 of delivery, plates, first-year duty and registration fee on top. Every number on this page was read on 14 November 2026 from a Honda document or a Government one.

Honda's British range is eight cars and not one of them has a conventional engine on its own. Honda calls it “a lineup of advanced electric, full hybrid, and plug-in hybrid vehicles”, and the live range page bears that out: Super-N at £18,995, Jazz at £28,475, HR-V at £32,725, Civic at £34,325, ZR-V at £35,215, Prelude at £41,595, CR-V full hybrid at £43,995 and CR-V plug-in hybrid at £44,995. The gap between the electric car and the cheapest hybrid is £9,480, which is the opposite of the usual shape and owes nothing to a subsidy — as the grant section below explains, Honda does not get one.

Honda also publishes something most marques will not: a full derivative price list per range, with the basic price, the VAT, the retail price, the on-the-road figure, the carbon dioxide output, the first-year duty and the monthly company car tax at both rates. Eight of those documents were readable on 14 November 2026. None of them carries an effective date, and on three ranges the list and the live page do not agree.

Eight price lists, and where each range starts and stops

A Honda price list is a two-page PDF per range, and it is more generous with its arithmetic than most manufacturers manage. Each row gives the grade, the gearbox, the basic price, the VAT at 20%, the recommended retail price, the on-the-road price, the WLTP carbon dioxide figure, the first-year duty, the P11D value and the monthly company car tax bill at 20% and 40%. There is no configurator to work through and no login. A footnote on every one spells out what the on-the-road figure buys: “On the road prices include delivery charge, number plate allowance, 12 months Vehicle Excise Duty and Vehicle First Registration Fee (currently £55).”

The Super-N EV list is the shortest: one grade, Advance, in four paint combinations at £18,995, £19,295, £19,670 and £19,970 on the road. Nil carbon dioxide, £10 of first-year duty, a P11D of £18,025 and a company car bill of £13 or £14 a month at the basic rate.

The Civic e:HEV list runs three grades. Elegance is £32,960 retail and £34,325 on the road at 108g/km; Sport is £34,530 and £35,945 at 113g/km; Advance is £37,880 and £39,295 at 114g/km. Paint lifts the top Civic to £40,320 on the road. The ZR-V e:HEV list is the same three-grade shape and a tier dearer: £36,525, £38,765 and £40,895 on no-cost paint, at 130, 131 and 132g/km, rising to £41,920 for an Advance in Premium+ paint.

The Prelude list has one entry, a 2.0 i-MMD Hybrid Advance with an eCVT at £40,180 retail and £41,595 on the road, £42,295 with premium paint, 117g/km and 54.3mpg combined. The two CR-V lists are the top of the range: the full hybrid opens at £45,705 retail and £48,075 on the road for an Elegance and tops out at £52,295 for an Advance in Premium+, while the plug-in opens at £46,720 and £47,795 for an Elegance Tech and reaches £51,720. The Jazz Crosstar list sits between £30,795 and £31,795 on the road at 108g/km.

So the readable span is £18,995 to £52,295, a factor of 2.75 across eight cars — which, set against the 326 electrified and combustion models SMMT counts as being on sale in Britain in 2025, is a deliberately narrow shop window.

Not one of the price lists is dated, and three disagree with the live site

Every Honda price list ends with the same sentence — “All prices and specifications are subject to availability and alterations without notice” — and not one of the eight carries a date on which it took effect. The only chronological marking anywhere is a model-year code in the heading, “26YM”, and a dated folder name in the web address that a reader never sees.

That matters because on three ranges Honda's own two numbers are not the same number. The live range page gives the ZR-V from £35,215; the ZR-V price list gives £36,525 for the cheapest derivative, £1,310 apart. The CR-V full hybrid is £43,995 on the range page and £48,075 on the list, £4,080 apart. The CR-V plug-in hybrid is £44,995 against £47,795, £2,800 apart. Both figures in each pair are Honda's, published simultaneously, and there is no effective date on either document to settle which is current, so no single price for those three ranges is stated as fact anywhere on this page. Ask a retailer to confirm the figure in writing before ordering one.

The Super-N is the reassuring case and shows what the rest should look like: £18,995 appears identically on the range page, the model page, the buy-online page and the price list.

There is a second-order problem underneath this. Honda's content server still hands out price lists going back years, under folder names like December 2021, January 2022, October 2023, April 2024 and April 2025, and because none of them is dated inside the document they are indistinguishable from the current one. A “21YM All-new Jazz Price List” was still being served on 14 November 2026 quoting £19,910 on the road for the cheapest Jazz — £8,565 below today's £28,475, and nothing on the page says so.

The HR-V is the sharpest instance. Its newest price list on Honda's own server is the April 2024 edition, which opens at £31,495 against the £32,725 the live page now shows, and prints £210 of first-year duty for a 122g/km car. The rate for 111 to 130g/km is now £455. A live Honda document is quoting a tax figure that has since more than doubled.

A grant worth £3,750 to 21 other cars, and nothing to any Honda

The Government publishes the list of cars its Electric Car Grant applies to, and it is a short, dated page anyone can read. On 14 November 2026 it held two tiers: 21 cars entitled to £3,750 off at the point of sale, and 52 entitled to £1,500. The money is taken off the invoice by the retailer, not reclaimed later.

No Honda is on either list. That is a fact about the list rather than an inference about Honda, and the list does not say why any marque is absent, so nothing is claimed here about the reason. What can be said is the consequence. Honda's cheapest car is electric, costs £18,995 and would comfortably clear the scheme's £37,000 price ceiling, yet it competes against rivals whose own windscreen price is cut by £3,750 before anybody opens a finance quote. On a £18,995 car, £3,750 is 19.7% of the purchase price.

Qualifying is a matter for the manufacturer rather than the buyer: makers apply for vehicle eligibility, and the published criteria turn on a verified science-based emissions target or an equivalent independent assessment, with the scoring weighted 70% to where the battery cells were made and 30% to where the car was assembled. Honda's absence could change at any time, since the list is revised, so check it on the day rather than taking this paragraph as settled.

The duty bill ranges from £10 to £1,410 inside one electrified range

Vehicle excise duty on a new car is charged once at registration against the carbon dioxide figure, then at a flat £200 a year afterwards — £210 if paid over twelve monthly direct debits, or £110 for six months. The first-year bands run from £10 at nil emissions through £115 at 1 to 50g/km, £405 at 101 to 110, £455 at 111 to 130, £560 at 131 to 150 and £1,410 at 151 to 170.

Honda's range covers almost all of that. The Super-N pays £10. The CR-V plug-in hybrid, at 18 to 19g/km, pays £115. The Jazz and the entry Civic, both at 108g/km, pay £405. The Prelude at 117g/km and the dearer Civics at 113 and 114g/km pay £455. The ZR-V pays £455 as an Elegance at 130g/km and £560 as a Sport or Advance at 131 and 132g/km — a one-gramme step across a band boundary costing £105. And the CR-V full hybrid, at 151 to 152g/km, pays £1,410. That is a £1,400 spread inside a single manufacturer's showroom, all of it on cars Honda describes as electrified.

The £1,410 figure deserves its own sentence, because “full hybrid” sounds like a tax answer and on this car it is not one. A CR-V e:HEV sits in the same first-year band as a large petrol saloon, and the band begins one gramme below its 151g/km rating.

Then there is the expensive car supplement: £440 a year for five years from the second time the car is taxed, which is £2,200, on any car with a list price above £40,000, or above £50,000 if it is zero-emission. Three of Honda's ranges are plainly over the line — both CR-Vs and the Prelude — and the near-misses are where the useful arithmetic sits. A ZR-V Advance is £39,375 retail in no-cost paint, £625 under, and £40,075 in Premium paint, which clears the threshold by £75. A colour choice worth £700 therefore carries £2,200 of tax behind it. The Prelude, Honda's only single-grade range, clears £40,000 by £180 on its cheapest paint, so every Prelude pays the supplement. No Honda comes close to the £50,000 electric threshold, because the only electric Honda is £18,995.

One caveat on that sum, and it is Honda's lists that create it. The Government's rule is expressed as a list price, and Honda prints a retail price and an on-the-road price in separate columns — £39,375 and £40,895 on that same ZR-V Advance. Which figure the licensing agency measures decides whether a buyer is £625 clear or £895 over, and the margins above are too narrow to assume. Get it confirmed in writing before specifying the paint.

Three years of warranty, five annual renewals, and a servicing bill with no published end

Honda advertises eight years of warranty and sells three. The standard cover is 3 years or 90,000 miles from the warranty start date, whichever arrives first, and the eight-year figure is the ceiling of something Honda calls a Service-Activated Warranty. Its terms, last updated June 2026, extend the cover “in successive 12-month periods, up to a maximum of 60 consecutive months”, so 36 months plus five annual renewals reaches 96 months — or 100,000 miles, whichever comes first.

Each renewal has to be earned. “To activate each extension, the 3rd annual service and all subsequent scheduled services must be carried out at an authorised Honda Retailer or Honda Service Centre”, and each one must be logged in Honda's Digital Service Record. Miss one, or use an independent garage from year three onwards, and the cover stops at the last renewal. The terms also exclude “Cars that have not fully complied with the Maintenance Plan” during either period, so the servicing condition reaches backwards as well as forwards.

Two eligibility limits narrow it further, and both are easy to miss. The offer “is only available to non-commercial vehicles registered from 1st April 2026”, so a car registered in March 2026 has three years and nothing more. And clause 5.1.3 of the warranty conditions excludes “Commercial or business use (by way of example but not limited to rental, transport, taxis, etc.)” — which, read with the first condition, means the eight years is a private-buyer benefit and a company car driver cannot have it. The extension does at least follow the car: clause 10.1 says it “shall transfer to a person who has acquired your Car”, subject to the new owner keeping to the terms.

So what does keeping it alive cost? Honda's THREE Service Plan is £999 on a petrol-hybrid and £699 on the Super-N, covering “the first manufacturer-recommended scheduled services for up to 3 years or 37,500 miles, whichever comes first”. On a £28,475 Jazz that is 3.5% of the car; on an £18,995 Super-N the £699 is 3.7%. Note the mileage: 37,500 miles is 12,500 a year, well under the 90,000 miles the warranty itself allows, so a 20,000-mile-a-year driver exhausts the plan inside two years. The plan can only be bought “up until 14 days post vehicle registration” and is “not available for business customers”. And it stops exactly where the renewals begin: the fourth service onwards, which is the first one that actually buys an extension, has no published price anywhere on Honda's site.

What is unconditional is the corrosion cover, and it is the most generous set of terms on this page: 3 years unlimited mileage on painted surfaces, 5 years against exhaust perforation, 10 years on suspension, driveshafts, brake and fuel lines and the fuel tank, and 12 years unlimited mileage against corrosion of inner and outer body panels. Hybrid components on an e:HEV or e:PHEV are covered for 5 years or 90,000 miles. The high-voltage battery is guaranteed to keep at least 70% of its original capacity for 8 years, and that term is not service-activated.

One small inconsistency in the battery clause, offered as a measurement point rather than an accusation: the live warranty page gives the limit as “8 years or 100,000 miles”, while the standard warranty conditions give “8 years from the Warranty Start Date or 160,000 km”. Honda's own service-activated terms treat the two as interchangeable — “100,000 miles or 160,000 kilometres (as applicable in the country of registration or purchase)” — but 160,000km is 99,419 miles, so the metric ceiling arrives about 580 miles earlier than the imperial one.

An ombudsman gave Honda an award, and Honda's own complaints leaflet does not mention it

On 25 November 2025 The Motor Ombudsman announced that Honda UK had won its Customer Service Star Award in the vehicle manufacturer category, the brand's third such award and its second in succession. The entry that won it involved organising repairs to a Japanese import after a recall over suspension issues — a car Honda had never sold here. Nominations come from customers who contacted a manufacturer's head office, the ombudsman cut the field to three finalists per category (Alfa Romeo UK, Genesis Motor UK and Honda UK), and a panel of industry and consumer experts picked the winner. Chief Ombudsman and Managing Director Bill Fennell said “The calibre of entries in this year's competition was extremely high”.

That matters more than a marketing badge, because the award is only open to businesses signed up to the Motor Industry Code of Practice for New Cars and Vehicle Warranties — so the award doubles as confirmation that Honda is inside the code, and the code is a free, binding, published route to an independent decision about a new car. A buyer who cannot get a warranty dispute resolved has somewhere to take it that is neither Honda nor a court.

Which is what makes the paperwork odd. Honda Motor Europe Limited publishes an Internal Complaints Procedure giving a sensible ladder — a summary resolution within 3 business days, written acknowledgement within 5 business days, an update if the matter is still open after 4 weeks, a final response within 8 weeks and a referral right lasting 6 months. The only external body it names anywhere is the Financial Ombudsman Service. The Motor Ombudsman is not mentioned in it at all. A customer following Honda's own document to its end is pointed at the ombudsman for financial services, which cannot look at a faulty car, and not at the ombudsman whose award Honda has just won twice.

The reason the financial regulator appears at all is that two separately regulated companies sit at the same Bracknell address. Honda Motor Europe Limited — company 00857969, incorporated on 1 September 1965 and called Honda (U.K.) Limited until 1 April 1991 — is on the Financial Services Register under firm reference 996942. Honda Finance Europe Plc, company 03289418, incorporated 4 December 1996 and registered for “other credit granting not elsewhere classified”, holds firm reference 312541 and trades as Honda Financial Services. One address, two companies, two ombudsmen, and neither ombudsman can look at the other's half of the purchase.

The finance arm's own complaints guidance runs the same timescales and adds the motor commission position: six months to refer a normal complaint, extended for commission cases to “up to 29th July 2026 or 15 months after receiving a final response letter”. Its most recent dated reference is the financial regulator's final motor finance compensation rules of 30 March 2026. One wrinkle worth flagging: the complaints address on that document is printed as “Honda Financial Services Plc”, which is a trading name rather than a registered company, and the document does not explain the difference.

The live retail offer sits with the same company. On 14 November 2026 Honda was advertising a £2,000 Honda Deposit Contribution on every new retail Jazz at 5.9% APR representative over a 49-month personal contract purchase, for orders placed with Honda (UK) and approved by Honda Finance Europe between 1 November 2026 and 31 March 2027. On a £28,475 car, £2,000 is 7% off before any negotiation, and it is the first representative APR this site has been able to quote from a car maker's own finance company.

Euro NCAP scored the Civic well, the HR-V less well, and has not scored the cheapest Honda at all

Euro NCAP publishes a datasheet per model with four percentage scores, and the two Hondas it has assessed came out a long way apart. The Civic, published in November 2022 on a 2.0 hybrid Sport in left-hand drive, scored 89% adult occupant, 87% child occupant, 82% vulnerable road users and 83% safety assist, with the rating extended to the Elegance and Advance grades. The HR-V, reported in April 2022 on a 1.5 hybrid Advance in right-hand drive, scored 82%, 75%, 72% and 78%, applying to every HR-V.

The named weaknesses are more useful than the headline numbers. On the Civic, “Protection of the driver's chest was rated as weak”, “protection of the pelvis region was mixed”, and the car “lacks a system to prevent secondary collisions in the event of an accident”. On the HR-V, “protection of the pelvis was poor almost everywhere along the front edge of the bonnet”, “with some poor results recorded on the stiff windscreen pillars”, the rear passenger's chest protection was marginal, and readings “indicated weak protection of the head of the 10 year dummy” after the seat belt “had partially slipped off the shoulder” of that dummy.

Both cars share one shortfall, and it is the same sentence twice. The Civic “does not have a system to detect driver fatigue”; on the HR-V “there is no system to monitor for fatigued driving”, costing it the whole of a one-point driver-monitoring allocation. Euro NCAP has tightened its protocol since 2022, so neither result is directly comparable with a car assessed under a later cycle, and both should be read against the year printed on the datasheet.

Two of Honda's eight cars have no entry that could be found at all — the Super-N and the Prelude, which are the newest two and, in the Super-N's case, the cheapest. No ratings agency scores a car marque as a whole, so there is no brand-level safety figure to quote for Honda or anybody else; the assessment is model by model and two of these models have not had one.

What £18,995 actually buys: 29.6kWh, 128 miles and 162 litres

Honda's specification page for the Super-N gives a 29.6kWh battery, 162Nm of torque and a WLTP combined range of 128 miles, rising to 199 miles on the WLTP City cycle. Energy consumption is 14.8kWh per 100km. On direct current it takes 50kW and goes from 15% to 80% in 30 minutes; on alternating current the on-board charger is rated 6.6kW single-phase or 10kW three-phase, which Honda translates into roughly four hours on a 7kW home unit and roughly three on an 11kW one. No power output in kilowatts or horsepower is published on either the model page or the specification page.

The dimensions explain the price and set the limits of it. The car is 3,599mm long, 1,573mm wide and 1,608mm tall on a 2,516mm wheelbase, and the boot holds 162 litres with the rear seats up — 618 litres loaded to the window with them folded, 967 litres to the roof. For context, that 162 litres is a weekly shop and a cabin bag, not a family's luggage, and the 128-mile range is a commuting figure rather than a motorway one. At £18,995 with £10 of first-year duty and £13 a month of company car tax at the basic rate, the trade-offs are priced in honestly enough; they are still trade-offs.

One restriction is easy to miss and is stated plainly by Honda: the Super-N “is only available in Great Britain and is not available in Northern Ireland”. A Northern Irish buyer reading the £18,995 headline cannot act on it, and the cheapest Honda available to them is the £28,475 Jazz — £9,480 more.

Across the range, the carbon dioxide picture is worth stating against the market. SMMT puts the average new car sold in Britain in 2025 at 91.8g/km, down 10.1% on the year, with battery cars taking 19.2% of 2,020,520 registrations. Of the six Honda price lists that print a figure, four are above that average: the Jazz at 108g/km, the Civic at 108 to 114, the Prelude at 117 and the ZR-V at 130 to 132, with the CR-V full hybrid well clear at 151 to 152. Only the Super-N at nil and the CR-V plug-in at 18 to 19g/km come in under it. An all-electrified line-up is not automatically a low-emission one, and Honda's own documents are where you can see that.

Breakdown cover for three years, with a ceiling and a gap worth knowing about

Hondacare Assistance comes with a new car for 3 years from the date of first registration, and for up to 4 years from the date of sale on an Approved Used Honda. Honda Motor Europe Limited provides it and subcontracts the work, with overseas claims handled through The AA's Overseas Assistance Accounts. The territory is the United Kingdom, Isle of Man, Channel Islands and Republic of Ireland, plus named mainland European countries from Albania to Turkey and a list of Mediterranean islands.

The British side is the straightforward half: roadside attendance, recovery, and a Recovery Plus package offering a hotel at “maximum £100 per person up to the total sum of £300”, public transport on the same cap, or a replacement car up to 1,600cc for up to 72 hours. Europe is where the ceilings start to matter: roadside repair up to £175, car hire “up to £75 per day”, alternative travel up to £750 per party per trip, emergency accommodation at £65 a head a night to £400 per party per trip, storage up to £100 and up to £600 to send somebody out to collect the car — all inside an overall European limit of £2,000 per party, per trip, excluding unaccompanied vehicle recovery.

Two exclusions are worth reading before relying on any of it. There is no repatriation cover: getting an occupant home is a matter for personal travel insurance, and the document says so. And “Recovery Plus is not available following an accident” — so the hotel, the public transport and the courtesy car are all for breakdowns, not collisions, which is the opposite way round from how most drivers assume this works. Parts, fuel, oil, keys, ferry and toll charges are excluded, as is garage labour not provided at the scene, and recovery is refused on commercial, trade-plate and recently imported vehicles.

Like the price lists, the assistance document carries no reference number, version or effective date anywhere. It gives an overseas claim line on 01256 493730 option 6, a European reimbursement line on 01256 493580 and a Customer Solutions number on 0344 209 0556, and refers the reader to a glovebox atlas for the emergency number itself — which is of limited use to somebody reading it before they buy.

Common questions

What is the cheapest new Honda?

The Super-N Advance at £18,995 on the road, which is an electric car with a 29.6kWh battery and a 128-mile WLTP range. Honda's price list breaks that down as £15,020.83 basic, £3,004.17 of VAT and £18,930 retail, with delivery, plates, 12 months of duty and the £55 first registration fee taking it to £18,995. The cheapest Honda with an engine is the Jazz at £28,475.

Does any Honda qualify for the Electric Car Grant?

No. On 14 November 2026 the Government's eligible-vehicle list held 21 cars in the £3,750 band and 52 in the £1,500 band, and no Honda appeared in either. The list does not say why a marque is absent, and it is revised, so check it on the day. Manufacturers apply for eligibility themselves; a buyer cannot.

Is the Honda warranty really eight years?

It is three years or 90,000 miles as sold, extendable in five successive twelve-month steps to 96 months or 100,000 miles. Each extension requires the third annual service and every subsequent one to be done by an authorised Honda retailer or service centre and logged in Honda's Digital Service Record. It applies only to non-commercial cars registered from 1 April 2026. The 12-year body corrosion cover and the 8-year battery capacity guarantee are not conditional in the same way.

How much is road tax on a new Honda?

Anywhere from £10 to £1,410 in the first year. The Super-N pays £10 at nil emissions, the CR-V plug-in £115, the Jazz and entry Civic £405, the Prelude and dearer Civics £455, the ZR-V £455 or £560 depending on grade, and the CR-V full hybrid £1,410 at 151 to 152g/km. After the first year every car pays the £200 standard rate, plus £440 a year for five years if the list price exceeds £40,000 — which catches both CR-Vs, the Prelude and the top ZR-V grades.

Who provides Honda finance, and who handles a complaint?

Honda Finance Europe Plc, company 03289418, firm reference 312541, trading as Honda Financial Services from Cain Road, Bracknell. A finance complaint goes to it and then to the Financial Ombudsman Service within six months of a final response. A complaint about the car itself goes to Honda Motor Europe Limited, company 00857969, and then to The Motor Ombudsman under the New Car Code — though Honda's own complaints document does not mention that route.

Why do Honda's prices differ between its own pages?

Because the price lists are undated. Honda's live range page and its downloadable lists give different figures for the ZR-V (£35,215 against £36,525), the CR-V full hybrid (£43,995 against £48,075) and the CR-V plug-in (£44,995 against £47,795), and nothing on either document establishes which came later. Superseded lists from as far back as December 2021 are also still served, one of them quoting £19,910 for a Jazz. Ask for the price in writing.

Does Honda still sell a petrol-only car in the UK?

Not in its 2026 car range. All eight models — Super-N, Jazz, HR-V, Civic, ZR-V, Prelude and the two CR-Vs — are electric, full hybrid or plug-in hybrid, which Honda describes as “a lineup of advanced electric, full hybrid, and plug-in hybrid vehicles”. That does not make them all low-emission: four of the six price lists that print a carbon dioxide figure are above the 91.8g/km British average for 2025.

What does the Super-N give up to reach £18,995?

Space and range, mostly. The boot holds 162 litres with the rear seats up, 618 litres to the window with them down, and the car is 3,599mm long. WLTP range is 128 miles combined, or 199 on the city cycle. Direct-current charging peaks at 50kW for a 15% to 80% top-up in 30 minutes, and a 7kW home charger takes about four hours. It is also sold in Great Britain only.

Our verdict

Honda sells eight cars in Britain, every one of them electrified, and the cheapest is a 29.6kWh electric city car at £18,995 on the road with £10 of first-year duty behind it — £9,480 less than the cheapest hybrid, and arrived at without a penny of Government help, because no Honda is on the Electric Car Grant list. That is the strongest thing on the forecourt, alongside 12 years of unlimited-mileage body corrosion cover, two Customer Service Star Awards from The Motor Ombudsman in three years, and the increasingly rare courtesy of a published price list that prints the VAT, the duty band and the company car tax rather than hiding behind a configurator. The reservations are about the paperwork rather than the engineering. Honda dates none of those price lists, keeps five years of superseded ones live on the same server, and on the ZR-V and both CR-Vs its list and its live page differ by £1,310, £4,080 and £2,800 — so nobody should order one of those three without the figure confirmed in writing. The eight-year warranty is three years with five renewals attached to Honda servicing, closed to business users and to anything registered before 1 April 2026, and its own service plan runs out at exactly the point the renewals begin. A private buyer who services at a Honda dealer and wants a small, cheap, well-covered electric car is being offered a good deal here; a company car driver, a high-mileage driver or a Northern Irish buyer should read the small print twice, because three of the headline benefits do not reach them.
