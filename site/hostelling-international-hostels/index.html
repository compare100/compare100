Hostelling International Hostels: Membership, Booking Terms and What HI Actually Runs

Rates and terms checked 5 September 2026 · Hostels · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Hostelling International is the federation that a great many British travellers have used without ever learning its name. Its member associations run more than 2,300 hostels, it counts “more than 2,63 million members from around the world”, and it traces the network back to a German schoolteacher, Richard Schirrmann, who opened the first youth hostel in his own school at Altena in Westphalia in 1914. The federation itself was founded in 1932 as the International Youth Hostel Federation, adopted the Hostelling International name in 2006, and still describes itself as working “for Good, Not For Profit”. In England and Wales its member association is YHA; in Scotland it is Hostelling Scotland. Both are registered charities, and a card bought from either one works across the whole federation.

What separates HI from an ordinary bed-booking site is that it publishes a standard and pays somebody outside the organisation to check it. Hostels in the HI Quality & Sustainability programme are, in HI's own words, “certified every 3 years by an external auditor”, and the management system behind that programme is recognised by the Global Sustainable Tourism Council. The GSTC's published register lists it as “HI-Q&S (Hostelling International Quality and Sustainability Management System)”, owner Hostelling International, United Kingdom, version 1 July 2018, with recognition dates of 14 January 2016 and 16 June 2019. Very few accommodation brands of any size submit to a fixed outside audit cycle at all.

One structural point is better known before you book than after. HI runs the search and takes the deposit, but it is not the party selling you the bed. Its own booking terms say so without hedging: “Booking made on hihostels.com are a contract with the hostel or Youth Hostel Association who runs the hostel, not HI.” Nor do you have to join to use it — “You do not need to be a member…in order to make your booking” — although membership is compulsory at the door in some countries, and elsewhere non-members “pay a small surcharge or can purchase a membership card at the hostel”.

A federation of charities, not a hotel chain

The organisation behind hihostels.com is a British one, which surprises most people who assume a global hostel network must sit somewhere warmer. The International Youth Hostel Federation is company 02250706, incorporated on 3 May 1988 as a private company limited by guarantee without share capital, using the exemption that lets it drop “Limited” from its name. Its registered office is 7 Bell Yard, London WC2A 2JR, its SIC code is 94990 — activities of other membership organisations — and its last accounts were made up to 31 December 2025. Its own website terms of use put the position in one line: “the International Youth Hostel Federation (IYHF) operating as Hostelling International (“HI”)”, Registered Charity No. 1117014. The company has carried three names in its life, trading as HOSTELLING INTERNATIONAL between 3 April 2006 and 15 April 2010 and as IYHF TRUST before that.

Underneath the federation sit the national associations, and the British ones are older than the federation's UK company by half a century. YHA (England and Wales) is company 00282555, incorporated on 12 December 1933 as the Youth Hostels Trust of England and Wales Limited, registered at Trevelyon House, Dimple Road, Matlock, and filed under SIC 55202, which is the code for youth hostels and almost nothing else uses. Its stated charitable object has not been modernised into blandness: “To help all, especially young people of limited means, to a greater knowledge, love and care of the countryside, and appreciation of the cultural values of towns and cities.” Hostelling Scotland is the Scottish Youth Hostels Association, a Scottish charity registered with OSCR.

This matters for a practical reason rather than a sentimental one. A charity that owns its buildings prices differently from a platform that owns none, it publishes accounts you can read, and it has a reason to keep a hostel open in a valley that no commercial operator would touch. It also means the money you spend has a visible destination, which is unusual in online travel.

Who you are actually contracting with

HI's booking terms are short and, on the points that matter, unambiguous. The contract for the stay is with the hostel or the national association, not with HI. HI “collect[s] a non-refundable deposit payment at the time of booking” and the balance is settled with the hostel. Cancellation is free provided “the cancellation must be made at least 24 hours prior to the arrival time”, and the terms fix that arrival time for you whether or not you told anyone when you were turning up: “The arrival time at your destination hostel is considered as 18:00 (6pm) local time to the hostel.” In practice that means a booking for a Saturday has to be cancelled by 18:00 on the Friday.

Miss that and the charge is set out plainly. “If a cancellation is not received and you do not arrive for a booking of 1 night you will be charged 1 night, plus applicable taxes. If a cancellation is not received and you do not arrive for a booking of 2 or more nights you will be charged 2 nights, plus applicable taxes.” Even where you cancel in time, “the booking deposit is always non-refundable, unless stated otherwise in the booking conditions”.

The limitation of liability follows from the same structure and is worth reading twice: “In the event of a problem arising, our liability, if any, is limited to the fees paid by you on this website.” Since what you pay on the website is the deposit, that is the ceiling on any claim against HI itself. Disputes about the room, the cleanliness or the money you paid on arrival belong with the hostel and its association. The terms are governed by “the laws of England and Wales” and “[t]he courts of England and Wales shall have exclusive jurisdiction”, which is a genuine convenience for a British reader booking a bed in Ljubljana and a point most international booking platforms do not offer.

What a card costs in Britain, and what it takes off the bill

HI's own site is coy about price. The membership pages say only that “[p]rices vary depending on your place of residence” and that a membership “is valid for 12 months” from the date of purchase; the actual figure appears nowhere on hihostels.com. For a British buyer the number comes from the national association, and it is small.

YHA charges £20 a year for adult membership paid by direct debit and £10 a year for ages 16 to 25, and the return is “10% off every booking including pre-booked breakfast”. Under-26s get “an additional 5% discount…stacked on top of your 10% discount”. Hostelling Scotland runs a longer ladder, with rates that “apply from 5 April” and are “non-refundable”: £15 per annum for adults aged 26 and over, £6 per annum for a young person aged 25 or under, £150 for life, £30 per annum for groups of ten or more, £25 per booking for a temporary group, and £3 per overnight if you would rather not join at all.

On a £25 dorm bed, 10% is £2.50, so a £20 YHA card pays for itself over eight nights, or over roughly five for a 21-year-old stacking the extra 5%. A Scottish £6 young person's card pays for itself faster still. Two conditions attach. “Individual adults (over 16) must have their own membership” unless a family or group card covers them, and membership becomes “non-refundable, non-transferable to another person after activation”, with cancellation permitted but carrying no refund. Buying the card through a third party is the one route HI warns against: “If you book through an online travel agent or 3rd party, we cannot guarantee your HI membership will be honoured.”

The standard behind the badge, and how far it reaches

HI-Q&S is the best thing on the site and it is undersold. It is described as “a long-term programme of continuous improvement that allows hostels and associations to advance their quality and sustainability practices”, hostels are “certified every 3 years by an external auditor”, and the management system is GSTC-recognised. The federation's About page goes further and tells the reader that “all our hostels meet the HI standards, which guarantee a range of good quality safe accommodation, in friendly surroundings at affordable prices”.

The programme's own page gives the scale, and it does not match that sentence. “HI-Q&S is used in 32 Associations and over 400 Hostels worldwide.” Against a network of more than 2,300 hostels and 57 or 58 associations, that is a little over one hostel in six and roughly half the associations. The audit is real, the coverage is partial, and only the first of those two facts is advertised.

There is a British yardstick to set beside it, and no hostel listing on hihostels.com carries it. VisitEngland runs a dedicated hostel assessment scheme in which “[o]ne of our professional assessors will visit as a mystery guest”, and it publishes the standards in full. Minimum entry requires self-catering facilities, wet clothing storage, daily cleaning, a first aid kit and emergency contact information; bedrooms need “a minimum floor space per bed (or bunk) of 4 square metres”, a two-metre ceiling, an external window and beds “at least 1.9m (6'3") x 0.76m (2'6")”; kitchens need “a minimum of 4 cooking rings”. Star bands run 60–74% for three stars, 75–86% for four and 87–100% for five, with sectional floors underneath — four stars requires “no area to score less than 67%” and a “cleanliness minimum score 76%”. The scheme also runs two lower tiers, Quality Assessed and Visitor Ready, and states that “[n]ot all types of hostel are given a star rating, but all of them must meet a set of minimum requirements”. If a hostel you are considering in England holds one of those ratings, it is worth more than any score collected by the seller, and HI does not show it.

Booking a bed is not a package holiday

Two pieces of British law shape what you get when you press the button, and neither is mentioned on the site. The first is the Package Travel and Linked Travel Arrangements Regulations 2018, which define a package as “a combination of at least two different types of travel services for the purpose of the same trip or holiday”, the types being “the carriage of passengers; the provision of accommodation; the rental of cars, other motor vehicles, or motorcycles; [or] any other tourist service”. A hostel bed on its own is one type, so a bed booked alone is not a package and carries none of the insolvency protection or organiser liability that a package carries. Nor is it an ATOL matter, because no flight is involved. Booking accommodation by itself is one of the least protected purchases in travel, and that is true of every hostel platform rather than of HI in particular.

That regime is about to move. The Package Travel and Linked Travel Arrangements (Amendment) Regulations 2026 come into force on 6 April 2027, remove the concept of linked travel arrangements altogether — the instrument directs simply “Omit regulation 26 (insolvency protection and information requirements for linked travel arrangements)” — widen the definition of a package to catch separate selections and separate payments made on “a single visit to, or contact with, the trader's point of sale”, and add a third-party refund obligation running to 14 days. A traveller who books a bed and a ferry in one sitting from one seller after that date may find themselves better protected than they are now.

The second piece of law explains the non-refundable deposit, and it is the answer to a complaint people often make unfairly. Regulation 28(1)(h) of the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 excludes from the ordinary cancellation right “the supply of accommodation, transport of goods, vehicle rental services, catering or services related to leisure activities, if the contract provides for a specific date or period of performance”. The 14-day right to change your mind that applies to almost everything else bought online — “the cancellation period ends at the end of 14 days after the day on which the contract is entered into” for a service — simply does not attach to a room booked for a named night. HI's 24-hour rule is therefore a concession rather than a legal minimum, and a more generous one than a good deal of the hotel market offers.

The case for going straight to the hostel

Filter hihostels.com to England and you will find roughly seventy properties spread over seven pages, and all but a handful of them are YHA. That is not a criticism of the search — YHA is the English member association, so of course they are its hostels — but it does change the arithmetic for a domestic trip, because YHA sells the same beds itself and says openly what the intermediary layer costs it.

YHA's own book-direct page states that “[l]ast year, about £2 million of YHA's income was absorbed by third party booking agents”, money the charity would rather “invest…in improving young people's health, wellbeing and life skills”. It offers a price undertaking that no aggregator matches: “If you find a better rate elsewhere online, we will refund the difference”, applying where within 24 hours of booking on yha.org.uk you find an equivalent offer for the same property at a lower price. It says the direct site carries “our complete range of accommodation” and is “[t]he only way to redeem promotional codes and take advantage of special offers”. And critically, the 10% member discount and the extra 5% for under-26s are described throughout as applying to bookings “made online at yha.org.uk”.

The sensible conclusion is not that the HI site is worthless but that it earns its keep in different places. Abroad, where you have no account with the Slovenian or Portuguese association and no idea which properties are affiliated, one search across the whole federation with one membership number behind it is genuinely useful, and the same card that costs £20 in Matlock works at the door in Lisbon. At home, where the operator is a charity you can book with directly, is running a best-rate refund, and is quietly losing about £2 million a year to intermediaries, the case for the middle layer is much weaker.

Numbers on the site that do not agree with each other

Read HI's pages side by side and the network changes size. The About HI page describes “58 Member Associations operating over 2,300 hostels in hundreds of amazing destinations”. The network directory on the same site describes “57 Member Associations with over 2,300 hostels as well as affiliate hostels in 2 destinations”. One association is a trivial difference; publishing both figures on one website is the sort of thing that makes a careful reader check everything else.

The bigger gap is between the federation and its own members. HI says over 2,300 hostels. YHA tells its members that their card gives “[a]ccess over 4,000 hostels through automatic Hostelling International membership” and, on its dedicated page, “over 4.000 hostels in 90 countries”. Hostelling Scotland says membership “gives you access to over 4,000 hostels around the world with dual membership of Hostelling International”. Two national associations advertise a network almost twice the size the federation claims for itself, and nothing on any of the three sites reconciles the two counts. The likeliest explanation is that the larger number sweeps in affiliated and partner properties that HI does not count as its own, but no page says so, and a reader choosing on network size is being given two different answers by the same organisation.

A third gap is smaller and easier to fix. The membership pages promise “thousands of travel discounts at home and abroad”. The benefits page lists six partners: 15% off an Airalo eSIM, 15% off luggage storage with Bounce, 10% off European shipments with Eurosender, up to 10% on selected products through DiscoverCars, 25% off pickpocket-proof travel garments from Clever Travel Companion, and free or tip-based tours through Freetour.com. It adds that “[m]ore benefits and discounts might be available at your destination”, which is doubtless true and is not the same as thousands.

One page has simply been left behind. A Covid-19 measures page marked “Updated June 2022” is still published, still tells readers that “[m]any hostels in our network now require guests to present a proof of COVID-19 vaccination and/or a negative test result”, and still describes a refund concession “designed to ensure we survive during this turbulent time”. Four years on, that page is a liability rather than a service.

For scale, the market these beds sit in is growing slowly rather than quickly. VisitBritain's inbound forecast published on 27 August 2026 expects 44.2 million inbound visits to the UK in 2026 spending £33.9 billion, growth of 2% in both visits and nominal spending but a 1% decline in spending in real terms, putting volume at 103% and nominal value at 119% of 2019 while real spend is still only 90% of the 2019 level. European markets are forecast to grow 4% by volume and 7% by value while long-haul markets fall 3% and 2%. Short-haul European visitors are exactly the market a hostel bed is priced for, and they are the part of the market that is still expanding.

Where it wins

  • One membership and one search across a federation of more than 2,300 hostels and 57 to 58 national associations
  • Participating hostels are “certified every 3 years by an external auditor” under HI-Q&S, whose management system is recognised by the Global Sustainable Tourism Council
  • The federation is a UK registered charity (1117014) and its British members, YHA and Hostelling Scotland, are charities too
  • You do not need to join to book, and membership runs 12 months from the date of purchase rather than to a calendar year end
  • British membership is cheap: £20 a year at YHA, £10 for 16 to 25s, £15 at Hostelling Scotland and £6 for those aged 25 and under
  • A 10% member discount on YHA bookings, with a further 5% stacked on top for under-26s
  • Free cancellation up to 24 hours before an assumed 18:00 arrival, which is more generous than the law requires for a dated booking
  • Terms governed by the laws of England and Wales, with exclusive jurisdiction in the English courts

Where it falls short

  • The booking deposit is non-refundable in almost all circumstances and the terms never state what percentage of the price it is, so you cannot tell what you are risking until you reach the payment screen
  • HI's liability is “limited to the fees paid by you on this website” — that is, the deposit — because the stay itself is contracted with the hostel or national association rather than with HI
  • HI-Q&S covers “32 Associations and over 400 Hostels” out of a network of more than 2,300, while the About page tells readers that “all our hostels meet the HI standards”
  • The price of membership appears nowhere on hihostels.com; you have to go to a separate site or ask at the hostel door to find out what a card costs
  • HI's own pages give 57 and 58 member associations, and its British members advertise “over 4,000 hostels” against the federation's “over 2,300”, with nothing anywhere reconciling the two
  • “Thousands of travel discounts at home and abroad” turns out to be six named partners on the benefits page
  • A Covid-19 measures page last updated in June 2022 is still live and still tells travellers that many hostels require proof of vaccination or a negative test
  • Hostel listing pages show no nightly price until you enter dates and no independent quality rating at all, even where an English hostel holds a VisitEngland star rating
  • For a stay in England or Wales you are almost always booking a YHA hostel, and YHA's 10% member discount, promotional codes and best-rate refund apply only to bookings made at yha.org.uk

Common questions

Do I have to be a member to book an HI hostel?

No. HI states that “[y]ou do not need to be a member…in order to make your booking”. In some countries membership is compulsory to stay, and in others non-members “pay a small surcharge or can purchase a membership card at the hostel”. Where it is optional, a card bought in advance earns the member rate on the booking itself.

What does HI membership cost in the UK?

HI does not publish a price on its own site — it says only that prices “vary depending on your place of residence”. You buy through the national association. YHA charges £20 a year by direct debit for adults and £10 a year for ages 16 to 25. Hostelling Scotland charges £15 a year for those aged 26 and over, £6 for those 25 and under, £150 for life membership and £3 per overnight for non-members, with rates applying from 5 April.

When can I cancel without being charged?

The cancellation must be made “at least 24 hours prior to the arrival time”, and HI's terms treat your arrival time as “18:00 (6pm) local time to the hostel” regardless of when you actually intended to turn up. The deposit is non-refundable either way. Fail to cancel and not turn up and you are charged one night on a one-night booking or two nights on any longer booking, plus taxes.

Is my money protected if the hostel goes under?

Not by the package travel rules. A package requires “a combination of at least two different types of travel services for the purpose of the same trip or holiday”, and a bed booked on its own is one service, so the insolvency protection and organiser liability in the Package Travel and Linked Travel Arrangements Regulations 2018 do not apply. HI's own liability is capped at the fees you paid on its website. Paying by credit card, or holding travel insurance, is the practical answer.

Are HI hostels independently inspected?

Some are. Hostels in the HI Quality & Sustainability programme are “certified every 3 years by an external auditor” and the management system is recognised by the Global Sustainable Tourism Council, but the programme is “used in 32 Associations and over 400 Hostels worldwide” rather than across the whole network. In England there is a separate route: VisitEngland runs a hostel assessment scheme in which an assessor visits as a mystery guest, with star bands of 60–74%, 75–86% and 87–100%. HI does not display those ratings.

Should I book on hihostels.com or with the hostel directly?

Abroad, the HI search is genuinely useful and the membership discount travels with you. In England and Wales, the hostel is almost always YHA, which offers to “refund the difference” if you find a better rate online within 24 hours, applies its 10% member discount and 5% under-26 discount to bookings at yha.org.uk, and says direct booking is “the only way to redeem promotional codes and take advantage of special offers”. For a domestic stay, going direct is usually both cheaper and better for the charity.

Our verdict

Hostelling International is a well-run federation doing something unusual: publishing a quality standard, paying an outside auditor to check it every three years, and putting the whole thing behind a UK charity with accounts anybody can read. For a trip through several countries, one £20 card and one search across 2,300-odd hostels is a genuinely good deal, and the 24-hour cancellation rule is more generous than the law obliges anyone to be on a dated booking. The site's weaknesses are of presentation rather than substance — a membership price it never quotes, a quality programme that reaches a sixth of the network while the marketing implies all of it, three different network sizes across the federation and its own members, and a pandemic page four years past its usefulness. And for a hostel in England or Wales, the operator is YHA, which will refund the difference if you find the bed cheaper elsewhere and loses about £2 million a year to intermediaries. Use HI abroad, where the federation is the point; think twice before using it to book a bed forty miles from home.
