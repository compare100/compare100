Hostelworld Review: The Deposit, the Balance and What You Pay at the Door

Rates and terms checked 5 September 2026 · Hostels · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Hostelworld has been selling hostel beds since 1999, and it is one of the very few businesses in budget travel whose numbers you can actually check. Hostelworld Group plc floated on the London Stock Exchange's main market on 2 November 2015 at 185 pence a share, valuing the company at about £176.8 million, and it has published audited accounts ever since. The platform carries roughly 17,000 hostels across more than 180 countries and 13.7 million guest reviews, took 7.0 million net bookings in 2025 on net revenue of €93.8 million, and now sees 63% of its bednights booked through the app rather than the website.

The stay itself is not sold by Hostelworld, and its terms say so in plain words: "your contract for travel services (including accommodation) is with the travel services provider, not Hostelworld". You pay a deposit online — the company's own guide puts it at "about 12-15% of the total cost" — and you settle the balance in person at the hostel, usually alongside a local city tax the property is obliged by law to collect. That split decides who you deal with if anything goes wrong, so it is worth knowing before you book rather than after.

Free cancellation is offered on a large share of properties, and where a hostel sets a standard policy the deadline is, in Hostelworld's words, "normally at least 24 hours before your agreed check-in date". Cancel inside the window and the deposit comes back. There is also a Booking Guarantee for the rare occasion when a reception desk has no record of you, and a best price promise if you find the same bed cheaper elsewhere within a day of booking. Both have conditions attached, and both are set out further down this page.

Where the money goes when you press book

Two of Hostelworld's own documents describe the same payment in different terms, and reading them side by side is the quickest way to understand the business. The consumer booking terms call it "a deposit (being a part payment towards the total value of your booking)". The company's investor presentation is more direct about what it is for: Hostelworld says it will "charge hostels a commission when consumers book on our platform – we collect that commission directly from consumers in the form of a deposit". Both statements are true at once. The money you hand over at the point of booking is the platform's fee, taken from you rather than invoiced to the hostel, and the hostel is paid the rest when you turn up.

That makes the size of the deposit a published figure, because the commission rate is in the accounts. It ran at 15.3% in 2024, 16.2% across 2025, and 17.7% in the six months to 30 June 2026 against 15.8% a year earlier. Set that against a net average booking value of €13.43 in 2025 and €14.90 in the first half of 2026 and the arithmetic is undramatic: on a typical booking the platform keeps something in the region of €14 to €15, and the remainder of the bed price is settled at the hostel counter.

Sitting alongside the deposit is a booking fee. The terms mention it once, to say that "where We charge a booking fee, it is non-refundable in all circumstances", and no amount appears anywhere in the document. That matters more than it first looks, for a reason covered near the end of this page: the booking fee is also the number Hostelworld uses to cap what it can ever owe you.

The balance, the city tax and the costs the search page does not show

Because only the deposit passes through Hostelworld, everything else is a transaction between you and the hostel, and hostels charge for a longer list of things than most hotels do. Hostelworld's own guide to extra costs is unusually candid about it. City or tourist tax is collected "in cash, at check-in or check-out" and hostels are "required by law" to take it: roughly €0.20 to €2 per person per night in France, €1 to €4 in Italy, €0.50 to €4 in Spain, and in the Netherlands around 12.5% of the booking total, which on a week in Amsterdam is a real number rather than loose change.

The rest of the list is smaller but adds up. Bed linen is included almost everywhere; a bath towel is "often extra" at €1 to €3. Lockers can carry a daily fee of €1 to €3 or a refundable deposit on the padlock. Late check-in, where somebody has to stay awake to let you in, runs from €5 to €25. Breakfast is €3 to €6 for a basic spread and €10 or more for a buffet.

A live listing shows how this looks in practice. Castle Hostel 1004 in Bled, rated 9.4 from 2,932 reviews at the time of checking, states a city tax of €3.13 per person per night, luggage storage at €2 per guest and an early or late check-in charge of €5 per person — none of which is in the price you compare on the search results page. Two nights for two people there carries €12.52 of tax alone. Add the extras up before deciding that one hostel is cheaper than another.

Why there is no fourteen-day cooling off on a booked bed

Buy almost anything else online from a UK address and you get 14 days to change your mind. Accommodation is carved out of that right by regulation 28(1)(h) of the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, which excludes services tied to "a specific date or period of performance" — accommodation, transport of goods, vehicle rental, catering and leisure services. A bed booked for the night of the fourteenth is exactly that, so no statutory cancellation right ever attaches to it.

Everything you can cancel, then, you can cancel because a hostel chose to let you, not because the law says so. That is why the cancellation window varies property by property. Hostelworld's terms say deadlines are "normally at least 24 hours before your agreed check-in date, unless otherwise stated", and the Bled listing above sets its own at 72 hours. A non-refundable rate is a different animal again: book one and, in the platform's own words, "you will be charged the full amount of your booking" if you cancel. Simply not turning up is worse still, because the terms let the property charge your card "an amount not exceeding the charge for one night's stay" without asking you first.

The practical reading is that a free cancellation badge is a commercial concession worth checking the exact hours on, and that the discount on a non-refundable rate has to be big enough to be worth losing the whole booking over.

Tours, experiences and the package rule that changes in 2027

A bed on its own is a single travel service. Under regulation 2 of the Package Travel and Linked Travel Arrangements Regulations 2018 a package needs a combination of at least two different types — carriage of passengers, accommodation, vehicle rental or another tourist service — so accommodation booked alone carries no organiser liability and no insolvency protection. There is no ATOL cover either, because no flight is involved. That is true of every accommodation platform rather than of this one in particular, but it is worth stating plainly: if a hostel closes between your booking and your arrival, nothing in the package travel regime stands behind your deposit.

Hostelworld is more exposed to the next change than a pure bed platform would be, because it also sells tours and experiences alongside accommodation. The Package Travel and Linked Travel Arrangements (Amendment) Regulations 2026 come into force on 6 April 2027. They delete the linked travel arrangement regime outright, they widen the definition of a package to catch travel services bought under separate contracts where one trader facilitated "the separate selection of and separate payment for each of the travel services" during a single visit to its point of sale, and they replace the old right of redress with a refund obligation running to 14 days. A traveller who books a bed and a walking tour in the same session on the same platform may well be buying a package from April 2027 who is not buying one today.

Nothing on the consumer site addresses this yet, which is unremarkable given the date. It is a reason to check the position again next spring rather than a criticism of the platform now.

What the accounts show about a business built on €15 bookings

The published numbers explain a good deal about how the site behaves. For the year to 31 December 2025, reported on 26 March 2026, net revenue was €93.8 million against €92.0 million, on 7.0 million net bookings. Adjusted EBITDA fell 9% to €19.9 million and the margin slipped from 24% to 21%. Operating profit was €8.4 million and profit for the year €7.0 million. Direct marketing cost €45.3 million, or 48% of generated revenue — close to half of every euro taken. The company still paid a total dividend of 2.40 cent a share and bought back 3.1 million shares for £3.9 million under a £5 million programme, ending the year with €12.2 million of cash and €1.6 million of net debt.

The half-year figures published on 29 July 2026 were stronger. Net revenue rose 12% to €52.2 million on 3.8 million transactions, up just 1%, with the average transaction value up 11% to €14.90. Adjusted EBITDA rose 11% to €8.2 million at a 16% margin, profit for the period was €0.5 million, cash reached €15.0 million and the group moved to €2.5 million of net cash. Marketing absorbed 49% of revenue. Europe produced €26.0 million of the revenue, Asia, Africa and Oceania €16.4 million and the Americas €9.8 million, from a headcount of about 275.

Read those two sets together and the strategy is obvious enough: bookings are barely growing, so revenue growth has to come from taking more per booking. Chief executive Gary Morrison said as much, attributing the half's growth to a tool that "lifted our effective commission rate to 17.7%, up from 15.8% a year earlier". Nobody is hiding it, and a traveller who understands it can shop accordingly.

Reviews written only by people who slept there

The review base is the strongest thing on the platform and it is worth being specific about why. Hostelworld's review guidelines state that "we only accept reviews from customers who have actually booked and stayed at a property booked through our website". A great many travel and retail sites accept reviews from anybody with an account; requiring a completed, verified stay is a meaningfully higher bar, and with 13.7 million reviews collected over more than two decades the sample sizes on individual hostels are large.

Scores are a weighted average across six categories — cleanliness, location, staff, atmosphere, facilities and security — with cleanliness, location and staff carrying more influence than the rest. Hostelworld's own advice on reading them is sensible: treat 50 to 200 reviews as showing "reliable trends emerging", 200 to 1,000 as "strong consistency" and 1,000 or more as "long-term trustworthiness", and "scan the last 10 reviews for the most accurate snapshot", because a hostel under new management can turn in either direction faster than an average will show.

The rules around all of this changed on 7 April 2025, when the consumer protection provisions of the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 came into force. Posting or commissioning fake reviews is now explicitly banned, concealed incentivised reviews with it, and the Competition and Markets Authority's guidance of 4 April 2025 requires businesses that publish reviews to take "reasonable and proportionate" steps to detect and deal with them, with any incentive "made apparent". Penalties reach 10% of global turnover. In its first year under those powers the CMA opened investigations into 14 businesses, secured £760,000 of consumer refunds and £4.7 million of fines, issued 157 advisory and warning letters, wrote to 54 firms about reviews and opened five investigations off the back of it. A platform that only accepts reviews from verified stays starts that regime in a comfortable position.

Two guarantees, and the conditions under each

The Booking Guarantee covers the specific nightmare of arriving to find no reservation. If "the property reception agent has no record of your reservation", Hostelworld credits your account with the deposit you paid plus "US$50 extra for the inconvenience caused". Three conditions matter. You must contact the company within 48 hours of arrival through the guarantee form. The money arrives as account credit, not cash, and must be used within 6 months. And it does not apply where a listed property "ceases operation due to unforeseen circumstances" — a hostel that has shut down being, on the face of it, one of the commoner reasons a reception desk cannot find your name.

The best price promise says that "if you find a booking cheaper on any other website, we'll refund you the difference". You have 24 hours from making the booking to claim, and the rival deal must be an exact itinerary match on property, room type, dates and conditions. Blind bookings, membership and loyalty rates, rewards programmes, special promotions and offline group bookings are excluded. The timing is the part to note: the claim "will only be processed after you have checked out of the property and we have verified your stay", with payment by bank transfer within up to 14 days after checkout. You are being reimbursed weeks after the trip, not undercharged at the time.

Neither promise is unusual by the standards of online travel, and having both in writing is better than having neither. They are worth reading as insurance against specific failures rather than as a general assurance.

Elevate, third-party listings and how the results are ordered

The clearest gap between what the company tells investors and what it tells travellers concerns the ordering of search results. In its 2025 results Hostelworld described "the successful launch of ‘Elevate’, our marketplace monetisation tool, which increased our commission rate to 16.7% in H2", and the half-year statement credited Elevate with lifting the rate to 17.7%. What Elevate does mechanically is not explained in either document, and nothing on the consumer site sets out how hostels are ranked, whether a property that pays more appears higher, or how a traveller would tell.

That question has history in this sector. On 13 September 2019 the Competition and Markets Authority concluded action against the accommodation booking industry: 31 firms in total, six of them under formal enforcement — Booking.com, Expedia, Agoda, Hotels.com, ebookers and trivago — on practices including "pressure selling, misleading discount claims and the effect that commission has on how hotels are ordered", together with commitments to stop "giving a false impression of a room's popularity" and to "always display the full cost of a room upfront". Hostelworld was not among the 31 and those undertakings do not bind it. The 2024 Act does bind everybody, and drip pricing — showing one price and adding fees as the customer proceeds — is one of the practices it prohibits.

A second change is worth knowing about for a different reason. In December 2025 Hostelworld launched third-party inventory across 50 destinations and has since extended it to around 18,000, with engagement "strongest on searches with fewer direct Hostelworld results". Some of what a search now returns is accommodation sourced through another supplier rather than a hostel the company deals with directly. That is a reasonable answer to thin results in an unfamiliar city; it does mean the review base and the relationship behind a listing are not uniform across the page, and the site does not label which is which.

Booking from Britain, under Irish law

The corporate structure is more British than the Dublin address suggests, and it is worth a paragraph because it decides who you would be dealing with. The contracting party is Hostelworld.com Limited, registered at 8 Harcourt Street, Dublin 2, D02 AF58, VAT number 9689072B, and the terms are "governed by the laws of Ireland". Above it sits Hostelworld Group plc, company 09818705, incorporated on 9 October 2015 and registered at 1 Chamberlain Square Cs, Birmingham B3 3AX, under SIC code 64209, with accounts last made up to 31 December 2025. A third company in the group carries the history of a rival: Hostelworld Services Limited, company 02841908, was incorporated on 3 August 1993 as MBO MBI Limited, traded as HostelBookers.com Limited from 2 July 2003, and took its present name on 26 May 2015.

Two practical consequences follow. First, the liability cap. Hostelworld's terms limit its "maximum aggregate liability to you" to "the value of the booking fee that has been paid for that booking" — not the deposit, not the room price, the booking fee, which is the one number the terms never put an amount against. Second, there is no ombudsman. Accommodation booking is not a regulated financial service, so the Financial Ombudsman Service has no jurisdiction over it, and no equivalent scheme exists for travel platforms.

If a booking goes badly, the realistic routes are the hostel itself, the Booking Guarantee where it applies, and your card provider. Note one detail before assuming the last of those covers you: section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act 1974 bites where the cash price of the item is more than £100 and no more than £30,000, and a deposit of €14 or €15 on a cheap bed sits well below that floor. A chargeback through the card scheme is the more likely mechanism, and the terms on those vary by issuer, so confirm the position with your own bank rather than taking it as read.

Where it wins

  • Around 17,000 hostels in more than 180 countries, with 13.7 million reviews behind the scores
  • Reviews come only from people who booked and stayed through the site, which is a stricter test than most travel platforms apply
  • A small deposit holds the bed; the bulk of the money stays in your pocket until you arrive
  • Free cancellation is widely available, and the deposit is returned in full inside the hostel's window
  • Audited, published accounts — you can read the company's revenue, margins and cash position for yourself
  • The Booking Guarantee pays your deposit back as credit plus US$50 if the hostel has no record of your reservation
  • Six rated categories per property, weighted towards cleanliness, location and staff
  • App handles 63% of bednights and shows traveller profiles and chat for the hostel you have booked

Where it falls short

  • The booking fee is never given an amount anywhere in the terms, yet it is non-refundable in every circumstance and is also the ceiling on Hostelworld's liability to you — “limited solely to the value of the booking fee that has been paid for that booking”.
  • The deposit percentage is not stated in the booking terms either. A company blog post says “about 12-15%” while the group reported an effective commission rate of 17.7% for the first half of 2026, and nothing reconciles the two figures.
  • Hostels can now pay to improve their position through Elevate, the company's marketplace monetisation tool, and nothing on the consumer site explains how properties are ordered in search results or whether paying more moves one up the page.
  • Your contract is with the hostel and the terms are governed by Irish law, so a dispute about the stay is not something Hostelworld resolves for you and there is no UK ombudsman scheme covering accommodation bookings.
  • The best price promise is only paid after you have checked out and the stay has been verified, by bank transfer up to 14 days later — and the claim itself must be made within 24 hours of booking.
  • The Booking Guarantee pays a site credit rather than cash, it expires after 6 months, and it does not apply where a property “ceases operation due to unforeseen circumstances”, which is one of the likelier ways a booking fails.
  • The HOSCAR awards are described only as “voted for by YOU”, with no published methodology, no minimum review count and no threshold a hostel must clear to be eligible.
  • Since December 2025 some results are third-party inventory rather than hostels Hostelworld deals with directly, and the search page does not flag which is which.

Common questions

How much does Hostelworld take when I book?

You pay a deposit at the time of booking, which Hostelworld's own guide describes as "about 12-15% of the total cost". The group's accounts put its effective commission rate at 17.7% for the first half of 2026, up from 15.8% a year earlier, on an average booking of €14.90. The rest of the bed price is paid directly to the hostel when you arrive.

Can I cancel and get the deposit back?

Usually, if you are inside the hostel's window. Hostelworld says deadlines are "normally at least 24 hours before your agreed check-in date, unless otherwise stated", and individual properties set their own — 72 hours is common. Non-refundable rates charge the full booking value. There is no statutory 14-day cooling-off period, because regulation 28(1)(h) of the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013 excludes accommodation booked for a specific date.

What happens if the hostel has no record of my booking?

The Booking Guarantee credits your account with the deposit plus US$50, provided you contact Hostelworld within 48 hours of arrival and the claim is verified. The credit expires 6 months after it is applied, and the guarantee does not cover a property that has ceased operating.

Are the reviews on Hostelworld reliable?

They are drawn from a narrower pool than most: only customers who booked and stayed through the site may leave one, and there are 13.7 million of them. Scores are a weighted average across cleanliness, location, staff, atmosphere, facilities and security. Read the most recent 10 alongside the headline number, and treat a property with fewer than 50 reviews as unproven.

Do I need anything besides the balance when I arrive?

Bring cash for city tax, which hostels must collect and which is not in the price you compared: roughly €1 to €4 per person per night in Italy, €0.50 to €4 in Spain and around 12.5% of the booking total in the Netherlands. Towels at €1 to €3, lockers at €1 to €3 a day and late check-in at €5 to €25 are common extras.

Who do I complain to if something goes wrong with the stay?

The hostel, in the first instance — your contract for the accommodation is with the property, not with Hostelworld, and the platform's terms are governed by Irish law. There is no ombudsman for accommodation bookings, and Hostelworld caps its own liability at the value of the booking fee. A card chargeback is often the practical fallback; section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act 1974 will rarely help, because it applies only where the cash price exceeds £100.

Our verdict

For finding a hostel bed in a city you do not know, Hostelworld is hard to beat: 17,000 properties, 13.7 million reviews written only by people who actually stayed, and a deposit small enough that a change of plan costs you very little. Read it as a directory with a payment system attached rather than as your counterparty, because that is exactly what the terms say it is. Take the free cancellation rate over the non-refundable one unless the discount is large, note the hostel's own deadline rather than assuming 24 hours, and carry enough cash for the balance and the city tax. The things worth grumbling about — an unnamed booking fee that also caps the company's liability, a commission rate climbing from 15.3% to 17.7% in eighteen months, and no published explanation of how the search results are ordered — are worth knowing before you book, not reasons to book somewhere else.
