Hotels.com Hotels

Rates and terms checked 14 November 2026 · Hotels · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Hotels.com publishes a London lead-in price of £64 a night, and the figure already has the taxes and fees in it. On 14 November 2026 its cheap-hotels-in-London page was titled “The Best Cheap Hotels in London from £64”, and every rate beside every property on it carried the words “includes taxes & fees” — the Tower Hotel by Thistle at £139, Zedwell Piccadilly Circus at £115, the Strand Palace at £175, Park Plaza London Westminster Bridge at £215, Sea Containers London at £259. That is a genuinely useful thing about this site and it is worth saying first, because the regulator spent most of 2025 and 2026 telling the industry to do exactly that.

The second thing to know is the loyalty maths, because it is the only part of the proposition that is specific to this brand. Hotels.com Rewards pays £100 in Hotels.comCash every time you reach ten nights, a pound of which buys a pound of discount. The catch sits in the definition of a night: it only counts if the room cost £75 or more, taxes and fees included, and was paid to Hotels.com. The £64 room in the headline above earns nothing at all.

And one structural fact, because it decides who you ring if something goes wrong. Hotels.com is not a British company and does not sell a holiday. Its terms, last revised on 3 November 2026, name Expedia, Inc. of 1111 Expedia Group Way W, Seattle as the company you contract with, and the only British company anywhere in them is Travel Partner Exchange UK Limited, which takes the payment. There is no ATOL number on the site because there is nothing here that needs one: this is rooms, not packages.

The £64 room, and the footnote that tells you what it measures

Three of the big booking platforms publish no London starting price whatsoever. Hotels.com does, in the title of its own page, and it does it with the tax in. On 14 November 2026 the cheap-hotels-in-London page read “The Best Cheap Hotels in London from £64”, and the cheapest listing on it was the Otherwander Soho Pod Hotel at exactly that figure. The same device is used at neighbourhood level: the City of London page was titled “from £72” the same day, the London City Centre page “from £141”, and the Tower Hamlets page “from £33”.

The footnote under those prices is the part worth reading twice. It says: “Lowest nightly price found within the past 24 hours based on a 1 night stay for 2 adults. Prices and availability subject to change. Additional terms may apply.” So the number is a historical low, not an offer. It describes something somebody could have booked at some point in the previous day, for two people, for one night, and it is honest enough to say so in writing — which is more than most of this sector manages.

Those four figures do not describe four different floors, and that is the thing to understand before trusting any of them. On three of the pages the cheapest property listed was the same hotel — the Otherwander Soho Pod Hotel — shown at £64 on one and at £73 on the other two for 15 to 16 November. So the London City Centre page was titled “from £141” with a £73 room sitting underneath it, and the City of London page was titled “from £72” with a £73 room as its cheapest. The Tower Hamlets page was titled “from £33” on one reading and “from £59” on another within the same hour, and the cheapest property on it was the Ibis budget London Heathrow Central at £34 — a hotel at the airport rather than in the borough. The Greater London page carried no lead-in price in its title at all and opened on the Twenty Nevern Square Hotel at £117, marked down from £131.

For orientation, the sorted London list on 14 November put real rooms at £115 to £259 a night for the middle of the city, and the multi-night deals priced out at £144 a night at Marlin Waterloo, £150 at the Kensington Hideaway and £251 at the Andaz London Liverpool Street. Treat the £64 as the floor of the catalogue rather than the price of a trip.

Ten nights buys £100, and the cheap rooms are not nights

Hotels.com has stopped pooling its loyalty currency with its sister brands and gone back to a scheme of its own, which makes it the one reason to prefer this site over any other window onto the same catalogue. The promise is flat and easy to read: “Get £100 in Hotels.comCash every time you reach 10 nights”, and “£1 of Hotels.comCash = £1”. It is free to join, there are no blackout dates, no minimum spend to start redeeming, and the balance can be spent in pieces rather than all at once.

A flat reward has a consequence that a percentage does not, and it is the single most useful calculation on this page. Because the £100 does not scale with what you spend, the rate you earn falls as your taste improves. Ten nights at the £75 qualifying floor is £750 of spending for £100 back, which is 13.3%. Ten nights at the £139 the Tower Hotel was asking is £1,390 for the same £100, which is 7.2%. Ten nights at Sea Containers London at £259 is £2,590 for £100, which is 3.9%. The scheme is at its most generous to people booking the cheapest rooms that still qualify, and it is worth planning around that rather than around the headline.

The floor itself is where a London shopper should pay attention. A qualifying night has to cost “£75+” including taxes and fees and has to be paid to Hotels.com. The £64 room in this page’s own headline does not qualify. Neither does the £65 or the £73 the Otherwander Soho Pod Hotel was quoted at on two other pages the same day, nor the £72 the City of London page starts at, nor the £34 Heathrow room. A traveller who books London’s genuinely cheap end can stay twenty nights a year on this site and collect nothing.

Three further rules are worth having in front of you. Nights where Hotels.comCash was redeemed do not count towards the next ten, so the reward cannot compound. Car hire and insurance do not count. And the balance is conditional on activity: pending nights and pending Hotels.comCash survive only if you make an eligible booking or a redemption at least once every twelve months, while confirmed Hotels.comCash survives on the same terms every eighteen months. Nights stay pending until the tenth is stayed, and a stay turns confirmed three days after the trip ends.

Four tiers, and the count goes back to zero in January

Status runs on a separate counter from the reward, and it is counted in plain nights rather than in spending: Blue at 0 to 4 nights stayed, Silver at 5 to 14, Gold at 15 to 29 and Platinum at 30 or more. The reset is the detail that catches people — “nights toward your tier progress reset to 0 at the start of each year”, so twelve nights in October and December buys Silver for a few weeks and then starts again.

Thirty nights inside one calendar year is a demanding target for a leisure traveller. At the £75 qualifying floor, Platinum represents £2,250 of stays in twelve months, and at the £139 the Tower Hotel wanted it is £4,170. The rewards page does not state whether the £75 floor that governs the £100 reward also governs a tier night, which leaves a reader unable to work out whether a run of cheap rooms moves them up the ladder or not; that needs confirming with the company before anybody plans around it.

The discounts attached to membership are advertised in round numbers and bounded by the word “up to”. Member prices are “up to 20%” and are “available to Hotels.com® app users and One Key™ members when you’re signed in”, on “selected hotels and selected dates only”. A second hotel booked for the same trip unlocks “up to 25% off on eligible hotels”, provided the two stays are “within 1,000 miles of each other” and “within 3 days of each other”, with no coupon and the saving applied instantly. What none of those pages says is what the percentage is calculated on — room rate or the total with taxes and fees — which matters on a site that otherwise quotes the total.

One piece of housekeeping has plainly not finished. The UK rewards pages talk about Hotels.comCash and describe pre-launch balances as having been “formerly known as ‘OneKeyCash’”, while the member-deals page still restricts its discount to “One Key™ members” and the help centre still states that “OneKeyCash is not redeemable for cash and can only be used on Hotels.com, Expedia and Vrbo”. Both currencies are described on the same site in the present tense. Pre-transition trips, the rewards page says, “do not count towards your £100 reward”.

What the regulator has asked this sector to fix, twice

Hotels.com is one of six companies that gave the Competition and Markets Authority formal undertakings about how they sell rooms. The announcement of 13 September 2019 names them: Expedia, Booking.com, Agoda, Hotels.com, ebookers and trivago, alongside 25 other firms that signed up to sector-wide principles. The CMA said it had “serious concerns it had around issues like pressure selling, misleading discount claims” and about “the effect that commission has on how hotels are ordered on sites”. Two of the principles are directly visible on the pages described above: “not giving a false impression of a room’s popularity” and “always displaying the full cost of a room upfront”.

The rules behind that have since been rewritten and sharpened. The CMA published its price transparency guidance, reference CMA209, on 18 November 2025, with a plain-English summary added on 7 January 2026; the full document runs to 58 pages. It tells a trader that “in the invitation to purchase, you must give the customer the total price of the product”, that it is “illegal to hide additional fees, taxes or other charges” later in the journey — “sometimes called ‘drip pricing’” — and to “always include mandatory local charges (like resort fees) and taxes in the invitation to purchase”. It also covers partitioned pricing, where the parts of a price are shown and the total is not. A business that gets this wrong can be “fined up to 10% of your turnover, or £300,000, whichever is greater” and ordered to compensate customers.

Measured against that, a London listing that reads “The price is £139 includes taxes & fees” is doing the right thing, and doing it on the search results page rather than at the payment screen. What the platform cannot do is reach a charge the hotel collects itself at the desk; a city tax or a resort fee payable on arrival sits outside the figure on the screen whoever shows it, and Hotels.com states that “there is no single cancellation policy on Hotels.com — instead, each property sets its own terms”.

For completeness on the older history: the CMA’s separate monitoring project into online travel agents’ pricing opened on 16 September 2015 and closed on 6 April 2017, naming Booking.com and Expedia, and was last updated on 20 August 2020 when both confirmed their commitments on wide parity clauses would continue to apply in the UK. It was run jointly with the European Commission and nine other competition agencies. Hotels.com is not named in that case, and it would be wrong to imply it is bound by it.

No ATOL, no package, and an insolvency clause pointing at EU law

Nothing on the UK site sells a flight, and no ATOL number appears anywhere in the terms. That is the correct state of affairs rather than an omission — the Civil Aviation Authority’s scheme protects a package that includes a flight, and a room booked on its own is not one — but a reader who has seen an ATOL number on a sister brand should know why it is absent here and what stands in its place.

What stands in its place is narrow, and the wording is worth quoting. Where a booking amounts to a linked travel arrangement, the terms say “you will NOT benefit from rights applying to packages under Directive (EU) 2015/2302”, and that “we have, as required by EU law, protection in place to refund your payments to us for services not performed”. The cover is insolvency protection with Liberty Mutual Insurance Europe SE, with International Passenger Protection acting as brokers, reachable on 0345 2661872 or at ippclaims@uk.sedgwick.com, and it applies where services are denied because of Hotels.com’s own insolvency. It expressly “does not cover contracts with parties other than us”.

Two limits follow from that sentence, and both are easy to trip over. If the money goes to the hotel instead of to the platform, the terms state plainly that “if any of your payments go directly to the relevant Travel Provider, we have not taken out any protection” — so a pay-at-the-property booking has no insolvency cover from this company at all. And the clause is pinned to a European directive rather than to the United Kingdom’s own Package Travel and Linked Travel Arrangements Regulations 2018, on a page served to British customers under English law. Both can be true at once and neither is unlawful, but a reader booking from Britain should not have to work out which rulebook the protection sits under.

Who takes the money, and where an argument would be heard

The terms were last revised on 3 November 2026 and are unusually direct about the division of labour: “we own and operate our Service and the Travel Providers provide the Travel Services to you.” The company behind “we” is Expedia, Inc. of 1111 Expedia Group Way W, Seattle WA 98119. Where the site itself is the travel provider on a package, that company is Travelscape, LLC of 5000 W. Kearney Street, Springfield, Missouri 65803. A payment table names the company that takes the money country by country, and the United Kingdom row reads Travel Partner Exchange UK Limited.

A search of the Companies House register on 14 November 2026 returned no British company called Hotels.com — the register holds a number of unrelated firms with “hotels” in the name and nothing belonging to this brand. So the British end of a Hotels.com booking is a payment company, and the counterparty on the contract is American. The governing law clause goes the other way and in the customer’s favour: “these Terms are governed by the laws of England and Wales”, with exclusive jurisdiction in the English courts and the option for Scottish and Northern Irish residents to choose their own. One carve-out cuts across it — the law applying to a payment transaction follows the location of whichever company took the payment.

On liability the terms promise less than a reader might expect and state no number. There is no monetary cap and no multiple of the booking price; instead the group “will not be liable for any direct, indirect, punitive, special, incidental or consequential losses or damages”, while preserving what cannot be excluded — “we do not limit or exclude our liability for fraud or personal injury or death”. The route for a complaint is internal: raise it with Hotels.com or the hotel, ideally before or during the trip, and “you are encouraged to raise any complaints within 30 days after travel”, by post to Seattle with a copy to supportUK@chat.hotels.com, or by telephone on 0203 027 6612 from the UK. No ombudsman, arbitration scheme or alternative dispute resolution body is named anywhere, so after the internal process the next step is a court.

The reviews, the cancellation window and the three answers to one question

Review scores sit out of 10 with a word attached — “9.0 out of 10, Wonderful, (9,440 reviews)” on one property read on 14 November 2026 — and the site describes them as “unfiltered, unedited, and written by real customers”. The mechanics it does publish are sensible. A review is written from the “Completed” tab of your own bookings, the site prompts you by email or push notification shortly after the stay, and the window is finite: “this will only be possible for 6 months after the end of a trip”. What it does not publish is how a score is composed, whether categories are weighted, how long a review stays up, or what the moderation rules are; there is a content guidelines link and no detail behind the claim of verification.

Cancellation is set by the hotel, not by the platform, and the free-cancellation page says so outright. It then gives the deadline three different ways on one page: the introduction offers a full refund “up to 24 hours before your arrival date”, the refund table says fully refundable rates cancel “24–48 hours before check-in (varies by property)”, and the questions below put it at “24 or 48 hours”. A footnote adds that “some hotels require you to cancel more than 24 hours before check-in” and that “some rates may require same-day cancellation”. Cut-offs run on the hotel’s local time, which on a long-haul booking is not the time on your phone.

The refund timings, by contrast, are specific and worth knowing: Hotels.com “typically processes refunds within 24 hours of cancellation”, the money can take “up to 7 business days to appear on your account”, and a refund to a gift card is reissued as a new card by email “within 4 hours”. The practical advice is the same as it always is on a platform that resells somebody else’s terms: read the cancellation line on the room you are actually buying, because the three sentences above describe three different rooms.

The scale behind the search box, and what is published about this brand

The money question is answerable from filings rather than marketing copy, because the owner reports quarterly. In the results released on 4 November 2026 for the three months to 30 September, Expedia Group took $34,008 million of gross bookings, up 11%, of which lodging was $24,875 million, up 10%. Revenue was $4,938 million and net income $1,030 million. Booked room nights came to 115.7 million in the quarter, up 7%, which by our own arithmetic is about 1.26 million room nights a day and an average lodging booking of roughly $215 a night.

Direct selling and marketing cost $2,184 million in the same three months. Set against the room nights, that is about $18.88 of marketing spend behind every booked night — a figure worth holding in mind when a results page starts telling you how many people are looking at a room. The consumer business grew 6% to $3,065 million while the arm that supplies the same hotel inventory to other companies’ booking forms grew 23% to $1,708 million, so the catalogue is increasingly reaching travellers through somebody else’s website.

About Hotels.com specifically, the filing says almost nothing. The brand appears once, in a list of “three flagship consumer brands – Expedia®, Hotels.com®, and Vrbo®”, with no revenue, no bookings, no room nights and no customer count of its own. Nor does the brand publish a property total: the Greater London page gives no count of hotels, the member-deals page says “hundreds of thousands of hotels” in its heading and “thousands of hotels worldwide” in its own description, and the London list is introduced only as properties “selected based on real traveller reviews and popularity among guests who’ve booked a night in London on Hotels.com”. The practical reading is that choosing between this brand and its siblings is a choice of loyalty scheme and interface, not of inventory — which is why the £100 arithmetic above is the part that actually decides anything.

Where it wins

  • Publishes a London lead-in price of £64 a night in the title of its own page, where three of the biggest rival platforms publish none at all
  • Every rate on the search results carries “includes taxes & fees”, which is what the CMA’s price transparency guidance of 18 November 2025 asks for and what the 2019 undertakings promised
  • The “from” price is defined in a footnote rather than left to the reader: “lowest nightly price found within the past 24 hours based on a 1 night stay for 2 adults”
  • The loyalty reward is a flat, legible £100 per ten nights at a stated £1-for-£1 value, with no blackout dates, no minimum redemption and free membership
  • Earning works out at 13.3% for somebody booking at the £75 qualifying floor, which is a better return than a percentage-based scheme pays on the same spend
  • Governed by the laws of England and Wales with exclusive jurisdiction in the English courts, and Scottish and Northern Irish residents may choose their own
  • Insolvency protection is actually named and reachable — Liberty Mutual Insurance Europe SE, brokered by International Passenger Protection on 0345 2661872

Where it falls short

  • The lead-in price does not always match the page it is printed on. The London City Centre page was titled “from £141” on 14 November 2026 while the cheapest property listed on it was £73, and the Tower Hamlets page gave “from £33” and “from £59” within the same hour, with its cheapest room a budget hotel at Heathrow airport rather than in the borough.

  • No monetary limit on liability appears anywhere in the terms — no cap and no multiple of the booking price — only a refusal of indirect and consequential loss, which leaves a reader with no figure to plan around.
  • No ombudsman, arbitration scheme or alternative dispute resolution body is named. After an internal complaint raised within 30 days of travel, the next step is the English courts.

  • The company publishes no verification mechanics behind the words “verified customer reviews”: no scoring categories, no weightings, no moderation rules and no statement of how long a review stays up.
  • The price guarantee page on the UK site returns no readable terms to anything other than a live browser session, so the conditions, claim deadline and exclusions could not be checked for this review and need confirming before relying on them.

Common questions

What is the cheapest London room on Hotels.com?

Its own cheap-hotels-in-London page was titled “The Best Cheap Hotels in London from £64” on 14 November 2026, and the cheapest listing on it was the Otherwander Soho Pod Hotel at £64 including taxes and fees. The footnote defines that as the “lowest nightly price found within the past 24 hours based on a 1 night stay for 2 adults”, so it is a historical low rather than an offer. Real mid-city rooms on the sorted London list that day ran from £115 to £259 a night, and the same hotel appeared at £73 on two other London pages the same afternoon.

How many nights do I need for the £100 reward, and do cheap rooms count?

Ten, and only if each one cost £75 or more including taxes and fees and was paid to Hotels.com. Rooms under that price do not count towards the ten at all, and neither do nights paid for with Hotels.comCash, car hire or insurance. The £100 is paid in Hotels.comCash at £1 to the pound, is not redeemable for cash, and turns confirmed three days after your tenth night is completed.

Is a Hotels.com booking ATOL protected?

No, and it does not need to be. ATOL covers a package that includes a flight, and the UK site sells rooms. The protection that does exist is insolvency cover with Liberty Mutual Insurance Europe SE, brokered by International Passenger Protection on 0345 2661872, and it covers money paid to Hotels.com for services not performed. If you pay the hotel directly, the terms say plainly that no protection has been taken out.

Who am I actually buying from?

Expedia, Inc. of 1111 Expedia Group Way W, Seattle WA 98119, under terms last revised on 3 November 2026. A UK payment is taken by Travel Partner Exchange UK Limited, and a Companies House search on 14 November 2026 found no British company called Hotels.com. The terms are governed by the laws of England and Wales with exclusive jurisdiction in the English courts, although the law applying to a payment follows the location of whichever company took it.

What happens if I need to complain?

Raise it with Hotels.com or the hotel, ideally before or during the trip, and within 30 days of travel. The UK line is 0203 027 6612, and written complaints go to Seattle with a copy to supportUK@chat.hotels.com. No ombudsman or alternative dispute resolution scheme is named anywhere in the terms, so if the internal process does not settle it the next step is a court. The terms state no monetary cap on liability.

Can I cancel for free?

It depends entirely on the rate, because “there is no single cancellation policy on Hotels.com — instead, each property sets its own terms”. The platform’s own page gives the deadline as “up to 24 hours before your arrival date” in one place, “24–48 hours before check-in” in another and “24 or 48 hours” in a third, with some rates requiring same-day cancellation and cut-offs running on the hotel’s local time. Refunds are processed within 24 hours and can take up to 7 business days to show.

Do the member discounts stack?

They are advertised separately and both are bounded by “up to”. Member prices are “up to 20%” for signed-in app users and members, on selected hotels and dates. Booking a second hotel for the same trip unlocks “up to 25% off on eligible hotels”, provided the two stays are within 1,000 miles and 3 days of each other. Neither page states whether the percentage is taken off the room rate or the total with taxes and fees.

Our verdict

Hotels.com is the clearest-priced of the big booking platforms and the only one that prints a London starting figure — £64 a night on 14 November 2026, with the taxes and fees already in it. That is worth something real, and it is what the regulator has twice asked this industry to do. Where it needs care is the loyalty scheme, because that is the only thing separating this site from the identical catalogue sold under its sister brands. Ten nights earns a flat £100, which is 13.3% back to somebody booking at the £75 qualifying floor and 3.9% to somebody booking at £259, and rooms under £75 earn nothing at all. If your London habit is £60 and £70 rooms, the scheme will never pay you; if it is £80 to £110 rooms booked ten nights a year, it is among the better returns in travel. Book the room on the price, check the cancellation line on the rate you are actually buying rather than on the page that describes it three ways, and pay the platform rather than the property if you want the insolvency cover to mean anything.
