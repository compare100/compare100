What £259 buys, and what the next rungs cost

The cheapest machine in the shop on 14 November 2026 was the HP Laptop 14-ep2000na at £259.00: an Intel N150 with four cores, 8 GB of memory soldered to the board, 128 GB of UFS storage rather than an SSD, a 14-inch 1366 x 768 screen at 250 nits, a 41 Wh battery and a weight of 1.41 kg. It ships with Windows 11 Home in S mode, which only runs apps from the Microsoft Store; HP’s page says there is no charge to switch out of S mode and that you cannot switch back, and it notes that the McAfee trial bundled with the machine is not compatible with S mode at all. HP prices this laptop against an RRP of £499.99, a claimed saving of £240.99.

The rungs above it, as HP listed them that day: an HP Laptop 14 with an Intel Core 3 and 512 GB at £379.00 (RRP £599.00); an HP Laptop 15 with a Ryzen 3 at £399.00 (RRP £700.00); the OmniBook 3 14 with a Snapdragon X and an OLED screen at £499.00 (RRP £799.99); the OmniBook 3 16 with a Core 5 and 16 GB at £649.00 (RRP £1,099.00); an HP Laptop 15 with a Core 7, 16 GB and 1 TB at £729.00 (RRP £749.99); the OmniBook 5 16 with a Ryzen 7 and 1 TB at £899.00; the OmniBook X 14 and the OmniBook X Flip, both Core Ultra 5 touchscreens, at £949.00 (RRP £1,299.00). Gaming starts with a Victus 15 carrying an RTX 4050 at £1,400.00, or £1,120.00 with the code BF20. At the top, the ZBook Fury G1i 16 with a Core Ultra 7, 32 GB and an OLED panel was listed at £3,874.79 against a previous £7,303.54, falling to £3,293.57 with BF15.

Desktops run from the OmniStudio 24 all-in-one with a Ryzen 3 at £565.00, or £480.24 with BF15, through OMEN 16L gaming towers at £927.19 to £1,495.20 after codes, to a Z2 workstation with a Core Ultra 9 and 128 GB at £5,039.99. The Black Friday pricing runs to 11:59pm on Wednesday 2 December 2026 or while stocks last, and the codes stack on top of the displayed price rather than replacing it, so the number on the tile is rarely the number you pay.

Two things on the listings are worth a second look before you order. On the OmniBook 3 shelf, a 16-inch model with a Snapdragon C1 is titled “4GB, 256GB” at £299.00 while the specification line beneath it reads 128 GB UFS — HP’s own tile disagreeing with itself, so confirm the storage on the product page. And on the same shelf a 16-inch OmniBook 3 with a Ryzen 5 was priced at £949.00 while the same chassis with the faster Ryzen AI 7 sat at £779.00; HP does not explain the gap, and nothing obliges it to, but it is the kind of thing a ninety-second comparison of the tiles saves you £170 on.

One, one, nought: the warranty code on every product page

Every consumer laptop page in HP’s UK store carries the same line under Manufacturer Warranty: “1 year (1/1/0) limited warranty includes 1 year of parts and labor. No on-site repair.” The three digits are HP’s shorthand for years of parts cover, years of labour cover and years of on-site service, and for the home range the last one is zero: a faulty machine goes back to HP rather than an engineer coming to you. HP’s own guide to checking warranty status says the period starts on the date of purchase or installation and that your dated receipt is the proof, so a laptop bought in a sale and unboxed at Christmas has lost the weeks in between.

HP’s limited warranty statement adds the detail the product page leaves out. The battery is warranted for one year against defects in materials and workmanship — not against the ordinary loss of capacity HP’s own specification pages warn about with the words “actual battery Watt-hours will vary from design capacity”. Replacement parts are warranted for 90 days or the remainder of the original period, whichever is longer. Software is supplied “as is”, with a 90-day replacement right on faulty media only. HP may repair with “new or previously used products or parts equivalent to new in performance and reliability”, and the warranty excludes damage from “accident, misuse, abuse, contamination” — which is why the store’s small print says accidental damage “requires an optional HP Accidental Damage Protection Care Pack”.

The Care Pack is the lever. On the £259 laptop, the £649 OmniBook 5 and the £729 HP Laptop 15 alike, the upsell on the product page was HP 3 years Pickup and Return Notebook Service at £23.77 including VAT: HP collects, repairs and returns the machine with shipping included, taking cover from one year to three for less than a tenth of the cheapest laptop’s price. The business range is priced differently — the EliteBook 6 G2a at £2,145.44 was offered a 3-year onsite Care Pack at £130.80 — and HP’s UK Care Pack terms say a pack must be bought “at time of sale of the supported HP product, or within 90 days of such purchase”, so the decision cannot be left for the first fault. The same terms, HP Care Pack Support Terms (United Kingdom), reference 4AA5-7123EEGB, February 2019, Rev. 3, give a consumer a full refund if a pack is cancelled within 30 days and no service has been used, a 14-day withdrawal right on a distance purchase, and 45 days on a multi-year plan, and they are governed by the law of England and Wales.

What they do not do is replace the law. HP’s own terms say the Limited Warranty sits alongside statutory rights under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, “which expire six years from delivery in England or Wales and five years in Scotland”. That sentence is HP’s, and it matters more than the 1/1/0 code: a laptop that fails in month fourteen is out of HP’s warranty and still inside the period in which a consumer can ask the seller to put right goods that were not of satisfactory quality or durable. On a direct sale the seller and the manufacturer are the same company, which removes the usual argument about who is responsible.

An RRP the shop sets for itself

HP’s Black Friday tiles lean on one word. The £259 laptop is “Saving on RRP £499.99”; the £379 Core 3 machine is set against £599.00; the £649 OmniBook 3 against £1,099.00, a claimed cut of 41%; the £949 OmniBook X against £1,299.00. The recommended retail price is a figure the manufacturer chooses, and on a manufacturer’s own store the recommender and the retailer are one company, so the question a shopper cannot answer from the tile is whether HP ever sold the machine at the higher figure, and for how long.

The guidance that governs the claim is the Chartered Trading Standards Institute’s Guidance for Traders on Pricing Practices, first produced in 2018 at the request of the business department and “reviewed and updated by CTSI in April 2026; previous versions are now withdrawn”. It says an RRP “must not be false; it must not be created purely in order to present the appearance of a discount” and “must represent a genuine selling price”, citing the advertising code’s view that a comparison is likely to mislead if the RRP “differs significantly from the price” at which the product is generally sold. On was-and-now prices it asks how long the product was on sale at the higher price compared with the period of the comparison, and says a claim is more likely to comply where that period is “the same or shorter than the period during which the higher price was offered”. Behind it sits section 226 of the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, under which the Competition and Markets Authority’s guidance CMA207, updated 18 November 2025, treats misleading information about “the existence of a specific price advantage” as an unfair commercial practice if it would change a buying decision.

None of that says HP’s RRPs are false, and this page does not. It says the proof is not on the page. The ZBook Fury tile shows the arithmetic at its most striking — a listed £7,303.54 cut to £3,874.79 before the BF15 code takes it to £3,293.57, a 47% reduction on the first pair alone — and gives no date for when the first figure was last charged. HP’s price guarantee, “if your item drops in price within 30 days, we’ll refund the difference”, is a genuine protection against the shop undercutting itself a week after you buy, but its terms page would not load without scripting on 14 November 2026, and an HP community thread about the guarantee shows a customer asking in writing for the £100 difference on a laptop that fell in price two days after delivery and an HP employee replying only to request a case number. Take a dated screenshot of the price when you order.

Three ways to pay monthly, with three different companies behind them

The store’s own promise is “0% interest for 36 months on orders over £599”, and the product pages do the sum for you: the £729 HP Laptop 15 is £20.25 a month, the £649 OmniBook 5 is £18.03, the £2,145.44 EliteBook 6 G2a is £59.60, and the ZBook Fury is £107.63. The £259 laptop shows no monthly figure because it falls under the threshold. Alongside sits PayPal Credit, for which the footer prints the representative example the rules require — an assumed credit limit of £1,200 at a purchase rate of 23.9% a year variable, 23.9% APR representative — and PayPal Pay in 3, with the required warning that it “may make other borrowing more difficult or expensive”. Credit is subject to status and open to UK residents aged 18 or over.

The third route is not credit at all but hire. HP introduces customers to Raylo, a trading name of Raylo Group Limited, FCA firm reference 841488, for a subscription that starts at £0 upfront: on 14 November 2026 Raylo’s HP page quoted an OmniBook 3 from £14.79 a month excluding VAT on a 12-month term, a ProBook 4 G2iS from £16.66, an EliteBook 8 G2i from £33.29 and an EliteBook X Flip from £48.95, with a monthly rolling option and lower payments on longer terms. The subscription includes HP Care Pack cover for its length, free next-day DPD delivery and 14-day free returns; at the end you can apply for a free upgrade, keep paying, or send the machine back, and if you cannot return it Raylo charges what it calls “a fair fee”. The same page states that HP Inc UK Limited acts only as a credit intermediary under firm reference 973346 and is not the lender. The Financial Conduct Authority’s register would not open on the day of writing, so that reference is reported as Raylo prints it.

Which is cheaper depends on how long you keep the machine. Twelve months of the OmniBook 3 at £14.79 excluding VAT is £177.48 before VAT for a laptop the store sells outright at £499.00 including it, so the hire costs roughly 36% of the purchase price for a year of use and no laptop at the end; the 0% route on a dearer machine costs nothing above the cash price provided every one of the 36 payments is made. The Raylo agreement is a hire contract, not a hire purchase, and ownership never passes.

Fourteen days, and a withdrawal form with two addresses

Because the shop sells at a distance, the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 give a consumer 14 days to cancel, starting the day after the goods come into their physical possession, and the trader must refund within 14 days of being told. The consumer pays the cost of sending the goods back only if the trader said so before the sale; a trader may deduct for handling “beyond what is necessary to establish their nature, characteristics and function”, and cannot make that deduction at all if it failed to explain the right to cancel. Sealed software loses the right once unsealed. A built-to-order configuration does not count as bespoke merely because stock options were combined to a customer’s choice.

HP’s mechanism for this is a withdrawal form, Withdrawal_form_UK.pdf, which asks you to “complete and return this form only if you wish to withdraw from the contract” and tells you not to send the goods to the address on it — HP Store Postsales issues return or collection instructions once the request is processed. The form carries two companies and two email addresses: Hewlett-Packard Limited at Cain Road, Bracknell, with ukgla.hpstore@hp.com, and HP Inc UK Limited at Earley West, Reading, with hpstore-uk@hp.com, and it does not say which applies to a given order. Companies House shows both still active — Hewlett-Packard Limited, number 00690597, was incorporated on 24 April 1961 and now gives its registered office as Wharfedale Road, Winnersh — while HP’s own company registration page names only HP Inc UK Limited. The consumer phone line on the form is 0207 660 3859. Check the order confirmation for the contracting company before you write.

Beyond the statutory 14 days the store’s own returns policy, its consumer terms and conditions of sale and its price guarantee terms all sit on pages that returned nothing but a notice that scripting was disabled when read on 14 November 2026, so this page cannot tell you whether HP offers a longer goodwill window on consumer laptops, who pays for the return courier, or how a restocking charge, if any, is calculated. One business machine, the EliteBook 6 G1q at £1,456.55 after code, carried the badge “Get a 100-day extended return — or your money back”; no consumer laptop checked carried it. The withdrawal right does not depend on any of this, but the goodwill terms might, and the honest position is that they were not readable.

Where the UK pages were written somewhere else

HP’s store is a global template, and the seams show in places that cost a reader money or time. The product pages offer HP SmartFriend Support, 1 Year at £40.00 including VAT as a tick-box add-on; HP’s own UK SmartFriend overview page prices the same one-year prepaid plan at £89, or £9.99 a month on a rolling subscription, with a full refund on cancellation within 30 days. Both are HP pages, both were live on 14 November 2026, and they are £49 apart on one year of 24-hour phone and remote support for a single computer of any brand. This page prints both and takes neither as the price.

HP’s UK Care Pack buying guide, dated 11 December 2022 under a contributing writer’s byline, tells British readers that laptop Care Packs start “at rates starting below $40”, desktop packs “around $150” and printer packs “below $20” — dollar figures on a gb-en page whose own store sells the laptop pack for £23.77. The UK warranty-check article says Care Packs are refundable if unused “with a rebate issued by mail”, which is an American mechanism; HP’s UK terms give a refund from the place of purchase. And the specification table on both the £259 laptop and the £649 OmniBook 5 reads “Power supply type 65 W AC power adapter (recommended, sold separately)” — 45 W on the smaller machine — while the fast-charge footnote on the same pages assumes you are “using HP adapter provided with the notebook”. The two statements cannot both describe what is in the box. HP almost certainly ships a charger; the point is that the page a shopper reads before paying does not say so without contradiction, and a reader who wants certainty should ask HP in writing before ordering.

One regulatory quirk belongs here too. The Product Security and Telecommunications Infrastructure regime that has applied to connected consumer products since 29 April 2024 — banning default passwords, requiring a route to report vulnerabilities and a published minimum period of security updates — carves out “desktop computers, laptop computers and tablet computers which do not have the capability to connect to cellular networks”. An HP laptop with Wi-Fi only is therefore outside it, and HP is under no UK duty to state how long it will patch the machine’s firmware; the support window a buyer can actually rely on is Microsoft’s for Windows 11, not HP’s. An HP Smart Tank printer on the same checkout is inside the regime.

The battery programme HP announced in 2018 and has not closed

HP’s safety record on laptops has one large entry and it is HP’s own document. The HP Notebook Computer and Mobile Workstation Battery Safety Recall and Replacement Program was announced in January 2018, expanded in January 2019 and expanded again in August 2019, and its page at batteryprogram687.ext.hp.com was still live on 14 November 2026. It covers ProBook 640, 645, 650 and 655 G2 and G3, ProBook 430 to 470 G4, x360 310 G2, ENVY m6, Pavilion x360 15, HP 11 notebooks, certain Mobile Thin Clients and ZBook 17 G3 and G4 and Studio G3 and G4 workstations “sold worldwide from December 2015 through October 2018”, plus batteries sold as accessories to December 2018.

HP publishes no count of affected machines on the programme page. The United States regulator does: the Consumer Product Safety Commission’s notice of 12 March 2019, recall 19-079, put the expansion at about 78,500 units on top of 50,000 recalled in January 2018, after “eight new reports of battery packs in the U.S. overheating, melting, or charring”, one minor injury and two reports of property damage totalling $1,100. No UK figure has been published by HP or by the Office for Product Safety and Standards, and the UK recalls register searched for “HP notebook” on 14 November 2026 returned five entries, none of them an HP computer.

Two features of the programme are worth knowing even if your machine is years newer. The batteries were internal — “not customer-replaceable”, in the regulator’s words — so the remedy was a free replacement by an authorised technician rather than a part in the post. And HP’s interim fix was a BIOS update that puts an affected battery into Battery Safety Mode, letting the laptop run from the mains with the battery disabled and unable to charge until HP says otherwise. If you are buying a used ProBook or ZBook of that era, run HP’s Battery Validation Utility before you pay. On the current range the only battery fact HP commits to in writing is the one-year defect warranty described above.

The company behind the checkout, and why the shelf is getting dearer

HP Inc UK Limited was incorporated on 27 January 2015, the year Hewlett-Packard split into HP Inc. and Hewlett Packard Enterprise, and files under SIC code 26200, manufacture of computers and peripheral equipment. Its full accounts to 31 October 2025 were filed on 7 June 2026; the next, to 31 October 2026, are due by 31 July 2027. HP Inc. has been its person with significant control since 9 March 2020, replacing HP Inc UK Holding Limited. Under the Reporting on Payment Practices regulations the company reported that between 1 November 2024 and 30 April 2025 it took an average of 41 days to pay its suppliers, with 1% of invoices paid within 30 days, 89% in 31 to 60 days and 10% later — a fact about how the shop treats the people who supply it, not its customers, and printed here because it is on the public record.

The parent’s numbers explain the prices. For the quarter to 31 July 2026 HP Inc. reported net revenue of $15.7 billion, up 12.5%; Personal Systems — the computers — brought in $11.8 billion, up 18%, at a 4.6% operating margin, with consumer revenue up 10% and commercial up 22%. Yet total units shipped fell 16%, consumer units by 19%. More money from fewer machines means a higher average price per computer, and HP’s own risk factors name “increasing memory and storage costs” as a pressure, while the chief executive spoke of “meaningful improvements in memory supply”. A shelf where 8 GB of soldered memory is the entry point is what that looks like from the buyer’s side. Printing, at $3.9 billion and an 18.1% margin, is still where HP makes most of its profit, which is why Instant Ink is promoted beside the laptops on HP’s own home page.

Where to turn when something goes wrong is less well signposted than the finance. HP’s Care Pack terms name no alternative dispute resolution scheme; they refer a dissatisfied consumer to “your local consumer authority or see the Citizens Advice website” and to the courts of England and Wales. There is no ombudsman for computers, and a problem with the credit element goes to the Financial Ombudsman Service against the lender — PayPal or Raylo — rather than against HP. Keep the order confirmation, because it names the contracting company the withdrawal form does not.

Where it wins

  • Free delivery on every order to the UK mainland, typically 1–2 working days for stock items, with no minimum spend
  • A 3-year Pickup and Return Care Pack at £23.77 on consumer laptops triples the standard cover for less than a spare charger
  • 0% interest over 36 months on orders over £599, with the monthly figure printed on each product page
  • A 30-day price guarantee refunding the difference if HP cuts the price after you buy
  • The manufacturer is the seller, so a Consumer Rights Act claim has one company to go to, and HP’s own terms acknowledge the six-year window in England and Wales
  • A deep range — 438 laptops and 248 desktops on one site — from a £259 N150 machine to workstations, all with the same warranty line

Where it falls short

  • The standard warranty is one year with no on-site repair, and the battery is covered against defects only, not against the capacity loss HP’s own pages warn of
  • The RRPs that the Black Friday savings are measured against are set by HP itself, and nothing on the tile shows when or for how long the higher price was charged
  • The returns policy, consumer terms of sale and price guarantee terms would not load without scripting on 14 November 2026, so the goodwill return window and any restocking charge could not be verified
  • HP’s own pages disagree with each other: SmartFriend is £40 on a product page and £89 on HP’s service page, and the specification line says the power adapter is “sold separately” while the footnote says it is provided
  • The withdrawal form names two different HP companies and two email addresses without saying which applies to your order
  • Accidental damage is excluded unless you buy a separate Accidental Damage Protection Care Pack, and no UK price for one could be found on the product pages checked
  • A Care Pack must be bought within 90 days of the hardware, so the decision to extend cover cannot wait for the first fault

Common questions

How long is the warranty on an HP laptop bought from HP’s UK store?

One year. Every consumer laptop page checked on 14 November 2026 carried the line “1 year (1/1/0) limited warranty includes 1 year of parts and labor. No on-site repair.” The three figures are years of parts, labour and on-site cover. The battery is warranted for one year against defects, replacement parts for 90 days. The period runs from the date on your receipt, not from the day you unbox it.

What does an HP Care Pack cost and when do I have to buy it?

On the consumer laptops checked, the product-page upsell was HP 3 years Pickup and Return Notebook Service at £23.77 including VAT. A 3-year onsite pack on a business EliteBook was £130.80. HP’s UK Care Pack terms say a pack must be bought at the time of sale or within 90 days of the hardware purchase, and can be cancelled for a full refund within 30 days if no service has been used.

Can I return a laptop to HP if I change my mind?

Yes, within 14 days of delivery under the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013, with the refund due within 14 days of HP being told. HP provides a withdrawal form and asks you not to post the goods until HP Store Postsales sends instructions. Whether HP offers a longer goodwill window on consumer laptops could not be read on 14 November 2026 because the store’s returns page needs scripting to display; one business laptop carried a 100-day extended return badge.

Is the 0% finance really interest-free?

HP advertises 0% interest over 36 months on orders over £599, and the product pages show the cash price divided by 36 — £729 becomes £20.25 a month. The alternative PayPal Credit carries a representative 23.9% APR variable, and Raylo offers hire from £14.79 a month excluding VAT with no ownership at the end. Credit is subject to status and HP Inc UK Limited acts as a credit intermediary, not the lender.

Are HP’s Black Friday savings against the RRP genuine?

This page cannot say, and that is the point. HP sets the RRP and sells the machine, so the £499.99 against which the £259 laptop is marked is HP’s own figure. The CTSI pricing guidance updated in April 2026 says an RRP must represent a genuine selling price and that a comparison is likely to mislead if the RRP differs significantly from the price generally charged. Judge the laptop on its cash price and specification.

Does the HP battery recall affect current laptops?

No. HP’s Battery Safety Recall and Replacement Program covers specific ProBook, ZBook, x360, ENVY, Pavilion and HP 11 models sold between December 2015 and October 2018, expanded in January and August 2019. The programme page is still live, so if you are buying a second-hand machine of that era, run HP’s Battery Validation Utility first. Current machines carry HP’s standard one-year battery defect warranty.

Our verdict

HP’s UK store is the simplest way to buy an HP: the whole range, free delivery in a day or two, a one-year warranty that a £23.77 Care Pack turns into three, and 0% over 36 months above £599. Buy on the specification and the cash price, not the RRP, add the pickup-and-return pack at checkout if you want cover past year one, and keep the order confirmation, because it is the only document that tells you which HP company you bought from.
