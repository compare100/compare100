HSBC Credit Cards Review 2026: Seven Cards, 18.9% to 100.2% APR

Rates and terms checked 4 October 2026 · Credit Cards · Compare100 editorial team

HSBC's Balance Transfer Credit Card advertises up to 36 months at 0% on a transferred balance for a 3.09% fee, which is £92.70 on a £3,000 debt. Those figures were taken on 4 October 2026 from HSBC's own product pages, and the 36-month window is at the long end of what any UK bank is publishing. There is no annual fee on it, and the minimum income is £6,800 of UK taxable income or pension before tax.

The wider range is seven cards and the rates run a long way: 18.9% on the student card, 24.9% on Purchase Plus, 25.9% on the balance transfer card, 26.9% on Rewards, 29.9% on both Classic and Premier, and 100.2% on the £290-a-year Premier World Elite Mastercard. Five of the seven carry no annual fee. Purchase Plus is the other side of the same coin as the transfer card: up to 24 months at 0% on spending, plus up to 17 months at 0% on transfers at a 3.49% fee. HSBC UK Bank plc is the ring-fenced bank that holds all of it, with 14.5 million retail and wealth customers and more than 23,000 staff.

One rule decides whether any of the 0% offers can help a given reader, and it is worth knowing before applying rather than after. A balance cannot be moved onto an HSBC card from another card issued inside the same group: HSBC's own second-card page states that "You can't transfer balances between two HSBC credit cards, including First Direct or M&S Bank." Transfers also have to be made within 60 days of the account being opened. An HSBC, first direct or M&S Bank balance is therefore the one debt this card cannot rescue, and HSBC counts over 15 million active UK customers.

Seven cards, and a rate ladder that runs from 18.9% to 100.2%

HSBC publishes seven personal credit cards and prices them for seven different jobs rather than selling one card with options. The cheapest borrowing rate on the list belongs to the Student Credit Card at 18.9% a year variable, available only to people who already hold an HSBC Student Bank Account, with a limit of up to £500. Its representative example is the only one in the range built on an assumed credit limit of £250 rather than £1,200, which is a more honest basis than most student cards are advertised on.

Above it sit the two promotional cards. Purchase Plus charges 24.9% variable once its offers run out and gives up to 24 months at 0% on purchases and up to 17 months at 0% on transfers made within 60 days, with a 3.49% transfer fee and a £5 minimum. The Balance Transfer Credit Card charges 25.9% variable, runs the 0% transfer window out to 36 months at a lower 3.09% fee, and gives only three months at 0% on purchases. Neither charges an annual fee and both ask for £6,800 of income.

The Rewards Credit Card is 26.9% variable with 2,500 points, which HSBC itself prices at £25, paid on the first transaction, six months at 0% on purchases and six months at 0% on transfers at a 2.99% fee. Classic is the plain card at 29.9% variable with no promotional period at all, and a minimum credit limit of £250. The Premier Credit Card matches Classic's 29.9% and is restricted to Premier customers.

At the top is the Premier World Elite Mastercard: a 29.9% purchase rate, a £290 annual fee, £60 a year for each additional cardholder, and a representative APR of 100.2% variable on the same assumed £1,200 limit. It earns 3 points per £1 on sterling spending and 4 points per £1 on non-sterling spending, against a 2.99% non-sterling transaction fee, and carries Priority Pass membership covering more than 1,700 lounges. Getting it means qualifying for a Premier account, which needs £100,000 of annual income paid in or £100,000 of savings and investments held with HSBC UK. A welcome offer of 70,000 points for £2,000 of spending inside 90 days was running when this page was checked and is advertised as ending on 1 December 2026.

What three years of nothing actually costs

The arithmetic on the headline product is short enough to do in one line. A 3.09% fee on a £3,000 transfer is £92.70. Spread across 36 months that is £2.58 a month, or an effective 1.03% a year for the use of the money.

The number to set it against is not HSBC's own 25.9% go-to rate but the market rate a reader is probably already paying. The Bank of England's Money and Credit release for August 2026, published on 29 September 2026, puts the effective rate on interest-charging credit cards at 21.55%, up from 21.45% the month before. A year of interest on £3,000 at that rate is about £646, so HSBC's one-off fee is worth roughly one-seventh of a single year's interest at the going rate, and the card then buys three years of silence. On that comparison the fee is cheap, and saying so is simply reading the two numbers next to each other.

The same release explains why the product exists at all. Net credit card borrowing rose to £1.2 billion in August 2026 from £0.9 billion in July, within £2.5 billion of total net consumer credit, and credit card balances were growing at 13.3% a year against 12.6% a month earlier. Card borrowing is growing faster than consumer credit as a whole, which was running at 9.6%. A bank with 14.5 million retail customers competing for that flow has every reason to buy balances off its rivals with 36 months of free credit, and the fee is what pays for it.

Sixty days, two words, and one group exclusion

Three conditions sit between the advertised 36 months and the 36 months a particular applicant gets, and all three are on HSBC's own pages.

The first is the 60-day window. A transfer qualifies for the 0% rate only if it is made within 60 days of the account being opened, and the clock runs from opening rather than from approval or from the card arriving. The promotional period on the balance transfer card then runs from the date of each transfer, not from the date of the account, so a reader who waits eight weeks still gets the full term but a reader who waits nine weeks gets nothing.

The second is the phrase "up to". HSBC advertises up to 36 months on transfers and up to 24 months on Purchase Plus spending, and the balance transfer card's own summary box describes a shorter term than the product page does. Both the promotional length and the go-to rate are set on application, and the summary box prices purchases and transfers as a band of 21.9% to 24.9% a year rather than a single figure, which means the rate quoted in the representative example is the rate offered to a representative applicant and not a promise to anybody.

The third is the group exclusion already stated above, and it has a second edge to it. Holding two HSBC cards is allowed — the maximum is two — but a balance cannot be shuffled between them, and no new card or credit limit increase is available for six months after one is opened, in either direction. A reader who takes Purchase Plus for its 24 months at 0% on spending has therefore also used up their six months and one of their two card slots, which matters if the balance transfer card was the real target.

The bank behind the card, in the regulator's words

The lender is HSBC UK Bank plc, authorised by the Prudential Regulation Authority and regulated by both the Financial Conduct Authority and the PRA under Financial Services Register number 765112. Companies House records it as company 09928412, incorporated on 23 December 2015 and active, a public limited company registered under SIC code 64191 for banks, with its registered office at 1 Centenary Square, Birmingham B1 1HQ.

That 2015 incorporation date is worth a sentence, because the bank is younger than the brand on the card. HSBC UK Bank plc was created as the ring-fenced UK retail bank required by post-crisis banking reform, which is why the registered office is in Birmingham rather than at the group's Canary Wharf headquarters. For a cardholder the practical effect is that the entity carrying the lending is a separate legally ring-fenced company from the international business, and the Financial Services Compensation Scheme limit applies at that entity across HSBC UK, first direct and M&S Bank together rather than once per brand.

The scale is HSBC's own published figure: 14.5 million retail and wealth customers, 600,000 business clients, over 15 million active customers in total and more than 23,000 colleagues. Credit cards are a small line inside that, and HSBC does not publish how many cards it has in issue or what share of its card balances carry interest, which is a gap other large issuers also leave.

The charges the headline rate does not cover

Interest on spending is the advertised number; the rest of the pricing sits in the summary boxes. Cash is the expensive corner of every card in the range. On the balance transfer card a cash advance is charged at 27.9% a year, 2.075% a month, with a fee of 2.99% of the amount and a £3 minimum. On Classic it is 35.9% a year, 2.592% a month, on the same 2.99% fee. Interest on a cash advance runs from the day it is taken, so the interest-free period on purchases does not apply to it.

The default charges are £12 for a late payment, £12 for going over the credit limit and £5 for a returned payment, with reasonable costs recoverable on enforcement. Spending abroad costs 2.99% of the transaction converted into sterling. Classic's summary box sets the interest-free period on purchases at a maximum of 56 days, and only where the transaction balance is paid in full and on time.

The minimum payment is the highest of three calculations: all interest added since the last statement plus any default charges plus 1% of the remaining balance; or 2.5% of the full amount owed; or £5. The first of those three is the one worth understanding, because a minimum payment built as interest plus 1% clears the principal at 1% a month and nothing faster. Payments are allocated to overdue minimum payments first, then that month's minimum, then any monthly instalment, then the rest of the transaction balance — which is the statutory order and works in the cardholder's favour, since the most expensive debt is cleared before the cheapest.

Where HSBC's own documents stop agreeing with each other

The summary box is the document a regulated lender points an applicant at before they borrow, and on the balance transfer card HSBC's does not match the page selling the product. The PDF published at hsbc.co.uk under the file name summary-box-balance-transfer carries the reference DCP140 and a copyright line reading ©HSBC Group 2021, and it states a representative APR of 21.9%, a 0% transfer period of 29 months from the date of transfer, and a transfer fee of 2.75% with a £5 minimum. The live product page, read the same day, states 25.9%, up to 36 months, and 3.09%. Every one of the three headline terms differs, in both directions: the term is longer than the summary box says and both the rate and the fee are higher.

The most likely explanation is a document that has not been refreshed since 2021 rather than a card being sold on two sets of terms, and HSBC's Classic summary box does match its product page at 29.9%, which supports that reading. It still leaves the applicant for the one product with a promotional offer worth having reading a five-year-old price list. Which set of figures governs an agreement signed today needs confirming with HSBC before anybody relies on either.

There is a smaller error on the same product. HSBC's balance transfer landing page gives a worked example reading "if you have a 3.09% transfer fee (min £5), then transferring a balance of £3000 to an HSBC balance transfer card would cost you £95.70." A 3.09% fee on £3,000 is £92.70. The figure quoted is £3 high and corresponds to a rate of 3.19%, so it looks like either a transposed digit or a fee that has moved since the example was written. It is £3 on a three-year loan and nobody is being harmed by it, but it is the kind of thing worth checking on your own statement rather than taking from the advert.

Complaints, and the rule that caps how long a balance can sit

HSBC publishes its own complaints return, which is more than many issuers volunteer, and the figures for the six months from 1 January to 30 June 2026 are these: 83,623 complaints opened across HSBC UK Bank plc, of which 73,367 were in the banking and credit cards category, at a rate of 2.26 complaints per 1,000 accounts. HSBC closed 60.1% of them within three business days and a further 39.3% within eight weeks. It upheld 58.8%, and recorded general administration and customer service as the main cause across every category.

Two of those numbers point in opposite directions and both are fair. A rate of 2.26 per 1,000 accounts is low, and closing three in five cases inside three days is fast work. An uphold rate of 58.8% means nearly three in five complainants turned out to be right, which is the number a prospective cardholder should weigh rather than the headline volume.

Set against that is a protection the reader gets whichever card they take. Under the FCA's persistent debt rules, in force from 1 March 2018 with firms required to comply by 1 September 2018, a lender has to act when a customer pays more in interest and charges than they repay of the balance over an extended period. The FCA found around 4 million accounts in that position, paying roughly £2.50 in interest and charges for every £1 of balance repaid. At 18 months the firm must make contact and prompt a change in repayments, warning that the card may be suspended; at 36 months it must offer a way to clear the balance in a reasonable period and, where the customer cannot, show forbearance that may include reducing, waiving or cancelling interest, fees or charges. The FCA estimated the package would save consumers between £310 million and £1.3 billion a year, and that around 1.4 million accounts a year would stop receiving automatic credit limit increases. A 36-month 0% transfer and a 36-month persistent-debt intervention are the same length by coincidence, but a reader who reaches the end of one is close to the threshold for the other.

Rewards that HSBC prices on one card and not the other

HSBC prints enough to value the Rewards Credit Card's points and not enough to value the Premier World Elite's. On the Rewards card the earn rate given on HSBC's rewards pages is 1 point for every £5 spent, and the welcome bonus is described as 2,500 points, worth £25. One point is therefore a penny, and 1 point per £5 of spending is a return of 0.2%. The anniversary bonus of 2,500 points for spending £10,000 or more in a year, available for the first five years, adds £25 on £10,000, which is another 0.25%. Points expire three years after they are earned.

The Premier World Elite earns 3 points per £1 on sterling spending, fifteen times the Rewards card's rate per pound. At the Rewards card's published penny a point that would be a 3% return and would clear the £290 annual fee at about £9,700 of annual spending. No UK card pays 3% on general spending, so the two cards' points are plainly not worth the same amount, and HSBC does not publish a redemption rate for the World Elite's points on its own site. Everything else returned by a search for that figure is a points blog rather than a primary source, so the break-even spend on the £290 fee cannot be calculated from the issuer's own material and is left as a question for HSBC.

On independent ratings there is a plain answer and it is a negative one. Defaqto rates insurance rather than consumer credit, and no ratings agency publishes a star rating or score for an HSBC credit card that could be quoted here. The FCA complaints return above and HSBC's own summary boxes are the only dated, primary numbers available on this product, which is a reason to read them closely rather than a reason to substitute a score from somewhere else.

Where it wins

  • Up to 36 months at 0% on balance transfers for a 3.09% fee — £92.70 on £3,000, or 1.03% a year spread over the term
  • Five of the seven cards carry no annual fee, and the minimum income on the three mainstream cards is £6,800
  • Purchase Plus covers the other need at up to 24 months at 0% on spending plus up to 17 months at 0% on transfers
  • A student card at 18.9% variable, advertised on an assumed £250 limit rather than a flattering £1,200 one
  • Payments are allocated to the most expensive balance first, and the £12 late and over-limit fees are at the market standard rather than above it
  • HSBC publishes its own complaints return twice a year, closing 60.1% of banking and credit card cases within three business days
  • The lender is a ring-fenced UK bank, FCA and PRA regulated under reference 765112, with 14.5 million retail and wealth customers behind it

Where it falls short

  • A balance cannot be transferred from another card inside the group — HSBC's own page names First Direct and M&S Bank — so the 36-month offer is closed to the one debt HSBC's 15 million existing customers are most likely to be carrying
  • The balance transfer summary box, the document an applicant is pointed to before borrowing, carries the reference DCP140 and a ©HSBC Group 2021 copyright line and states 21.9% APR, 29 months and a 2.75% fee against the live page's 25.9%, 36 months and 3.09% — all three headline terms disagree
  • HSBC's own worked example says a 3.09% fee on a £3,000 transfer "would cost you £95.70"; the correct figure is £92.70, and £95.70 is 3.19%
  • Transfers must be made within 60 days of the account opening, and both the promotional length and the go-to rate are "up to" figures set on application — the summary box prices the rate as a band of 21.9% to 24.9%
  • Representative examples assume a £1,200 credit limit while the minimum limits are £500 on the balance transfer card and £250 on Classic, so an applicant approved at the floor pays more in percentage terms than the advertised APR implies
  • Cash is punitive and the pricing is easy to miss: 27.9% a year on the balance transfer card and 35.9% on Classic, plus a 2.99% fee with a £3 minimum, with interest running from the day of withdrawal

Common questions

How long is HSBC's 0% balance transfer offer, and what does it cost?

Up to 36 months at 0% on the Balance Transfer Credit Card, for a fee of 3.09% of each transfer with a £5 minimum. On a £3,000 balance that fee is £92.70, which is £2.58 a month across the 36 months, or an effective 1.03% a year. Transfers must be made within 60 days of the account being opened, and the 0% period runs from the date of each transfer. The go-to rate afterwards is 25.9% a year variable, and the card gives only three months at 0% on purchases, so it is a card for clearing a balance rather than for spending on.

Can I move a first direct or M&S Bank balance to an HSBC card?

No. HSBC's second-card page states that you cannot transfer balances between two HSBC credit cards, including First Direct or M&S Bank, because all three brands sit inside HSBC UK Bank plc. The same applies to moving a balance between two HSBC-branded cards you hold yourself. If the debt you want to shift is already with one of those three, the 0% offer cannot take it, and this is the single most common reason an application of this kind is of no use to the applicant.

Why is the Premier World Elite's representative APR 100.2% when the purchase rate is 29.9%?

Because a representative APR has to include compulsory charges, and the card's £290 annual fee is one. The APR calculation spreads that fee across the assumed £1,200 credit limit, which is a large proportion of a small balance, so the figure lands at 100.2% variable on a 29.9% purchase rate. Nobody pays 100.2% in interest. The figure is a warning that the fee only makes sense on high spending, and the amount of spending needed to justify it cannot be calculated from HSBC's own pages because it does not publish what a World Elite point is worth.

How many HSBC credit cards can I hold at once?

Two. The second has to be a different card from the first, and the pairings are set: holders of Rewards, Premier or Premier World Elite can add Purchase Plus, Balance Transfer or Rewards, while holders of Balance Transfer, Purchase Plus or Classic can add Rewards, Premier or Premier World Elite. Each card has its own credit limit, limits cannot be moved between them, and you cannot apply for a new card or a limit increase within six months of opening one.

What does HSBC charge if a payment is late or the limit is exceeded?

£12 for a late payment, £12 for going over the credit limit and £5 for a returned payment, with reasonable enforcement costs recoverable on top. The minimum payment itself is the highest of three figures: interest since the last statement plus default charges plus 1% of the remaining balance; 2.5% of the full amount owed; or £5. Paying only the minimum on the first of those formulas clears the principal at about 1% a month, which is why the FCA's persistent debt rules require a lender to intervene at 18 months and again at 36.

Our verdict

Judged on the one thing a balance transfer card is bought for, HSBC's is at the front of the market: up to 36 months at 0% for a 3.09% fee is £92.70 on £3,000, about a seventh of what a year's interest at the Bank of England's 21.55% effective rate would cost, and it buys three years rather than one. Purchase Plus covers the other half of the job at up to 24 months on spending, and five of the seven cards charge nothing to hold. The things to check before applying are all HSBC's own small print rather than anything hidden: the 60-day transfer window, the "up to" on both the term and the rate, the £500 and £250 minimum limits behind the £1,200 assumed in the adverts, and the group rule that stops an HSBC, first direct or M&S Bank balance moving across at all. That last one disqualifies a large share of the people most likely to be reading, so it is the first thing to establish. The 2021 summary box and the £3 error in HSBC's own worked example are both worth raising with the bank; neither changes the conclusion that on price this is a competitive card, but both are reasons to read your own agreement rather than the advert.
