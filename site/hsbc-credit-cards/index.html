HSBC Credit Cards Review 2026: Seven Cards, 18.9% to 100.2% APR

Rates and terms checked 12 October 2026 · Credit Cards · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

HSBC's Balance Transfer Credit Card advertises up to 36 months at 0% on a transferred balance, for a fee HSBC's own pages put at either 3.09% or 3.19% — £92.70 or £95.70 on a £3,000 debt. Those figures were read on 12 October 2026 from HSBC's product, comparison and offer pages, and 36 months is at the long end of what any UK bank is publishing. There is no annual fee, and the entry requirement is £6,800 of annual UK taxable income or pension before tax.

The wider range is seven cards and the rates run a long way: 18.9% on the student card, 24.9% on Purchase Plus, 25.9% on the balance transfer card, 26.9% on Rewards, 29.9% on both Classic and Premier, and 100.2% on the £290-a-year Premier World Elite Mastercard. Six of the seven carry no annual fee, the World Elite being the only one that does. Purchase Plus is the other side of the same coin as the transfer card: up to 24 months at 0% on spending for new HSBC credit card customers, plus up to 17 months at 0% on transfers at a 3.49% fee. HSBC UK Bank plc is the ring-fenced bank that holds all of it, with 14.5 million retail and wealth customers and more than 23,000 colleagues.

One rule decides whether any of the 0% offers can help a given reader, and it is worth knowing before applying rather than after. A balance cannot be moved onto an HSBC card from another card issued inside the same group. HSBC's second-card page puts it twice, once as a note and once as an answer: “you can't transfer your balance between HSBC, First Direct or M&S Bank cards” and “No. You can't transfer balances between two HSBC credit cards, including First Direct or M&S Bank.” Transfers also have to be made within 60 days of the account being opened. An HSBC, first direct or M&S Bank balance is therefore the one debt this card cannot rescue, and HSBC counts over 15 million active UK customers.

Seven cards, and a rate ladder that runs from 18.9% to 100.2%

HSBC publishes seven personal credit cards and prices them for seven different jobs rather than selling one card with options. The cheapest borrowing rate belongs to the Student Credit Card at 18.9% a year variable, open only to people who already hold an HSBC Student Bank Account, with a credit limit between £250 and £500 and a cash rate of 23.6% a year, which is the lowest cash rate in the range by a wide margin.

Above it sit the two promotional cards. Purchase Plus charges 24.9% variable once its offers run out and gives up to 24 months at 0% on purchases — HSBC's own comparison page adds “For new HSBC credit card customers only” to that offer, which is a condition the product page leaves out — plus up to 17 months at 0% on transfers made within 60 days, at a 3.49% fee with a £5 minimum. The Balance Transfer Credit Card charges 25.9% variable, runs the 0% transfer window out to 36 months at a lower fee, and gives only three months at 0% on purchases. Neither charges an annual fee and both ask for £6,800 of income.

The Rewards Credit Card is 26.9% variable and earns more than its headline suggests: HSBC advertises “up to 10 points for every £1 you spend” at selected retailers through Mastercard Pay with Rewards, which has to be registered for before the purchase is made, and 1 point for every £5 spent anywhere else. It pays 2,500 points as a welcome bonus on the first transaction, another 2,500 for £10,000 of spending in each of the first five years, six months at 0% on purchases and six months at 0% on transfers at a 2.99% fee. Classic is the plain card at 29.9% variable with no promotional period at all, a minimum credit limit of £250 and a maximum of £1,000.

The Premier Credit Card is the one the previous version of this page undersold. It matches Classic's 29.9% and is restricted to Premier customers, but it charges no annual fee and still earns 1.5 reward points per £1 of sterling spending and 2 points per £1 abroad, pays 30,000 points for £2,000 of spending in the first 90 days on an offer advertised as ending 1 December 2026, carries Priority Pass access to more than 1,700 lounges at £24 per person per visit, and advertises up to 5% cashback on selected Apple products.

At the top is the Premier World Elite Mastercard: a 29.9% purchase rate, a £290 annual fee, £60 a year for each additional cardholder, and a representative APR of 100.2% variable on an assumed £1,200 limit. It earns 3 points per £1 on sterling spending and 4 points per £1 on non-sterling spending against a 2.99% non-sterling fee, pays 70,000 points for the same £2,000 of spending in 90 days on the same 1 December 2026 deadline, and makes the same 1,700 lounges free rather than £24 a visit. Getting it means qualifying for Premier, which needs £100,000 of annual income paid into the account or £100,000 of savings and investments held with HSBC UK.

What three years of nothing actually costs

The arithmetic on the headline product is short enough to do in one line, and it has to be done twice because HSBC publishes two fees for it. At 3.09% a £3,000 transfer costs £92.70, which is £2.58 a month across 36 months, or an effective 1.03% a year for the use of the money. At the 3.19% HSBC quotes on its comparison and offer pages it is £95.70, £2.66 a month, 1.06% a year. The gap between the two is £3 on a three-year loan.

The number to set either against is not HSBC's own 25.9% go-to rate but the market rate a reader is probably already paying. The Bank of England's Money and Credit release for August 2026, published on 29 September 2026 and still the current one until the September figures appear on 29 October 2026, puts the effective rate on interest-charging credit cards at 21.55%, up from 21.45% the month before. A year of interest on £3,000 at that rate is about £646, so HSBC's one-off fee is worth roughly one-seventh of a single year's interest at the going rate, and the card then buys three years of silence. On that comparison the fee is cheap, and saying so is simply reading the two numbers next to each other.

The sum that matters more is what happens if the 36 months turns out to be 14, which is the shorter offer HSBC publishes on the same page. The fee does not shrink with the term. £92.70 spread over 14 months is £6.62 a month rather than £2.58 — two and a half times the cost per interest-free month for the same money. A reader comparing cards on the fee alone is comparing the wrong number; the figure that decides the value is the fee divided by the months actually granted.

The same Bank of England release explains why the product exists at all. Net credit card borrowing rose to £1.2 billion in August 2026 from £0.9 billion in July, within £2.5 billion of total net consumer credit, and credit card balances were growing at 13.3% a year against 12.6% a month earlier. Card borrowing is growing faster than consumer credit as a whole, which was running at 9.6%. A bank with 14.5 million retail customers competing for that flow has every reason to buy balances off its rivals with three years of free credit, and the fee is what pays for it.

Sixty days, two words, and one group exclusion

Three conditions sit between the advertised 36 months and the 36 months a particular applicant gets, and all three are on HSBC's own pages.

The first is the 60-day window. A transfer qualifies for the 0% rate only if it is made within 60 days of the account being opened, and the clock runs from opening rather than from approval or from the card arriving. The promotional period on the balance transfer card then runs from the date of each transfer, not from the date of the account, so a reader who waits eight weeks still gets the full term and a reader who waits nine weeks gets nothing. One version of the offer also sets a floor on the transfer itself, at “balances transferred of £100 or more”.

The second is the phrase “up to”, and HSBC is more candid about it than most issuers. Its balance transfer offer page names the alternatives outright: “0% for 36 months on balance transfers with 29.9% APR and 0% for 3 months on purchases” or “0% for 14 months on balance transfers with 29.9% APR and 0% for 3 months on purchases”, with the product page adding a third shape at “0% for 14 months on balance transfers with 29.9% APR”. Note what the alternatives are priced at: 29.9%, four percentage points above the 25.9% in the representative example, which is the rate that governs anything still owed when the 0% ends. Both the promotional length and the go-to rate are set on application.

The third is the group exclusion already stated above, and it has a second edge to it. Holding two HSBC cards is allowed — the maximum is two — but a balance cannot be shuffled between them, and no new card or credit limit increase is available for six months after one is opened, in either direction. The pairings are set as well: HSBC states that Purchase Plus and the Balance Transfer card are “Available for existing Rewards, Premier and Premier World Elite credit card holders only”, so the two promotional cards cannot be taken one after the other. A reader who takes Purchase Plus for its 24 months at 0% on spending has therefore used up six months, one of their two card slots, and their eligibility for the transfer card at the same time.

The bank behind the card, in the regulator's words

The lender is HSBC UK Bank plc, authorised by the Prudential Regulation Authority and regulated by both the Financial Conduct Authority and the PRA under Financial Services Register number 765112. Companies House records it as company 09928412, incorporated on 23 December 2015 and active, a public limited company registered under SIC code 64191 for banks, with its registered office at 1 Centenary Square, Birmingham B1 1HQ.

That 2015 incorporation date is worth a sentence, because the bank is younger than the brand on the card. HSBC UK Bank plc was created as the ring-fenced UK retail bank required by post-crisis banking reform, which is why the registered office is in Birmingham rather than at the group's Canary Wharf headquarters. HSBC's own description is that “HSBC UK is a ring-fenced bank and wholly owned subsidiary of HSBC Holdings plc”. For a cardholder the practical effect is that the entity carrying the lending is a separate legally ring-fenced company from the international business, and the Financial Services Compensation Scheme limit applies at that entity across HSBC UK, first direct and M&S Bank together rather than once per brand.

The scale is HSBC's own published figure, unchanged on this check: 14.5 million retail and wealth customers, 600,000 business clients, over 15 million active customers in total and more than 23,000 colleagues. Credit cards are a small line inside that, and HSBC does not publish how many cards it has in issue or what share of its card balances carry interest, which is a gap other large issuers also leave.

Two charges HSBC has quietly stopped making

The best news on this card range since it was last checked is a subtraction. HSBC's credit card fees page now states of the over-limit charge that “Previously, an over-limit fee of £12 was charged if you exceeded your agreed credit limit” and that “This fee has now been removed, and you will not incur any charges for going over your limit”; and of the returned payment charge that “Previously, a returned payment fee of £5 was applied if a payment from your Direct Debit or cheque was rejected” and “This fee has been removed, and you will not be charged for returned payments”. Both were live figures on this page a week ago. HSBC gives no date for either removal, so when they went is a question for the bank.

What survives is the £12 late payment fee, charged “If you're late paying your minimum monthly payment” and appearing on the next statement, plus reasonable costs if HSBC has to enforce repayment. Two charges gone and one left is a better charging structure than almost any rival publishes, and it is the kind of change a bank makes without a press release.

Cash is still the expensive corner of every card in the range and the rate depends on which card you hold. A cash advance costs 27.9% a year (2.075% a month) on the balance transfer card, 29.9% a year (2.207% a month) on Premier, 35.9% a year (2.592% a month) on Classic and 23.6% a year (1.784% a month) on the student card, in each case on a fee of 2.99% of the amount with a £3 minimum. Interest on a cash advance runs from the day it is taken, so the interest-free period on purchases does not reach it. Spending abroad costs 2.99% of the transaction converted into sterling. Every summary box in the range sets the interest-free period on purchases at a maximum of 56 days, and only where the transaction balance is paid in full and on time.

The minimum payment is the highest of three calculations: all interest added since the last statement plus any default charges plus 1% of the remaining balance; or 2.5% of the full amount owed; or £5. The first of those three is the one worth understanding, because a minimum payment built as interest plus 1% clears the principal at 1% a month and nothing faster. Payments are allocated to the most expensive balance first, which is the statutory order and works in the cardholder's favour.

Where HSBC's own documents stop agreeing with each other

This is the part of the range that needs reading with a pen. On 12 October 2026 HSBC was publishing three different prices for one card. Its product page and its single-card comparison page both give the Balance Transfer Credit Card a representative 25.9% APR with a 3.09% fee. Its four-card comparison page gives the same card 24.9% APR and a 3.19% fee. Its balance transfer offer page carries both fees in two adjacent sentences — “up to 36 months interest free for balance transfers (3.09% fee, min £5)” and “up to 36 months with no interest to pay on balances transferred of £100 or more (3.19% fee, min £5)”. The 36 months is the one term every page agrees on.

That resolves something the previous version of this page got wrong, and it is worth saying so plainly. It criticised HSBC for a worked example reading “would cost you £95.70” on a £3,000 transfer, on the grounds that 3.09% of £3,000 is £92.70. £95.70 is exactly 3.19% of £3,000, and 3.19% is a fee HSBC publishes on two of its own pages. It was never an arithmetic error; it was the other fee. The worked example itself could not be found on any HSBC page on this check, so it appears to have been taken down.

Behind the live pages sit the summary boxes, which are the documents a regulated lender points an applicant at before they borrow, and three of them have not been refreshed in years. The balance transfer box linked from HSBC's own comparison page carries the reference DCP140 and a copyright line reading ©HSBC Group 2021, and states a representative APR of 21.9%, a 0% transfer period of 29 months rather than 36, and a fee of 2.75%. The Premier box is DCP253, ©HSBC Group 2022, and gives a representative 23.9% APR against 29.9% on the product page. The Premier World Elite box is RFB1537 MCP55679, ©HSBC Group 2021, and is the widest gap of the three: “Representative 59.3% APR variable (including annual fee of £195)” with a purchase rate of 18.9% and a minimum credit limit of £5,000, against 100.2% APR, a £290 fee and a 29.9% purchase rate on the live page. The document understates the annual fee by £95 and the purchase rate by 11 percentage points on the most expensive card in the range.

All three boxes also still list the £12 over-limit fee and the £5 returned payment fee that the fees page says have been removed, which is the clearest sign that these are documents nobody has refreshed rather than two sets of terms. That is the fairer diagnosis and it is still a real problem: an applicant for the one product in the range with an offer worth having is reading a five-year-old price list, and every figure in it is wrong in the bank's favour on the rate and in the customer's favour on the fees. Which set of figures governs an agreement signed today needs confirming with HSBC before anybody relies on either.

There is a smaller mismatch of the same family on the student card, and it is the harmless kind. Its product page says “Based on an assumed credit limit of £250” while the card grid and its own comparison page both say £500. The summary box, DCP131 of 2021, settles what the two figures actually are: £250 is the minimum credit limit and £500 the maximum. The product page has put the minimum where the assumed limit belongs. Nothing material turns on it, because the card carries no fee and the representative APR is 18.9% either way, but it is the same lack of upkeep showing up in a second place.

Complaints, and the rule that caps how long a balance can sit

HSBC publishes its own complaints return, which is more than many issuers volunteer, and the figures for the six months from 1 January to 30 June 2026 are unchanged on this check. In the banking and credit cards category it opened 73,367 complaints at a rate of 2.26 per 1,000 accounts, closed 60.1% of them within three days and a further 39.3% after three days but within eight weeks, upheld 58.8%, and recorded general administration and customer service as the main cause. The two closure columns are separate groups rather than running totals, so the figure that matters is their sum: 99.4% of banking and credit card complaints were closed inside the eight-week regulatory window and 0.6% were not.

One correction to make here. The previous version of this page gave a total of 83,623 complaints opened across HSBC UK Bank plc. There is no total row on HSBC's table at all. Its six product categories — banking and credit cards 73,367, home finance 4,246, credit related 4,200, investments 1,207, insurance and pure protection 423 and decumulation and pensions 80 — add to 83,523, and that sum is this page's arithmetic rather than a figure HSBC prints. Credit cards sit in the best-performing of those categories: home finance closed only 29.3% of cases within three days and upheld 62.4%, and investments closed 13.5% within three days.

Two of the card figures point in opposite directions and both are fair. A rate of 2.26 per 1,000 accounts is low, and closing three in five cases inside three days is fast work. An uphold rate of 58.8% means nearly three in five complainants turned out to be right, which is the number a prospective cardholder should weigh rather than the headline volume.

Set against that is a protection the reader gets whichever card they take. Under the FCA's persistent debt rules, in force from 1 March 2018 with firms required to comply by 1 September 2018, a lender has to act when a customer pays more in interest and charges than they repay of the balance over an extended period. The FCA found around 4 million accounts in that position, paying roughly £2.50 in interest and charges for every £1 of balance repaid. At 18 months the firm must make contact and prompt a change in repayments, warning that the card may be suspended; at 36 months it must offer a way to clear the balance in a reasonable period and, where the customer cannot, show forbearance that may include reducing, waiving or cancelling interest, fees or charges. The FCA estimated the package would save consumers between £310 million and £1.3 billion a year, and that around 1.4 million accounts a year would stop receiving automatic credit limit increases. A 36-month 0% transfer and a 36-month persistent-debt intervention are the same length by coincidence, but a reader who reaches the end of one is close to the threshold for the other.

What a reward point is worth, on HSBC's own two numbers

HSBC publishes two valuations for its own points and they differ by a factor of three. The Rewards card's welcome bonus is advertised as “2,500 welcome points worth £25”, which makes a point a penny. The charity redemption on the same rewards pages is priced differently: “Every 1,500 points is worth £5 of support” for Shelter, which makes a point a third of a penny. For shopping vouchers, airline miles and hotel stays, the three redemptions most cardholders would use, HSBC publishes no rate at all and says only that “The cash equivalent of your points will be credited to your account”. Points have to be redeemed within three years from the end of the calendar month in which they were earned.

At the penny valuation the Rewards card returns 0.2% on general spending, because 1 point per £5 is a penny per £5. The anniversary bonus of 2,500 points for £10,000 of spending adds £25 on £10,000, which is another 0.25%, so a cardholder spending exactly £10,000 a year gets about 0.45% back. The 10 points per £1 at selected retailers is a 10% return at the same valuation, which is why HSBC caps it to a registered list rather than offering it on everything.

The interesting sum is the one between the two Premier cards, because HSBC prices the alternative itself. The Premier Credit Card is free and earns 1.5 points per £1; the World Elite costs £290 and earns 3. The fee therefore buys an extra 1.5 points per £1, which at a penny a point is 1.5% — so the fee is recovered at about £19,300 of annual sterling spending. On the charity valuation the same calculation needs about £58,000. Two things close the gap in the first year: the welcome bonuses differ by 40,000 points, worth £400 at a penny and £133 at the charity rate, and the World Elite makes lounge visits free where the Premier card charges £24 a person, so the fee is also about twelve lounge visits. A reader who will take twelve lounge visits or spend £19,300 is being offered a fair deal; a reader who will do neither is paying £290 for points.

That sum rests on a valuation HSBC has published for one card's welcome bonus and never confirmed for the Premier range, so treat the £19,300 as the best case rather than the answer, and ask HSBC what a World Elite point redeems at before paying the fee. On independent ratings there is a plain answer and it is a negative one: Defaqto rates insurance rather than consumer credit, and no ratings agency publishes a star rating or score for an HSBC credit card that could be quoted here. HSBC's own complaints return and its summary boxes are the only dated, primary numbers available on this product.

Common questions

How long is HSBC's 0% balance transfer offer, and what does it cost?

Up to 36 months at 0% on the Balance Transfer Credit Card. HSBC quotes the fee as 3.09% of each transfer on its product page and 3.19% on its comparison and offer pages, with a £5 minimum either way. On a £3,000 balance that is £92.70 or £95.70, which is £2.58 or £2.66 a month across the 36 months. Transfers must be made within 60 days of the account being opened and the 0% period runs from the date of each transfer. The go-to rate afterwards is 25.9% a year variable, and the card gives only three months at 0% on purchases, so it is a card for clearing a balance rather than for spending on.

Can I move a first direct or M&S Bank balance to an HSBC card?

No. HSBC's second-card page states that you cannot transfer balances between two HSBC credit cards, including First Direct or M&S Bank, because all three brands sit inside HSBC UK Bank plc. The same applies to moving a balance between two HSBC-branded cards you hold yourself. If the debt you want to shift is already with one of those three, the 0% offer cannot take it, and this is the single most common reason an application of this kind is of no use to the applicant.

Does HSBC still charge an over-limit or returned payment fee?

No. HSBC's credit card fees page says the £12 over-limit fee “has now been removed, and you will not incur any charges for going over your limit”, and that the £5 returned payment fee “has been removed, and you will not be charged for returned payments”. The £12 late payment fee remains, as do reasonable costs if HSBC has to enforce repayment. HSBC gives no date for either removal, and every card summary box on its site still lists both charges, so ask which document governs your agreement.

Why is the Premier World Elite's representative APR 100.2% when the purchase rate is 29.9%?

Because a representative APR has to include compulsory charges, and the card's £290 annual fee is one. The calculation spreads that fee across the assumed £1,200 credit limit, which is a large proportion of a small balance, so the figure lands at 100.2% variable on a 29.9% purchase rate. Nobody pays 100.2% in interest. Note that the card's own summary box gives a different answer — 59.3% APR including a £195 annual fee, on an 18.9% purchase rate — and carries a 2021 copyright line, so the two documents are five years and £95 apart.

Is the £290 Premier World Elite worth more than the free Premier card?

It depends on how much you spend and how often you fly. The free Premier Credit Card earns 1.5 reward points per £1 of sterling spending; the World Elite earns 3, so the fee buys an extra 1.5 points per £1. At the penny a point HSBC puts on the Rewards card's welcome bonus, that recovers £290 at about £19,300 of annual spending. The welcome bonuses differ by 40,000 points, worth £400 at the same valuation, and lounge visits are free on the World Elite against £24 per person on the Premier card, so the fee is also about twelve lounge visits. HSBC does not publish what a Premier point redeems at, so treat those figures as the best case and ask before paying.

How many HSBC credit cards can I hold at once?

Two. The second has to be a different card from the first and the pairings are set: HSBC offers Purchase Plus and the Balance Transfer card only to existing Rewards, Premier or Premier World Elite holders, offers Rewards to holders of Balance Transfer, Purchase Plus, Classic, Premier or World Elite, and offers the two Premier cards to Balance Transfer, Purchase Plus and Rewards holders who also hold an HSBC Premier Account. Each card has its own credit limit, limits cannot be moved between them, and you cannot apply for a new card or a limit increase within six months of opening one.

Our verdict

Judged on the one thing a balance transfer card is bought for, HSBC's is at the front of the market: up to 36 months at 0% for a fee of 3.09% or 3.19% is £92.70 or £95.70 on £3,000, about a seventh of what a year's interest at the Bank of England's 21.55% effective rate would cost, and it buys three years rather than one. Purchase Plus covers the other half of the job at up to 24 months on spending, six of the seven cards charge nothing to hold, and HSBC has quietly dropped both its over-limit and returned payment fees since this page was last checked. The things to check before applying are all HSBC's own small print rather than anything hidden: the 60-day transfer window, the "up to" on both the term and the rate, the minimum credit limits of £500 and £250 behind the £1,200 and £1,000 assumed in the adverts, and the group rule that stops an HSBC, first direct or M&S Bank balance moving across at all. That last one disqualifies a large share of the people most likely to be reading, so it is the first thing to establish. What has not improved is the paperwork. Three cards are sold on live pages that disagree with their own summary boxes, and on the Premier World Elite the regulated disclosure understates the annual fee by £95. On price this is a competitive range; read your own agreement rather than the advert, and ask which figure governs it.
