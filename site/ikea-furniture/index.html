IKEA Furniture Review: 365-Day Returns, Prices and the Refund Card

Rates and terms checked 15 November 2026 · Furniture · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

IKEA gives you 365 days to change your mind, which is the longest return window in British furniture retailing by a wide margin: Dunelm allows 28 days, Habitat and Amazon 30. A BILLY bookcase is £55, a two-seat KLIPPAN sofa £299 with a 10-year guarantee on the frame and cushions, and a standard double VESTMARKA sprung mattress £129, reduced from £139. Figures read from ikea.com/gb/en on 15 November 2026.

The scale behind those prices is the reason they hold. IKEA Limited took £2.25 billion of retail sales in the financial year to 31 August 2025 across 20 full-sized stores, six small stores and four Plan and Order Points, with 43.9 million store visits and 43.2% of turnover placed online. Its parent group put £117 million into cutting prices in the previous financial year and more than £10 million into pay in 2025. Sales volumes rose 4.4% while the value of those sales fell, which is what a price cut looks like in a set of accounts.

One thing is worth knowing before you load the trolley. The product page on a £55 bookcase says “Return within 365 days for a full refund”, with an asterisk; the policy behind the asterisk says that “items that have been opened, assembled or are not sellable at full retail value will only be refunded to a refund card”, and that the balance on that card “cannot be converted into cash”. The 365 days are real. Whether the money comes back as money depends on whether you opened the box.

Three hundred and sixty-five days, and the moment the box is opened

Two sentences on IKEA’s own returns policy do all the work. The first: “you can return most* unopened IKEA products within 365 days for a full refund”. The second: “we take back tested products within 365 days — even assembled ones”. Taken together they are the most generous published return terms of any furniture retailer on this site, and an assembled wardrobe going back a year later is not something Dunelm, Habitat or Amazon offer at any price.

The third sentence is the one to read twice. “Items that have been opened, assembled or are not sellable at full retail value will only be refunded to a refund card”. IKEA’s gift and refund card terms add that the balance “cannot be converted into cash” and cannot be used to settle a credit card or an IKEA Business account. On the buy-back terms the card is described as redeemable “for an unlimited period of time from the date of issue”, so nothing expires — but nothing leaves IKEA either.

The mechanics are generous where they are cheap and thin where they are not. Anything under 10kg that fits inside 60 x 50 x 50cm goes back free through InPost at more than 7,000 drop-off points, though only on orders placed online and never as an exchange. An express return registered in advance produces a barcode “valid for 14 days”, for private customers only. A home collection can be arranged from the returns page, and no charge for it is published anywhere except on mattresses, where collection is £25 — 19.4% of the price of the £129 double. Mattresses get their own 365-night trial with one exchange allowed inside the year, a request to “wait at least a month” before deciding, and any price difference credited, again, to a refund card.

Two of IKEA’s own live pages also disagree about where a return will be refused. The returns page says “returns are not accepted in our Plan and Order points such as Preston and Hull”; the returns policy page says “returns are not accepted in our Plan and Order points such as Preston and Stockport”. Both were read on 15 November 2026 and neither publishes the full list, so a shopper at a Plan and Order Point cannot tell from either page whether their parcel will be taken back.

What the law insists on, and where IKEA’s goodwill begins

The distinction that makes sense of all of this is the one between the fortnight Parliament guarantees and the year IKEA volunteers. Under the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, an online shopper has 14 days after the last item in the order is delivered to cancel. Regulation 34 of those Regulations then settles how the money comes back: reimbursement “without undue delay” and no later than 14 days after the goods are received or the consumer supplies evidence of sending them, with no fee charged for making the refund, and — the clause that matters here — the trader “must make the reimbursement using the same means of payment as the consumer used for the initial transaction” unless the consumer expressly agrees otherwise.

Inside those 14 days, in other words, a refund card is not something IKEA can impose on an unwilling customer. The same regulation allows a deduction only where the value of the goods is diminished by handling “beyond what is necessary to establish the nature, characteristics and functioning of the goods”, a test the Regulations gloss as handling that “goes beyond the sort of handling that might reasonably be allowed in a shop”. Opening a flat pack to check that a wardrobe clears the ceiling is squarely the kind of thing a shop allows.

From day 15 to day 365, none of that applies, because the window is IKEA’s own and IKEA writes the terms. That is the honest frame for the refund card, and it is also why the policy’s own wording is worth quoting: “it is at IKEA’s sole discretion what is considered “tried and tested” and “used””. A regulation that sets an objective standard against a policy that reserves the judgement to the retailer is a real difference, and it falls on a date rather than on a condition. The 351 days after the statutory fortnight are a gift; they are just not a gift of money.

Proof of purchase works the same way round. “Where a proof of purchase is provided, we issue refunds back to the payment type used to make the purchase”; without one, returns “may be refused, or issued to a refund card”. IKEA offers to find a lost receipt, which most retailers will not do, and the custom-made, food and perishable exclusions are the ones the Regulations themselves carve out.

£5, £25, £40 and the weight of the box

IKEA prices delivery by weight rather than by basket value, and publishes the whole grid. For a lighter order — under 75kg in total with no single box above 25kg — standard delivery is £5 up to 15kg and £10 from 15kg to 75kg, with express at £10 and £15. Between 75kg and 100kg a £10 surcharge applies. For a heavier or fragile order, or one with any box over 25kg, standard delivery is £25 up to 150kg and £40 up to 1,000kg, express £30 and £45, and a four-hour slot £35 and £50. Above 1,000kg there is a £60 surcharge. A weekend slot adds £5. The minimum order value for delivery at all is £15.

Standard delivery is quoted at 5 to 30 days, which is a five-week spread on a single service level, and express is next day for orders placed before 2pm “or up to 4 days”. IKEA Family members get small-parcel delivery free once they spend £60, against £5 for everybody else: the shortfall and the charge are the same £5, so on a basket in the fifties the cheapest way to pay for delivery is to buy something else. There are also 101 Collect Near You points, and in-store delivery for shoppers who drove in and could not fit it all in the car — £29 from the store, £39 for same-day and £49 for a chosen time, the last two run by Taskrabbit and available at Birmingham, Croydon, Greenwich, Lakeside, Warrington and Wembley.

Assembly starts at £33 and the labour is Taskrabbit’s rather than IKEA’s: “you will be charged a professional assembly rate based on the products you want assembled”, with no published rate card behind it. On a £55 bookcase, £33 is 60% of the price of the thing being built. The Taskers fit tip-over restraints and “will also secure items to the wall at no additional cost”, which matters on a BILLY: its own product page carries a tipping hazard warning and says the unit “must be securely anchored”. Wall and ceiling fixings themselves are done at the customer’s request and risk under IKEA’s Secure it policy. A bedroom measuring and planning service is £25 and reimbursable against an order; a home move and setup service is free of charge; kitchen installation carries a five-year workmanship guarantee and no published price at all.

Twenty-five years on a kitchen, three years on a shower hose

The guarantee list is unusually specific, and it is ordered by how hard a thing is to replace rather than by what it costs. The METOD kitchen system carries 25 years against defects in material and workmanship, valid for domestic kitchen use; the ENHET kitchen system and every kitchen mixer tap get 10 years; KNOXHULT and all IKEA appliances get five, with the budget LAGAN and TILLREDA ranges cut to a “free 2 year guarantee”. Bathrooms run to 10 years on wash-basins, mixer taps and shower mixers and on the ENHET bathroom series, TOLKEN countertops and FAXÄLVEN mirrors, and drop to three years on shower accessories — riser rails, heads, hoses and the parking bracket.

Furniture sits in the middle. Sofa and armchair frames and cushions are covered for 10 years, as are the BESTÅ storage system, PAX and KOMPLEMENT wardrobes and their interiors, mattresses carrying the right logo, and the main parts of the BEKANT, IDÅSEN, MITTZON, TROTTEN and RODULF desks along with the moving parts and frames of thirteen named workchairs, from MARKUS to MULLFJÄLLET. Kitchenware does best of all: 15 years on pots, pans and woks without a non-stick coating, and 15 years on most IKEA knives, with the FÖRDUBBLA, HACKIG, UPPFYLLD, SKALAD and SMÅBIT knives, cutlery and steak knives and sharpeners all excluded. Every guarantee runs from the day the product is received.

One entry on that page is a loose end. Sofas and armchairs are listed at 10 years and then at 25 years for “some products” which the page does not name, pointing instead at a PDF. A reader comparing a £299 KLIPPAN against a dearer range cannot tell from the guarantee page which bracket either one falls into, and a 15-year difference in cover is not a detail. The KLIPPAN’s own product page settles its own case — 10 years, with a removable cover washable at 40C — but it should not take a product page to answer a question the guarantee page is for.

Selling it back at 45%, and the list of things they will not take

IKEA will buy its own furniture back, and it publishes the grade ladder, which almost no retailer does. An item in as new condition with no scratches is worth 45% of the original price; very good, with minor scratches, 35%; well-used, with several scratches, 25%. IKEA Family members get 15 percentage points on top of each, so a £55 BILLY bought and returned unmarked is worth £24.75 at the standard rate and £33.00 as a member — the page’s own arithmetic on IKEA’s own percentages. Estimates are subject to inspection by a co-worker at the store you choose. Payment is “an IKEA refund card for the agreed amount”; a business account gets a digital discount code instead, valid five years, which “may only be applied against your purchase of new IKEA products and cannot be exchanged for cash”.

Then read the exclusions, because they are most of a furniture shop. The terms decline beds except extendable children’s beds, mattresses and bed textiles, sofas and armchairs and any other upholstered or leather product, kitchens including worktops, cabinets and fronts, system wardrobes, appliances and electricals, rugs, cookware, tableware, bathroom accessories, other soft goods, oversized items, anything used outdoors, and anything non-IKEA, modified or unassembled. A £299 KLIPPAN is upholstered and therefore outside the scheme entirely; a PAX wardrobe is a system wardrobe and outside it too. The scheme is aimed squarely at the boxed, flat, bolted-together end of the range — and it will not take an item that has not been assembled, which is the same act that turns a cash refund into a card.

What happens next is the half of the story the policy pages leave out, and IKEA’s UK report supplies it. In the year to 31 August 2025 the business sold 3.3 million Re-shop and Re-use items in the UK for £21.1 million, of which £1.6 million came online across 53,000 items — up from 21,000 the year before. A further 546,000 items were rewrapped and returned to stock, and 517,970 spare parts were given away free. That is a genuinely circular operation rather than a slogan, and it also explains the refund card: an opened box is a Re-shop item, and IKEA would rather pay for it in credit that comes back through the till. Buy from that section and the loop closes on itself — the second-hand pages state that “you have 365 days to return the purchased item, and we’ll refund you on a refund card”, with no cash route at all, on goods sold “as-is” and reservable for 48 hours at 26 UK stores.

Three per cent of everything, paid to a company in Delft

The shop is not the brand. Inter IKEA Systems B.V. describes itself as “the owner of the IKEA Concept and worldwide IKEA franchisor”, and states the price of admission plainly: “IKEA franchisees pay Inter IKEA Group an annual fee of 3% over their net sales”. In return they “get access to the IKEA trademarks” and are authorised to sell the range and operate the stores. Inter IKEA Group’s own total revenue was €26.3 billion in the year to 31 August 2025, against €26.5 billion the year before.

The British stores belong to the largest franchisee. Ingka Group — Ingka Holding B.V. and its controlled entities, based in Leiden — is owned by a Dutch foundation, Stichting INGKA Foundation, with Stichting IKEA Foundation and Stichting IMAS Foundation alongside it, and operates “under franchise agreements with Inter IKEA Systems B.V.” Its FY25 revenue was €41.5 billion, down 0.9%, on a net profit of €1.4 billion, across 620 stores and other formats against 574 the year before, with 166,460 co-workers, store visits up 1.3% and online visitors up 4.6%. The UK was its fourth-largest market at 6.8% of sales, behind Germany, the United States and France.

Set those two figures side by side and the shape of the system is clear. Total IKEA retail sales across every franchisee were €44.6 billion in FY25, down 1.0% and down 0.3% once currency is stripped out, on 915 million store visits, up 1.9%. Ingka’s €41.5 billion is therefore about 93% of the whole network — the page’s own division — and 3% of it is roughly €1.25 billion a year flowing to the franchisor. Applied to IKEA Limited’s £2.25 billion of British retail sales, the fee is about £67.5 million a year for the right to use the name, against the £117 million the group says it put into cutting prices in FY24. Neither number is hidden and neither is an accusation; they are simply the two halves of how a flat pack gets to be £55.

The two sides of the family do not quite agree on how many of them there are. Ingka’s own page calls itself one of 12 franchisees; Inter IKEA’s year in review counts 13 groups of IKEA franchisees worldwide. Both were read on 15 November 2026 and neither reconciles the other. Inter IKEA’s FY26 results page returned a server error on two separate attempts, so the most recent figures that could be read here are FY25 ones.

A swivel chair, a mirror and nine entries on the register

The Government’s product safety register, searched for IKEA on 15 November 2026, returns nine recalls across five years. Two of them are furniture. The ODGER swivel chair in anthracite, case 2212-0179 of 3 January 2023, covers chairs with date stamps “before and including 2221” and was recalled because the product “presents a risk of injuries due to the possibility for the chair to crack and break at the star base”, causing the user to fall; IKEA asked owners to stop using it and return it to a store for a full refund. The LETTAN mirror, case 2301-0066 of 16 January 2023, covers four article numbers — 604.352.69, 004.353.14, 804.353.05 and 804.353.10 — sold from February 2020 with date stamps up to 2105, because “the wall fittings for the mirror may break, causing the mirror to fall”. It was made in China and recalled from end users. Both were notified by Local Authority Trading Standards and both are recorded as failing the General Product Safety Regulations 2005, and on both the published risk level is “Not provided”.

The remaining seven are housewares and electricals rather than furniture: a BLÅVINGAD fishing game (2303-0118, 27 March 2023), a METALLISK espresso maker (2206-0033, 7 June 2022), HEROISK and TALRIKA tableware (2105-0173, 28 May 2021), an ASKSTORM 40W USB charger (2401-0040, 12 January 2024), VÄRMFRONT power banks (2407-0105, 31 July 2024) and, most recently, the IKEA 365+ HJÄLTE stirring spatula and VÄRDEFULL garlic press, both dated 15 June 2026. Nine recalls in five years, on a range running to thousands of articles sold through 620 stores, is a low rate rather than a high one, and the two furniture entries are both fixings and frames rather than fire.

IKEA publishes a direct route for safety problems, at product.safety.uk@ikea.com, which is more than most retailers do. Note that the regulator’s own newer domain, productsafety.gov.uk, refused automated access on 15 November 2026; the same register is served on gov.uk and that is where these nine were read.

Who answers the phone, and the body that cannot look at it

One number covers everything: the Customer Support Centre on 01733 520006, open 07:00 to 20:00 Monday to Saturday and 07:00 to 19:00 on Sunday, with a co-worker on live chat from 09:00 to 17:00 and a chatbot around the clock. The registered office is 1 Bow Churchyard, London EC4M 9DQ, which the company moved to on 7 July 2025.

What the contact page does not carry is a complaints procedure. There is no published acknowledgement target, no final-response deadline, no escalation ladder and no alternative dispute resolution scheme or ombudsman named anywhere on it. The Furniture and Home Improvement Ombudsman is the body that hears this kind of dispute in Britain, and its member list — read on 15 November 2026 — includes B&Q, DFS, Dreams, Furniture Village, Marks and Spencer, Wickes and Wren Kitchens, and no IKEA entity at all. A dispute about a sofa therefore ends with IKEA or in the county court.

The credit side is the exact opposite, and the asymmetry is worth seeing whole. IKEA Limited “acts as a credit broker and not a lender and only offers finance products from Ikano Bank AB (publ)”, so anything bought on the IKEA Family credit card at 23.9% APR representative variable, or on the 0% loan of £1,500 to £15,000 over 24, 30, 36 or 48 months, sits inside a regulated relationship with the Financial Ombudsman Service behind it. The furniture does not. Buy a £2,000 kitchen on the loan and the way you paid for it has an ombudsman; the kitchen itself has a phone number.

Two smaller things sit on the public record. IKEA Limited’s accounts for the year to 31 August 2025 were filed on 16 September 2026 and run to 108 pages, against 35 pages the year before; nine months from that year end falls on 31 May 2026, which puts the filing about 108 days past the deadline on the page’s own arithmetic. And the leadership named in IKEA’s October 2025 results release, Peter Jelkeby, ceased to be a director on 1 January 2026 and has moved to lead IKEA Germany; David McCabe, a 30-year veteran who joined in 1996, took over as Country Retail Manager and Chief Sustainability Officer for the UK and Ireland on 1 July 2026, announced on 13 April 2026.

  • A 365-night mattress trial with one exchange allowed inside the year, and a published £25 collection charge rather than an unstated one
  • Free returns by post through InPost for anything under 10kg and inside 60 x 50 x 50cm, at more than 7,000 drop-off points

Common questions

How long do I really have to return IKEA furniture?

365 days, which is the longest window in UK furniture retailing: Dunelm allows 28 days and both Habitat and Amazon 30. Unopened products go back for a full refund, and IKEA states that it takes back tested products within the same 365 days “even assembled ones”. Separately from that policy, the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013 give you 14 days from delivery of the last item in the order to cancel an online purchase outright.

Will I get my money back, or a voucher?

It depends entirely on whether the box was opened. IKEA’s policy says items that have been opened, assembled or are not sellable at full retail value “will only be refunded to a refund card”, and the card terms state that the balance cannot be converted into cash. An unopened item with proof of purchase is refunded to the payment method you used. Inside the statutory 14 days, regulation 34 of the 2013 Regulations requires the refund to be made by the same means of payment you paid with, so the refund-card rule bites from day 15 onwards.

What does IKEA charge for delivery and assembly?

Delivery is priced by weight. An order under 75kg with no box over 25kg is £5 up to 15kg and £10 to 75kg on standard delivery; a heavier or fragile order is £25 up to 150kg and £40 up to 1,000kg, with a £60 surcharge above that and £5 extra for a weekend slot. The minimum order value is £15, and IKEA Family members get small-parcel delivery free once they spend £60. Assembly starts at £33 and is carried out by Taskrabbit Taskers, who also fit tip-over restraints and wall fixings at no extra charge.

How much will IKEA pay to buy my furniture back?

45% of the original price for an item graded as new with no scratches, 35% for very good with minor scratches and 25% for well-used, with 15 percentage points more for IKEA Family members — so a £55 BILLY in unmarked condition is worth £24.75, or £33.00 as a member. Payment is an IKEA refund card, not cash. Check the exclusions first: sofas, armchairs, beds, mattresses, kitchens, system wardrobes, appliances, rugs, outdoor furniture and anything unassembled are all outside the scheme.

What guarantee comes with an IKEA sofa or kitchen?

Sofa and armchair frames and cushions carry 10 years against defects in materials and workmanship, and the KLIPPAN’s own product page confirms 10 years. Kitchens run from 25 years on the METOD system, through 10 years on ENHET and every kitchen mixer tap, to five years on KNOXHULT and IKEA appliances, with LAGAN and TILLREDA cut to two. Note that the guarantee page also lists 25 years for “some” sofas and armchairs without naming which, so ask before assuming the longer term applies.

Is there an ombudsman if an IKEA complaint goes nowhere?

Not for the furniture. IKEA’s contact page names a Customer Support Centre on 01733 520006 and no alternative dispute resolution scheme, and no IKEA entity appears in the Furniture and Home Improvement Ombudsman’s member list, where B&Q, DFS, Dreams, Furniture Village, Marks and Spencer, Wickes and Wren Kitchens all do. Anything bought on IKEA credit is different: the lender is Ikano Bank AB (publ) and that relationship has the Financial Ombudsman Service behind it.

Our verdict

On the figure this category is compared on, IKEA wins by a distance: 365 days against 28 at Dunelm and 30 at Habitat and Amazon, and it takes back assembled furniture rather than only sealed boxes. The prices behind it are real too — £55 for a BILLY, £299 for a two-seat KLIPPAN with 10 years on the frame, £129 for a double mattress with a 365-night trial — and the guarantee ladder runs to 25 years on a METOD kitchen. The catch is not the window but the currency: open the flat pack and the refund becomes a card whose balance cannot be turned into cash, and the asterisk on the product page does not say so. Inside the statutory 14 days the law settles that in your favour; after it, the terms are IKEA's to write. Buy with the box unopened until you are sure, keep the receipt, and treat the 365 days as what it is — a year to decide, on IKEA's terms rather than Parliament's.
