JoJo Maman Bebe Review: Prices, Delivery and the B Corp Scorecard

Rates and terms checked 15 November 2026 · Children's Fashion · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

A cotton bodysuit at JoJo Maman Bébé costs £12, a three-pack of footed sleepsuits £30 at £10 each, and a lined pramsuit £36, across 1,378 baby lines and 1,206 children's lines listed on jojomamanbebe.co.uk on 15 November 2026. Home delivery is free once an order passes £60 and £4.95 below it, and free at more than 500 collection points whatever the basket comes to. Every price and document quoted below was read that day on the company's own pages, on the public registers and on its own published reports.

The thing that sets this shop apart from everything else in the category is an audit. JoJo became a certified B Corporation in August 2016 — the first childrenswear brand in Britain to do it — and B Lab's public register gives it an overall B Impact Score of 92.6, against the 80 a company has to reach to qualify and a median of 50.9 for the businesses that complete the assessment at all. The brand's own page adds that it is reassessed every three years. Very few retailers of anything pay an outside body to score them on governance, workers, community, environment and customers and then publish the result, and the reports that come out of it are the reason this page can quote carbon figures, factory grades and a fibre mix at all.

One structural point belongs up here because it decides which company a shopper is dealing with. The terms, dated 10/11/2026, open: “This website is operated by, and the goods are supplied by Next Retail Limited trading as JoJo Maman Bébé”, company 04521150, Desford Road, Enderby, Leicester LE19 4AT, VAT 179 7658 90. JoJo Maman Bébé Ltd itself is company 02737508, incorporated on 5 August 1992, and its own modern slavery statement for February 2025 to January 2026 gives the shareholders as Foxford Capital L5 DAC at 56% and Next Holdings Ltd at 44%. There are 40 shops — 38 in the UK and two in Ireland — so the 500-plus collection points are mostly somebody else's counter.

What £12 buys, and the five bodysuits it takes to get free delivery

The cheapest thing in the baby range on 15 November 2026 was a White Born in 2026 Bodysuit at £12, with a Natural Farmyard All Over Embroidered Bodysuit at £12 to £13 beside it. Above those, the bodysuit sub-category runs £15 to £16 for a single broderie or embroidered-collar body and £22 to £23 for a two-pack, across 16 lines. A Green Sheep Cotton Footed 3 Pack Sleepsuit is £30, and the product page does the division for you at £10 each. Single sleepsuits and waterproof booties are £22, a Gruffalo dressing gown £28, a tunic-and-leggings set £30 to £32, a fairisle cardigan £32 to £34, a 3.5 tog baby sleeping bag £41 and a cosy borg pramsuit £36 or £36.50. Outerwear is the top of the range: £47.50 to £49.50 for a waterproof lined coat, £55.50 to £59.50 for a fleece-lined puddle suit and £65.50 to £70 for a four-in-one jacket.

That is a boutique price list rather than a supermarket one, and the delivery arithmetic follows from it. Standard next-day delivery to a home address is £4.95 through Evri, ordered by 11pm, and the help centre puts it plainly: “We deliver to most UK postcodes the next day for just £4.95 via Evri.” An Evri ParcelShop or locker is the same £4.95. On the cheapest bodysuit in the shop, £4.95 is 41% of the garment; on a £22 sleepsuit it is 22%.

The way round it is £60, and five of those £12 bodysuits bring a basket to exactly £60 — which both pages word as “over £60”, so the fifth may not be enough. The home page carries the threshold as “FREE next-day home delivery on orders over £60*”, with the asterisk resolving to “Excludes personalised items and orders requiring 2-person delivery. Offer ends 21/12/2026. T&Cs apply.” The help centre article behind it, last updated on 9 November 2026, states the same figure as a standing fact — “Delivery is free for orders over £60” — with no end date on it anywhere. Both pages agree on the number; only one of them treats it as temporary, and a parent planning a January order has no way of telling from the shop's own pages which reading holds. One pramsuit at £36 and one dressing gown at £28 come to £64 and clear it; a single £41 sleeping bag does not.

The route that costs nothing at any basket size is collection. The home page offers “Free next-day delivery to 500+ stores” and the help article widens that to “most JoJo Maman Bébé stores and hundreds of other locations”, ordered by 11pm. With 40 shops of its own, almost all of those 500-plus counters belong to other retailers, which is worth knowing before driving to one expecting a nursery department. Returning to a shop is free as well, and the terms note that where a whole order goes back the delivery fee is refunded with it. There is also a loyalty scheme, Grow with JoJo, whose current promotion gives £10 off orders over £75 after other discounts, excluding staff purchases, delivery charges, sale items and gift vouchers.

A score of 92.6, and the 3.9 inside it

B Lab's public register gives JoJo Maman Bébé Limited of Monmouthshire an overall B Impact Score of 92.6, built from governance 16.8, workers 20.3, community 25.3, environment 26.1 and customers 3.9. The register prints the two numbers that give those any meaning: a company has to reach a Qualification Score of 80 to certify at all, and “The median score for ordinary businesses who complete the assessment is currently 50.9.” So 92.6 is comfortably clear of the bar and most of the way again above the median, on a sector listing of Apparel within Wholesale/Retail and an operating footprint of the United Kingdom and Ireland. The company's own page says it became a B Corp in 2016, that it was “the first childrenswear brand in the UK to become a B Corp”, and that “Every three years, we are reassessed to maintain our status and track our progress.”

The interesting part is the distribution. Four of the five impact areas carry 88.5 points between them and the fifth carries 3.9. The five components as published add to 92.4 against a stated overall of 92.6, each given to one decimal place and nothing on the register reconciling the pair. Customers is the area about the people buying the clothes, and it is the one a shopper has most reason to care about; it is also, on the brand's own published scorecard, the weakest thing about the business by a factor of four against its next-lowest area. B Lab's assessment weights that section lightly for a straightforward retailer, which is a large part of the explanation, and saying so is fairer than pretending the gap is a scandal. The point stands anyway: the audit that makes this brand unusual is mostly an audit of how it treats its staff, its suppliers and the planet, not of how it treats a customer whose parcel has gone astray.

Two dates are missing and both of them matter to anyone reading 92.6 as a current figure. The register publishes the month of first certification, August 2016, and no recertification date at all, so a visitor cannot tell from it when the 92.6 was last tested. The brand's own impact report says “Now in our tenth year as a B Corp”, which is consistent with a three-year cycle running 2016, 2019, 2022 and 2025, but nothing on either party's pages states which of those produced the published score.

There is a second reason the dates are worth asking about, and it is in B Lab's own rules rather than in any criticism of the company. B Lab UK's guidance on acquisitions, dated 23 January 2025, requires a certified business that changes control to “let B Lab know within 90 days after the effective date of the change in control” and then to “commit to completing recertification within a year”, with two years allowed for a Large Enterprise, and it adds that “the early recertification clause is not triggered if the new parent is already certified.” Next plc is not a certified B Corporation. Separately, B Lab's own recertification policy, effective 8 April 2025, records that “All companies with recertification dates in 2026 have been granted a 12 month extension to their recertification date” as the standards themselves are rewritten. None of that is evidence of anything wrong. It does mean that the single most distinctive claim on this shop's pages is the one claim a reader cannot date.

Four sizes below newborn, and nothing for a seven-year-old

The baby chart starts lower than most of the category manages. Before newborn at 56cm there are three further sizes — Premature at 48cm, Extra tiny baby at 50cm and Tiny baby at 52cm — and then 0-3 months at 62cm, 3-6 at 68cm, 6-9 at 74cm, 6-12 at 78cm, 9-12 at 80cm, 12-18 at 86cm and 18-24 at 92cm. For a parent whose baby arrived at 30 weeks, four sizes below newborn is not a detail, and almost nothing on a high street offers them.

The children's chart is where the holes are. It runs 12-18 months at 84cm, 18-24 months at 92cm, 2-3 years at 98cm, 3-4 at 104cm, 4-5 at 110cm, 5-6 at 116cm, 6-7 at 122cm, 8-9 at 134cm and 10-12 at 152cm. There is no 7-8 years, so the step from 6-7 to 8-9 is 12cm against the 6cm steps below it, and a seven-year-old measuring 128cm sits in the middle of a gap. The jump from 8-9 to 10-12 is 18cm wide and covers three school years in one garment. Nor do the two charts agree with each other: 12-18 months is 86cm on the baby chart and 84cm on the children's chart, the same label with two heights on the same page.

The top of the ladder is disputed by the shop's own navigation. The size guide offers 10-12 years at 152cm, while the menus across the site label the departments “Girls (2-9 years)” and “Boys (2-9 years)”. Both are the retailer's own words on the same website, so the honest answer is that the chart goes to 12 and the shop advertises to 9, and a parent of a ten-year-old should check what is actually listed rather than trust either. What the guide does supply is the method: it warns that JoJo sizes “vary slightly from other brands” and tells you to measure height with shoes off from the top of the head to the floor, the arm from the collar area of the shoulder to the wrist and the leg from waist to ankle down the outside. On a range where a single garment can be £55 and the next size up is 12cm away, measuring first is the advice worth taking.

Sending it back: four windows and a £2.50 charge

An online order can go back “within 28 days of receipt, unused and in their original condition”, counted from delivery to the home or to the shop if store delivery was chosen. A full-price item bought in a JoJo shop gets 30 days with proof of purchase, a sale item bought in a shop gets 14, and something bought at one of the JoJo concessions inside a FatFace store gets 28 days at either the concession or any JoJo shop, for refund or exchange. Four windows for one retailer is not a trap, but it is four numbers to keep straight, and only one of them is the one printed on the website. Orders placed between 20 September and 24 December 2026 are covered by a Christmas extension to Sunday 17 January 2027.

Handing something over a counter costs nothing and needs no paperwork: “No label required - the store assistant will simply scan your items at the till.” Everything else has a price. “A £2.50 returns charge* will be applied for each collection/return, regardless of the number of items you return”, whether a courier comes to the door or the parcel goes into a ParcelShop or locker, and on a card or PayPal order it comes out of the refund. The charge is per parcel rather than per garment, so sending four things back together costs the same £2.50 as sending one — which is the right way round, and worth saying.

Refunds run to a published timetable rather than a vague promise, which is unusual enough to credit: five working days for a card or gift-card refund on a shop return and ten working days where a courier or ParcelShop was used, against one and six working days on PayPal. The confirmation email carries an ARN. Statutory rights sit underneath all of this and the terms state them: “you have the right to cancel your contract at any time up to 14 days after the day on which you receive the goods”, so the 28 days on an online order are 14 days of law and 14 days of the shop's own goodwill.

Some things cannot come back at all, and the list is the ordinary one: “Personalised items, including our gift boxing service, cannot be returned or exchanged, unless faulty”, and “pierced jewellery items, cosmetics, toiletries and mattresses cannot be returned if unwrapped or unsealed unless faulty.” Gift sets have to go back whole, box and tissue paper included — “We cannot accept returns of individual items from gift sets” — with no swaps for a different size or item. The one soft spot in the paperwork is maintenance: the article covering shop returns was last updated on 9 November 2022, four years before the version of the policy the website is now running, and the article on how long you have points at an EXCEPTIONS TO THE RETURNS POLICY section that is not reproduced with it.

The poppers, and who named the garments

The shop keeps its own recall page, which is more than most clothing retailers do, and the first thing on it is an urgent notice about poppers attached incorrectly, warning that a popper could become accessible or detach and create a choking hazard. The notice names no product. It carries no style code, no batch number and no description of which garments are affected, and the article's own timestamps put it as created on 20 September 2024 and last updated on 16 October 2025.

The register that does name them is the Office for Product Safety and Standards. Alert 2510-0179, published on 24 October 2025 — eight days after the shop's own notice was last touched — is headed “Product Recall: JoJo Maman Bébé Trousers and Rompers” and describes the product type as “Rompers (Various) and Trousers”. The hazard is set out in the regulator's words: “The product presents a risk of choking as the poppers can detach or break off when worn”, “creating small parts that fit entirely within a small parts cylinder”, and “The product does not meet the requirements of the General Product Safety Regulations 2005.” Country of origin India, measure “Recall of the product from end users”, with owners told to stop using the garment immediately and return it to the nearest JoJo shop for a full refund. The full list of styles sits in a PDF linked from that alert, and the risk level is recorded as “Not provided”. A parent checking the shop's own page would learn that there is a popper problem; a parent checking the government register would learn which garments it is.

Two older recalls on the shop's page are handled properly and show what the first notice could look like. The Wee Gallery Shaped Bath Books in Fish & Friends and Frog & Friends are named with item numbers 9780711296190 and 9780711296213 and batch KT052024, with the fault stated — “The varnish coating of the product can begin to come away from the surface in water, causing flaking” — and the instruction to “stop using it immediately and return it for a full refund.” The Savannah Supersized Foam Playmat, item 230118, and the Urban Garden Foam Mat, item A93497, are named with four batch codes apiece (IN 0623, IN 0723, IN 0823 and IN 1023), the batch marking located on the bottom of the product or under the barcode, and the fault that “the film can become scratched and peel off after a certain period, creating small parts.” The line to ring for an online purchase is 0333 777 4529; a shop purchase goes back over a counter, and a recipient of a gift is told to contact customer service or a shop as soon as possible. On the OPSS register those playmats appear as reference 2409-0034 of 12 September 2024, alongside a Bebe au Lait Wooden Teether Rings recall, 2203-0071, from 1 April 2022.

One safety check costs nothing and needs only the parcel. The Green Sheep Cotton Footed 3 Pack Sleepsuit at £30 carries “KEEP AWAY FROM FIRE” on its own product page, which is the labelling the Nightwear (Safety) Regulations 1985 require on children's nightwear that has not been tested to the low-flammability standard. The regulations cover nightwear designed for and normally worn by anyone under 13, so on any sleepsuit, nightdress or dressing gown in this shop the wording inside the neck is the difference between a garment tested for flame spread and one that has not been. It is the single most useful thing a parent can read in the house, and it is printed before the sale as well as after it.

Reading the impact report's own numbers

The second annual impact report covers February 2025 to January 2026 and is introduced by Mark Wright as chief executive. It is the document that makes this page possible, and the figures in it cut both ways, which is the mark of a report worth reading rather than a brochure. Total operational carbon fell from 236 tonnes of CO2 equivalent to 146.41, with scope 1 down from 12.64 to 9.57 and scope 2 from 223.36 to 136.84 — an 89.59 tonne reduction in a year. Waste out of the shops fell 59%, landfill from 1.48% to 0.15% of it, with anaerobic digestion taking 17.31% against 0.67% the year before. LED lighting covers 80% of shop floors, 100% of windows and 65% of back-of-house areas. Air freight fell from 22% of goods shipped to 9% and sea rose from 68% to 86%, a 13 percentage point shift in one year, against a stated target to cut air freight by a further 2% a year for five years.

Scope 3 is not in the table. For a clothing business the emissions in the supply chain and in the garments themselves dwarf the lights and the vans, and the report lists measuring them as a future step with no date against it. So the 146.41 tonnes is a real, falling, honestly reported number that covers the smaller part of the problem, and a reader should not take it as the company's footprint.

The fibre mix is published in full: cotton 71%, polyester 19%, nylon 4%, viscose 4%, other fibres 1% and animal fibres 1%, with cotton and polyester together “accounting for 90% of our total fibre mix”. The headline claim on cotton is that 86% of consumption came from improved cotton programmes, and the breakdown underneath it is the part worth reading: Better Cotton on a mass balance basis 84%, conventional 14% and organic 2%. Mass balance means the volume is matched through the supply chain rather than the fibre in a particular garment being traceable to a particular farm, so 86% is a claim about the company's buying rather than about the bodysuit. Polyester is 55% regular and 45% recycled to the Global Recycled Standard. The sustainability page dates the certifications that sit behind those words — GOTS scope certification GOTS-11369 through the Soil Association in 2024, Global Recycled Standard scope certification in 2023, FSC Chain of Custody FSC-C168052 in 2022 and FSC certified rubber wellies in 2023 — alongside Better Cotton membership from 2019, foundation membership of the Ethical Trading Initiative and a first organic cotton babywear collection in 2025.

Factory auditing is reported in the same spirit. All active factories were audited against the code of conduct, 85% were rated in categories 1 to 3 and 15% in categories 4 to 6 on a scale where 1 is the lowest risk, and 57% of the non-compliances found were health and safety with 29% working hours. Of 65 suppliers screened, 98% track energy use, 58% have set targets and 51% run a recycling programme, and 72% of tier 1 suppliers are inside the sustainability audit. The modern slavery statement adds 274 tier 2 and tier 3 suppliers mapped, and that the audits are carried out with “the Next Code of Practice team who audit our factories against the Code of Conduct”. That statement says the business has “73 approved Tier 1 factories across five countries” and then lists them as China 44, India 13, Turkey 6, Sri Lanka 4 and Poland 1, which adds up to 68. Five factories are unaccounted for between one sentence and the next in the same document, and nothing on the page reconciles the two.

The hardest number in the report is the estate. It records 38 shops in the UK and two in Ireland and then states the total plainly: “we managed a total of 40 stores, down from 55 in the last reporting period.” Fifteen closures in a year is more than a quarter of the estate, and the report does not attribute it. The modern slavery statement filed on the Home Office registry for the previous year, approved by the board on 5 June 2025 and signed by the same chief executive, puts the company in a turnover band of £36 million to £60 million, which is the only sense of scale published anywhere.

Reloved, and a quarter of what the jumper fetches

The takeback scheme is the part of the sustainability story a customer can actually use, and it was relaunched in August 2025 with thelittleloop running it. There are two routes and they pay differently: through the trade-in tool, “You will receive 30% of the price we can sell the item for”, and through a clear-out bag, “You will receive 25% of the price we can sell the item for”. The credit converts into a voucher to spend at JoJo or with other partner brands, postage is free on ten or more items or with a clear-out bag, and the scheme will take “clean, wearable kids clothes from any brand” rather than only its own. Anything that cannot be sold “will be recycled by our UK recycling partner”.

Accepting other brands' clothes is genuinely more than the scheme needed to do, and a quarter to a third of a resale price is a defensible split for a business that has to grade, photograph, store, list and post each garment. What the impact report then says about the volume is the number to hold onto: “Since launch, over 1200 items have been saved for resale.” Against 1,378 baby lines and 1,206 children's lines currently listed, and a company turning over somewhere between £36 million and £60 million, 1,200 garments in roughly a year is a pilot rather than a channel. The home page advertises it as “Too good for just one childhood”, which is a fair description of the idea and some distance ahead of the throughput.

Two things are not published. The percentage is of “the price we can sell the item for”, and that resale price is set after the garment has been sent in, so a parent cannot work out beforehand what a particular coat will earn. And the page lists no exclusions at all, which reads generously but leaves the condition threshold — what counts as clean and wearable — entirely to the grader. Both of those need confirming with the scheme rather than from the website.

430 people, 14 of them men

The company publishes a statutory gender pay gap report, headed “Gender Pay Gap Report For Year 2025” and signed by Mark Wright, and it describes a workforce shaped like almost no other retailer's. The demographics section, dated as at 5 April 2026, counts 430 employees of whom 416 are women and 14 are men — about 97% — and the report states it in its own words: “Women continue to represent over 95% of our workforce.” The pay quartiles are 93%, 95%, 100% and 99% female, so one of the four quartiles contains no men at all.

What the report does not do is give the figure the exercise exists to produce. Under the headings “Mean Pay Gap” and “Median Pay Gap” it prints a two-bar chart per measure, labelled Mean - April 2025 and Median - April 2025, with 34.13 against 15.35 and 20.73 against 12.00 by gender — and no unit beside any of the four numbers and no percentage anywhere on the page. A reader cannot take a gap off it. The narrative does concede the direction: “The mean figure has further increased in 2025”, “The median figure has remained largely the same as in 2024”, and on bonuses, “both the mean and median bonus gaps have increased”. Bonus participation is the one place the report gives clean arithmetic, and it runs the other way: 247 of 416 women received a bonus, which is 59%, against 3 of 14 men, which is 21%.

With 14 men in a company of 430, a pay gap figure is fragile by construction — a single senior appointment moves it — and the honest reading is that this is a report about where the few men sit rather than about systematic underpayment of women. The report's own headings say as much, with sections called Top pay quartile, Retail representation and Male representation. Set against a B Corp workers score of 20.3, the listed next steps are all process and none of them carries a date: inclusive recruitment, mentoring and development, embedding inclusive behaviours, learning and education, support for parents and career development through new accountability levels and apprenticeships.

What the pages do not say

There is no complaints procedure to be found. The terms direct a dissatisfied customer to “the Complaints Process section of our website”, and the help centre's thirteen sections — Notification, My Account, Placing an Order, Delivery, Returns, Payments, Sale, Promotions & Discounts, Gift Cards, JoJo Reloved, Personalised Items and Gift Boxes, the Loyalty Programme and Other — contain no complaints section at all. No alternative dispute resolution scheme, ombudsman or trade body is named anywhere that could be read on 15 November 2026. Clothing has no statutory ombudsman, so nothing is being withheld that the law requires, but a brand whose own documents name an external auditor for its factories and an external certifier for its ethics names nobody at all for a customer it cannot satisfy.

The company that takes the order is named twice and differently. The terms open with the goods being supplied by Next Retail Limited trading as JoJo Maman Bébé, and the contract clause a few lines later reads “Your order for goods is accepted and a contract is formed between JoJo Maman Bébé and you when we despatch…”. JoJo Maman Bébé is a trading name in the first sentence and the counterparty in the second, and the two are not reconciled. That is a drafting gap rather than a trick, and the practical answer is the registered company number and VAT number printed alongside.

The history has been removed. This is a brand founded in Wales in the early 1990s with a story most retailers would lead on, and the About JoJo page carries no founding year, no founder, no origin, no shop count and no employee numbers — its only historical sentence is “We've been a proud, busy B Corp for some time, too”, against the sustainability page's 2016 and the register's August 2016. The page's own HTML metadata lists the author as Next Retail Limited. The company's registered office moved on 30 April 2026 and now reads Ingate Works, 4 Ingate Place, London SW8 3NS, where the B Corp register still shows Monmouthshire; the register also records the company's first name, from 5 August 1992 to 24 April 1996, as JOJO MAMAM BEBE LIMITED, a misspelling that stood for its first three years and eight months. The accounting reference date was shortened from 30 June to 31 January in 2023, full accounts to 31 January 2026 were filed on 10 September 2026 running to 34 pages, and the confirmation statement made on 5 August 2026 was filed on 25 August — so the filings are current, which is worth saying where it is true.

The one independent score that exists is not flattering. Which?'s shops survey, fielded in July and August 2025 across 2,317 people describing 6,099 experiences, scores brands on satisfaction and likelihood to recommend. JoJo Maman Bébé comes in at 76% on 125 responses against a category average of 80%, thirty-fifth of the 36 baby and child brands rated and above only The Range on 74%. Next, which operates this website and holds 44% of the brand, scores 83% on 276 responses and is one of the seven Which? Recommended Providers in the same table; Amazon is on 81%, John Lewis and Marks & Spencer on 82% each and Ikea on 80%. A survey of satisfaction is not a test of the clothes, and 125 responses is a small sample that Which? itself only publishes above a floor of 30 — but it is the only outside measure of this shop that anybody publishes, and it sits four points below the average of the shops it competes with.

A handful of smaller things could not be established and are worth a question rather than an assumption. The customer reviews and ratings policy, dated 08/09/2025, states that “We do not allow incentivised reviews on our website” and does not say whether a reviewer has to have bought the item. The impact report publishes no employee headcount, no turnover and no like-for-like sales. No scope 3 carbon figure exists. No shop count is printed on any live page on the website, only in the report. And the number of JoJo concessions inside FatFace stores, which have their own 28-day returns rule, is published nowhere.

Where it wins

  • Four sizes below newborn — Premature at 48cm, Extra tiny baby at 50cm and Tiny baby at 52cm before newborn at 56cm — which almost nothing else on a high street offers
  • A certified B Corporation since August 2016 and the first childrenswear brand in Britain to do it, scoring 92.6 against a qualifying 80 and a median of 50.9
  • Collection is free at more than 500 pick-up points with no minimum order, and handing a return back over a counter costs nothing and needs no label
  • The impact report publishes figures most retailers keep private: a fibre mix, factory audit grades, a carbon total down from 236 to 146.41 tonnes, and air freight cut from 22% to 9% of goods shipped
  • Refund timescales are published rather than promised — five working days on a card refund from a shop return, ten from a courier or ParcelShop, one and six on PayPal
  • The £2.50 return charge is per parcel rather than per garment, so four things go back for the same price as one
  • The takeback scheme accepts clean, wearable children's clothes from any brand, not only JoJo's, and pays 30% of the resale price through the trade-in tool
  • 1,378 baby lines and 1,206 children's lines, with a three-pack of cotton footed sleepsuits at £30 working out at £10 each
  • The company keeps its own recall page naming item and batch numbers, with a dedicated line on 0333 777 4529, and files its accounts and confirmation statements on time

Where it falls short

Common questions

How much is delivery, and when is it free?

Next-day delivery to a UK address or to an Evri ParcelShop or locker is £4.95, ordered by 11pm, and free once the order passes £60. Delivery to a collection point is free at any order value, and the home page puts that network at more than 500 shops. Note that the home page footnotes the £60 offer as ending 21/12/2026, excluding personalised items and anything needing two-person delivery, while the help centre article behind it states the same £60 with no end date — so confirm it in the basket before relying on it.

Who am I actually buying from?

The terms, dated 10/11/2026, say the website is operated by and the goods are supplied by Next Retail Limited trading as JoJo Maman Bébé, company 04521150, Desford Road, Enderby, Leicester LE19 4AT, VAT 179 7658 90. The brand company itself is JoJo Maman Bébé Ltd, 02737508, incorporated on 5 August 1992, and its own modern slavery statement gives its shareholders as Foxford Capital L5 DAC at 56% and Next Holdings Ltd at 44%.

How long do I have to send something back, and what does it cost?

Twenty-eight days from delivery on an online order, 30 days on a full-price purchase made in a JoJo shop, 14 days on a sale item bought in a shop, and 28 days on anything bought at a JoJo concession inside a FatFace store. Handing it back over a counter is free. A courier collection, ParcelShop or locker return is £2.50 per parcel however many items are in it, deducted from the refund. Orders placed between 20 September and 24 December 2026 can go back until 17 January 2027.

What sizes does the children's range cover?

The baby chart runs from Premature at 48cm, through Extra tiny baby, Tiny baby and newborn at 56cm, up to 18-24 months at 92cm. The children's chart runs from 12-18 months to 10-12 years at 152cm, but it contains no 7-8 years size, so the step from 6-7 years at 122cm to 8-9 years at 134cm is twice the width of the steps below it. The site's own menus advertise the departments as 2-9 years, which does not match the chart, and the guide warns that JoJo sizes vary slightly from other brands — so measure first.

Is JoJo Maman Bébé really a B Corp, and what does the score mean?

Yes. B Lab's public register lists JoJo Maman Bébé Limited with an overall B Impact Score of 92.6, made up of governance 16.8, workers 20.3, community 25.3, environment 26.1 and customers 3.9. A company needs 80 to certify and the median for businesses that complete the assessment is 50.9. It was first certified in August 2016 and the brand says it is reassessed every three years, though no recertification date is published on the register.

Have any JoJo Maman Bébé products been recalled?

Three recalls involving the brand sit on the government's product safety register. The newest, reference 2510-0179 published on 24 October 2025, covers various children's rompers and trousers whose poppers can detach and create a choking hazard, with the identifiers in a PDF linked from the alert. Before that, reference 2409-0034 of 12 September 2024 covers the Savannah and Urban Garden foam playmats, and 2203-0071 of 1 April 2022 the Bebe au Lait wooden teether rings. Ring 0333 777 4529 for an online purchase or take a shop purchase back to any JoJo shop.

What do I get for sending old clothes back through JoJo Reloved?

Thirty per cent of the price the item resells for if you use the trade-in tool, or 25% if you send a clear-out bag, paid as credit that converts into a voucher to spend at JoJo or with partner brands. Postage is free on ten or more items or with a clear-out bag, the scheme takes clean, wearable children's clothes from any brand, and anything that cannot be sold is passed to a UK recycling partner. The resale price is set after the garment arrives, so the payment cannot be worked out in advance.

Is there an ombudsman if something goes wrong?

None is named. The terms point to a complaints process section of the website, and no such section appears in the help centre's thirteen categories, nor is any alternative dispute resolution scheme, ombudsman or trade body named on any page that could be read on 15 November 2026. Clothing retail has no statutory ombudsman, so nothing legally required is missing, but a dispute that the customer service team cannot settle has no published route beyond the small claims track.

Our verdict

JoJo Maman Bébé sells the expensive end of children's clothing and is open about almost everything except its own history. A £12 bodysuit, a £30 three-pack of sleepsuits and a £36 pramsuit are not supermarket prices, and £4.95 delivery under £60 sharpens that; what you get for it is a range 1,378 lines deep in babywear that starts four sizes below newborn, free collection and free counter returns at more than 500 places, and a company that pays an outside body to score it and then publishes the result. Read the £60 threshold before ordering one thing, measure the child before buying anything above about £40, and check the OPSS register rather than the shop's own notice if a popper recall matters to you. The 92.6 B Corp score is real and unusual, and the part of it about customers is 3.9.
