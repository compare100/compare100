KAYAK Review: What the Free Search Costs You, and Who Gets Paid

Rates and terms checked 15 November 2026 · Cheap Flights · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

KAYAK costs nothing to use. There is no subscription, no membership tier and no KAYAK fee added to a fare, and the company says its results draw on more than 700 provider sites: “We have contractual partnerships with most of the 700+ provider sites that can be seen in our results.” It has been running since 2004, manages seven brands across more than 60 international sites in more than 25 languages, and employs over 800 people from 15 offices.

What KAYAK does not do is sell you anything. Its own explanation of how the site works is unusually blunt about it: “We are not a travel agent, do not sell flight tickets or make reservations, and are not involved in the payment.” The booking is “made with the Travel Provider named on the booking page”, and KAYAK’s website “only acts as a user interface”. That matters before you start rather than after, because it decides whose phone number you ring when a flight moves — the airline’s or the agency’s, never KAYAK’s.

The other thing KAYAK does that most comparison tools do not is write down how its own results are ordered. Its “How KAYAK works” page explains in plain English who pays it, which signals set the default sort, and where money enters the ranking. Very few search tools publish that at all, and having it in the company’s own words is what makes the rest of this page possible.

What the free search buys, and who settles the bill

KAYAK is paid by the companies whose prices it shows. Its own account of the arrangement is one sentence long: when you click through to a deal, “the provider of that deal simply pays us for either redirecting you to their site or helping you to book with them.” On top of that there is display advertising, and KAYAK notes that “Some companies even pay us when you click on an advert that interests you.” Nothing in the terms of use describes a fee KAYAK charges you for using the site.

So the £0 is real, and it is worth being precise about what it covers. The figure beside a flight is the provider’s own price. KAYAK lists the charges that can sit outside it — payment fees, service charges, checked-baggage fees and local taxes — and adds that your own card or bank may apply a currency conversion fee. None of those belong to KAYAK; all of them belong to whoever takes your money.

The scale of that advertising business is visible in the parent company’s accounts rather than KAYAK’s. Booking Holdings reported advertising and other revenues of $332m in the three months to 30 September 2026, up from $308m a year earlier, and $960m across the first nine months of 2026 against $885m. That line covers more than KAYAK, and the group publishes no separate KAYAK revenue figure at all, so treat it as the order of magnitude of the business model rather than a measure of this brand.

Three companies, two continents, and the one the small print names

The footer of KAYAK’s terms names the operator of the site: “Our website is operated by: KAYAK Europe GmbH Fraumünsterstrasse 16 8001 Zürich Switzerland.” The terms themselves then define “we” as KAYAK Software Corporation, the Stamford, Connecticut company behind the brand. Those are two different legal entities in two different countries, and the document uses both.

The choice of law follows the American one. The terms say that “the laws of the State of Connecticut (USA), without regard to its conflict of laws rules, will govern these Terms”, and that legal actions are to be filed “only in the state and federal courts located in New Haven, Connecticut (USA)”, while acknowledging that a consumer may be able to bring proceedings where they live. The document also contains an arbitration clause, flagged rather than set out. For data protection, KAYAK names Momondo A/S in Copenhagen as its EU representative and gives a postal address in Stamford for its data protection officer.

There is a British company in the structure, and it is easy to miss because the paperwork does not point at it. KAYAK Software (UK) Limited is company 06076185, incorporated on 31 January 2007, active, registered at The Monument Building, 11 Monument Street, London EC3R 8AF under SIC code 82990, with its last accounts made up to 31 December 2024. That address is the same London office KAYAK lists on its own about page, so the two tie up neatly. What the UK company is not is the party you contract with when you search, and nothing on the consumer site suggests it is.

One small inconsistency is worth noting because it is the sort of thing that makes a reader check the rest. The about page gives KAYAK’s Zürich office as Claridenstrasse 25, 8002 Zürich, while the operator notice in the terms gives Fraumünsterstrasse 16, 8001 Zürich. Offices move and legal notices lag behind; the point is simply that the two addresses are on the same company’s own pages and neither carries a date.

KAYAK publishes how its results are ordered, including the part money plays

Start with the flight list. KAYAK says that “by default flights are ranked to give you the best trade-off between price and convenience”, which is the sort of thing every search tool says. The interesting sentence comes a step later, once you have chosen a flight and are looking at the different places that will sell it to you: “the order of the other booking options will be based mainly on click-through-rate and cost per click.” That is a ranking of sellers by how much they pay and how often people click, stated by the company doing the ranking.

Hotels are ordered on a wider set of signals. The default “Recommended” sort “is based on a few key parameters: price, guest ratings and its popularity (measured in clicks)”, and then: “We also factor in the average revenue potential for us from each hotel result.” Two further sentences from the same page are worth having in front of you. On presentation: “For visual simplicity, we always round up the price to the next whole number.” On what you are shown: “Our algorithms may obfuscate one or several offers if they are not the solo cheapest.” And on prominence: “Providers can also become highlighted if they pay more for the clicks they get.”

None of that is hidden, and that is genuinely to KAYAK’s credit — the great majority of comparison sites describe their ranking as a secret sauce and leave it there. It does change how the results should be read. The top booking option for a flight is the one that performs commercially, which may or may not be the one with the service, the baggage allowance or the change policy you want, and a few pounds of difference between sellers can be worth the scroll.

There is a regulatory backdrop to exactly this question, and KAYAK is inside it rather than outside. On 13 September 2019 the Competition and Markets Authority announced that 25 firms had signed its sector-wide principles for complying with consumer protection law, after an investigation into “pressure selling, misleading discount claims and the effect that commission has on how hotels are ordered on sites”. Kayak and momondo are both on that list of 25, alongside Skyscanner, TripAdvisor, Airbnb, Google and Trip.com; a separate six — Booking.com, Expedia, Agoda, Hotels.com, ebookers and trivago — were subject to formal enforcement. The commitments included “always displaying the full cost of a room upfront”. Being a signatory is not a finding against anybody, and it means KAYAK took on a published standard on ordering and pricing seven years ago.

A Hacker Fare is two tickets, and that is the whole point of it

Hacker Fares are KAYAK’s own name for a return built out of two one-way tickets, often on different airlines. The aim, in KAYAK’s words, is “to find you cheaper prices and more flight time options than you’d get with a single return ticket”, and on plenty of routes it does both. If one carrier is cheap outbound and another cheap coming back, a through fare cannot match the pair.

What you are buying, though, is two contracts rather than one. KAYAK is explicit: “Each direction is its own booking with its own confirmation number”, “The tickets may be issued by different airlines or providers”, and each leg generates its own confirmation email. Because the tickets are independent, if the outbound is cancelled or retimed the second airline “isn’t required to rebook you” on its own ticket. Baggage is separate too — “Each airline has its own baggage policies and fees” — and so are the cancellation rules, where “What applies to your outbound flight may not apply to your return.”

KAYAK’s own advice when the second leg disappears after you have booked the first is to search for a one-way for that leg, or to “Contact the provider you used for the first booking to explore cancellation options.” It also warns, on its pricing help page, that “Because fares change frequently, please check that both of the listed fares are still available before proceeding.” That is a reasonable set of instructions and it is still a transfer of risk from the airlines to you. On a weekend city break with hand luggage only, the saving is usually worth it. On a long-haul trip with a tight connection, hold baggage and a non-refundable hotel at the other end, the arithmetic is different, and a single ticket with one airline responsible for the whole journey earns its premium.

The seven-day forecast, and the careful words around it

KAYAK’s Price Forecast tells you whether a fare for your dates looks likely to rise or fall over “the next seven days (or between your search and departure date whichever is shortest)”. It is built from the company’s own search traffic — “Travellers perform billions of requests for travel information on KAYAK’s websites and mobile apps each year”, with the forecast page putting it at over a billion requests annually — and KAYAK says its “scientists develop these flight price trend forecasts using algorithms and mathematical models”. The company also states that it checks the predictions daily, tracking flights across the forecast window and verifying whether earlier calls were right.

The claims made for it are modest, and they are modest in a specific way. The buy-now recommendation is described as “generally correct more often than not”. The disclaimer is that “Predictions based on past history can never be perfect, so we can’t guarantee they’ll be correct.” And the first piece of advice on KAYAK’s price trend page is not to lean on the forecast at all: “our first recommendation is that if you see a good price on a route, book it.”

What is missing is a number. KAYAK displays a confidence level and tells you to “Check the confidence level displayed”, but publishes no scale for it, no sample, no methodology document and no accuracy rate — so there is nothing to tell you whether high confidence means seven times in ten or nine. A tool that measures its own accuracy daily and does not publish the result is asking to be taken on trust. Use the forecast as a tie-breaker between two acceptable fares, not as a reason to wait.

Why the price can move between the list and the checkout

This is the complaint every metasearch attracts, and KAYAK answers it in more detail than most. It “does not set prices”, it “compiles data from many sites”, and it “does not guarantee prices”. Two mechanics explain most of the gap. First, speed: “we cache results in order to keep site speed up”, so what you see is a stored answer rather than a live one. Second, refresh rate: “At the very least, provider sites should give us an updated inventory of prices every 24 hours” — a floor set by KAYAK on its partners, not a promise of live pricing.

On top of that sits ordinary competition for the same seat. KAYAK notes that “Multiple people may try to book the same option at once, so some prices may no longer be available when you click through”, and that “Occasionally, problems in the price and availability data from travel sites can lead to mismatches.” There is a “Report an inaccurate price” form, which is a real remedy and worth using, because a provider feeding KAYAK stale prices is a problem KAYAK can act on and you cannot.

Across hotels the dataset is large — “over 5 million hotels and properties” plus third-party sites — which is part of why a daily refresh floor is the practical answer rather than a live feed. The sensible way to use any of it is to treat the KAYAK figure as a shortlisting tool and the provider’s own checkout as the price, and to re-check the total after baggage, seats and card fees are added rather than before.

What Booking Holdings has told its investors about KAYAK

KAYAK stopped being independent on 21 May 2013, when what was then priceline.com completed the purchase for “approximately $522.4 million in cash” plus “approximately 1,519,717 shares of common stock”, assuming KAYAK’s outstanding options as well. The deal announcement said KAYAK “will continue to operate as an independent brand within The Priceline Group”, and it still runs that way: KAYAK describes itself as part of Booking Holdings Inc., whose other platforms include Booking.com, Priceline, Agoda, Rentalcars.com and OpenTable.

The parent is in good health. For the three months to 30 September 2026 Booking Holdings reported gross bookings of $54.5 billion, up 10% year on year, 345 million room nights, up 7%, total revenues of $8.9 billion, down 1%, and net income of $2,717 million. Those are the numbers behind the search box, and they are a reasonable answer to anyone wondering whether a free tool will still be there next year.

The figures for KAYAK specifically are less comfortable, and they are published by the parent rather than the brand. At its annual goodwill test as of 30 September 2025, Booking Holdings “recognized an impairment charge of $180 million” against KAYAK’s goodwill, leaving “an adjusted carrying value of $203 million at September 30, 2025”, and a further “impairment charge of $277 million” against “the KAYAK asset group’s intangible assets (trade names and supply and distribution agreements)”, after which those were valued at $103 million and $76 million. That is $457 million written off one brand in one quarter. The stated reason is specific: “The impairments were primarily driven by a reduction in the forecasted cash flows for KAYAK, reflecting its meta-search business being impacted by expected increases in customer acquisition costs”, and the management discussion adds, flatly, “we assume that KAYAK will experience a decline in profitability.”

Read that next to the ranking disclosure above and the two halves fit together. A business that earns per click and pays more each year to acquire the customer who clicks is a business under pressure on exactly the mechanism it has published. Nothing on KAYAK’s own site mentions the write-down, which is normal for a consumer brand and still the kind of thing worth knowing. It does not make the search any less useful today; it is a reason to expect the commercial signals in the results to get stronger rather than weaker.

Nobody independent rates a flight search engine

Worth saying plainly, because the absence is easy to mistake for an oversight. Defaqto rates insurance products. Ofcom reaches communications providers. The national tourist board schemes assess the hotel or hostel you stay in. None of them assesses a travel search engine, and there is no ratings agency anywhere that does, so any claim you see about a metasearch being independently rated is worth looking at twice. What KAYAK has instead is its own review system — guest reviews converted to a 1 to 10 scale for flights and cars, with the company noting it “may stop showing reviews once they are three years old” — plus the published mechanics above and its 2019 CMA undertaking.

For the market a UK reader is actually in, the Civil Aviation Authority’s consumer survey of 9 February 2026 is the best free source there is. It found 62% of consumers had flown in the previous 12 months and 31% planning to fly more, rising to 72% of 18 to 34 year olds who had flown and 47% planning more, against 29% of 35 to 54 year olds and 22% of the over-55s, while 55% expected no change. Overall satisfaction with flying reached 88%, which the CAA called its highest level since 2016, and satisfaction with value for money hit 76%, the highest since 2021. Satisfaction with complaints handling rose 10 points to 72%, with 15% of consumers complaining after disruption.

The same release is honest about who is being left behind: 42% of disabled passengers had flown, and satisfaction fell to 82% among consumers with disability or accessibility needs and 80% among those who are not digitally confident or financially vulnerable, while 36% of non-flyers gave financial limitations as the main reason. Three quarters said they were concerned about the environment, but only 32% thought reducing the environmental impact of flying should be the industry’s top priority. Two caveats go with those figures: the CAA release gives no sample size and no fieldwork dates, and it says nothing at all about how people research or book, so none of it is evidence about search tools either way.

Common questions

Does KAYAK charge anything to use?

No. There is no subscription and no KAYAK booking fee, and nothing in its terms of use describes a charge for using the site. KAYAK is paid by the companies in its results: the provider “pays us for either redirecting you to their site or helping you to book with them”, and some advertisers pay per click. The price beside a flight is the provider’s own, and payment fees, service charges, baggage fees, local taxes and any card currency conversion sit outside it.

If my flight is cancelled, who do I contact?

The company named on the booking page, not KAYAK. Its terms say the booking is “made with the Travel Provider named on the booking page” and that the website “only acts as a user interface”, with disputes about a booking to be resolved with the provider. KAYAK also says it “does not handle the fulfilment of the travel product you booked”, so refunds and rebooking are the airline’s or the agency’s to do. Your statutory rights as an air passenger sit against the airline in the same way.

What is a Hacker Fare, and is it safe?

It is a return assembled from two one-way tickets, often on different airlines, to beat the price or the timings of a single return. KAYAK is clear that “Each direction is its own booking with its own confirmation number” and that the tickets are independent, so if the first leg is cancelled the second airline “isn’t required to rebook you”. Baggage policies and cancellation rules can differ between the two. It is a sound way to save on a short trip with hand luggage, and a worse bet on a long-haul itinerary with hold bags and a fixed hotel.

Why is the price different when I click through?

Mostly caching and refresh rates. KAYAK “does not set prices”, caches results “in order to keep site speed up”, and asks providers for “an updated inventory of prices every 24 hours” as a minimum, so a listed figure can be hours old. Fares also sell out while several people are booking the same option, and KAYAK notes that data problems can cause mismatches. It states plainly that it “does not guarantee prices”, and offers a “Report an inaccurate price” form when a provider is persistently out of date.

Can KAYAK’s price forecast tell me when to book?

Only loosely. The forecast covers “the next seven days (or between your search and departure date whichever is shortest)”, is built from KAYAK’s own search traffic of more than a billion requests a year, and is checked daily for accuracy — but no accuracy rate, sample or methodology is published, and the confidence level shown has no published scale. KAYAK describes its buy-now call as “generally correct more often than not” and warns that predictions “can never be perfect”. Its own first piece of advice is that if you see a good price, you should book it.

Who owns KAYAK?

Booking Holdings Inc., which also owns Booking.com, Priceline, Agoda, Rentalcars.com and OpenTable. The purchase completed on 21 May 2013, when priceline.com paid “approximately $522.4 million in cash” plus about 1,519,717 shares. KAYAK still runs as a separate brand and manages six others — SWOODOO, checkfelix, momondo, Cheapflights, HotelsCombined and Mundi. At 30 September 2025 the parent wrote $457 million off KAYAK’s goodwill and intangible assets, citing “expected increases in customer acquisition costs”.

Our verdict

KAYAK is a good free shortlisting tool that is honest about being a shortlisting tool. It takes nothing from you, it draws on more than 700 provider sites, and it says in its own words that it sells no tickets and is not involved in the payment — which is the single most useful fact on this page, because it tells you that the airline or agency on the booking screen is the party with your money and your obligations. Its ranking disclosure deserves more credit than it gets: very few comparison sites will tell you that sellers are ordered mainly on click-through rate and cost per click, that paying more buys prominence, or that an offer may be hidden when it is not the cheapest. Read with that in mind, the results are a reliable map of the market rather than a ranked recommendation, and the scroll past the top line is usually worth a few pounds. Treat the seven-day forecast as a tie-breaker rather than advice, since no accuracy figure for it exists; check a Hacker Fare twice if you are carrying bags or have a hotel booked at the other end; and take the quoted price to the provider’s own checkout before you believe the total. Booking Holdings writing $457 million off the brand in 2025 while telling investors to expect lower profitability does not make today’s search worse, but it does suggest the commercial signals inside these results are more likely to sharpen than soften.
