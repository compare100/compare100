Le Boat UK: Thames and Caledonian Canal Cruisers, Prices and the Money You Leave at the Base

Rates and terms checked 15 November 2026 · UK Boating Holidays · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

£1,079 buys seven nights in a two-cabin Comfort boat, which is the starting price Le Boat published on its own cost guide on 3 September 2026. The firm hires out river cruisers rather than narrowboats, and in Britain it does so from three bases: Benson in Oxfordshire and London (Chertsey) on the Thames, and Laggan on the Caledonian Canal. No boat licence is needed and a pre-departure briefing is included.

Those three bases sit inside a much bigger operation. Le Boat lists 18 destinations across nine countries and more than 900 boats in 43 named models, sleeping between two and twelve, split into Value, Comfort and Premier ranges. The business traces itself to 1969, when Michael Streat founded Blue Line with eight boats; the Le Boat name was created in 2007 by merging Crown Blue Line, Connoisseur and Emerald Star. The contracting company is Crown Travel Limited, registered number 02095375 at Platinum House, St Marks Hill, Surbiton, and it is a member of ABTA under number V6650.

Two dates decide whether a British trip is possible at all. Boats are available to hire between April and October only, and Scottish Canals closes the Caledonian Canal to sea-to-sea transit from 4pm on Sunday 1 November 2026 until 9am on Wednesday 24 March 2027. The minimum party is two adults aged 18 or over, or three on the Vision boats, so a solo week is not on offer.

Three British bases inside an eighteen-destination network

Le Boat is a continental business with a British corner. Nine of its 18 destinations are in France, with others in Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium, Italy, Ireland and Canada. Britain accounts for two of them — the Thames and Loch Ness with the Caledonian Canal — worked from three bases. That matters for a reader comparing the category, because the boat that arrives is a glass-and-fibreglass river cruiser with two to five cabins and an en-suite count to match, not a 7ft-beam steel narrowboat.

The fleet page names 43 models and groups them as Value (20 models), Comfort (17) and Premier (6). The published from-prices on that page are short-break prices rather than weekly ones: a three-berth Town Star starts at £189 for four nights, a six-berth Countess at £289 for three, and the twelve-berth Horizon 5 at £819 for three. Three models — Capri WHS, Cygnet WHS and Kingfisher WHS — carry a suffix the fleet page does not explain, and Le Boat also sells a “Mystery boat” from the Value range at £229 for two nights with the actual model confirmed only at check-in.

The corporate history is worth three sentences because the dates do not line up the way the marketing suggests. The brand dates its expertise to 1969 and Michael Streat's eight boats; the Le Boat name itself was assembled in 2007 out of Crown Blue Line, Connoisseur and Emerald Star, with Emerald Star retained in Ireland; and the company that takes the booking, Crown Travel Limited, was incorporated on 2 February 1987 under the name Contactarrow Limited, taking its present name 23 days later. Its registered activity at Companies House is 79110, travel agency activities, which is a more accurate description of the contract than “boat hire” is. Travelopia Holdings Limited has held three quarters or more of the shares since 6 April 2016.

What £1,079 covers, and what the engine hour adds to it

Le Boat prices per boat, not per person, which is the single most useful thing about the way it sells. The published figure covers hire of the boat and its equipment, the briefing before departure and daily technical support, plus the compulsory mooring fees in Scotland, Canada and Italy. Travel to the base, personal travel insurance, food, marina electricity and showers, and lock fees in Ireland and the Netherlands all sit outside it.

So does fuel, and fuel is the item that moves the total most. Le Boat does not sell the week with a tank of diesel in it; it meters the engine and bills at the end, at a rate its extras page puts at about £14 to £28 an hour. The company also publishes the number that turns that rate into money: its customers ran their engines for an average of 27 hours on a seven-night holiday in 2025. At its own rate band, that is £378 to £756 of diesel on top of the hire price. A fuel deposit is taken at the base first — £350 for up to six nights, £450 for seven and £525 for eight or more — and refunded or topped up when the boat comes back.

Put the published figures together for a week on the Caledonian Canal and the shape of the bill is clear: £1,079 for the boat, £113 of compulsory Scottish mooring, £378 to £756 of fuel at the company's own average engine use, and £182 upwards for the damage waiver dealt with below. A little over £1,750 is the realistic floor, and that is before a £52.50 weekly WiFi unit, a £49 bike, a £49 barbecue, secure parking at £49 a week, £125 to £200 for the end-of-hire cleaning service or £80 for each of up to two dogs. Early check-in and late check-out are £100 to £120 each.

The deposit left at the base is a four-figure number

This is the part of the booking a first-time hirer is least likely to have budgeted for. For 2026 departures without Damage Risk Reduction, the deposit payable at a UK base is £2,445 on a Value boat and runs up to £3,835 on a Luxury one. It is taken on a card at the base, and the booking conditions are at least straightforward about the ceiling: Le Boat says the deposit “will be retained (in part or in full)” against damage and that the hirer “will not have to pay more than the initial amount collected”.

Damage Risk Reduction is the way out of it. Buying the waiver cuts the Value deposit to £240 for 2026 departures and £250 for 2027, and on the dearest boats to £970 for a 2027 Liberty and £1,000 for 2028. The waiver is non-refundable and priced by the day: £26 to £28 in the cheapest of four boat groups for 2027, through £40 to £43 and £51 to £57, to £54 to £58 in the dearest, with every band a pound or two higher for 2028. Across seven nights that is £182 to £196 at the bottom and £378 to £406 at the top.

What Le Boat does not publish anywhere on the extras page is which boat model sits in which of the four groups. A reader working out what a week on a Vision 4 from Benson will cost can find the deposit by range and the waiver by group, but not the bridge between them, so the number can only be established by taking a quote. That is a documentation gap rather than a charge, and it is a fair thing to say about an otherwise unusually detailed price page — few operators in this category publish a four-year deposit table at all.

On the Thames the day is set by the lock keeper's shift

The non-tidal Thames is run by the Environment Agency, and its lock keepers' hours of duty are what a cruising day has to fit inside. The published table runs 9.15am to 4pm in March and November, 9am to 5pm in April and October, 9am to 6pm in May, June and September and 9am to 6.30pm in July and August, with locks closed between 1pm and 2pm for lunch throughout. Outside those hours a crew may work a lock itself, at its own risk — which on a hire cruiser with two nominated adults aboard is a different proposition from a lock keeper doing it.

Le Boat's own Thames rules narrow the water further. Navigation after dark is forbidden, the speed limit is 5mph, and cruising beyond Teddington Lock towards central London is prohibited outright, so the famous end of the river is not available from Chertsey. Downstream boats take priority at bridges and on sharp bends, non-slip footwear is required and flip-flops are specifically excluded.

The route card is honest about how slow river cruising is. Benson out and back by way of Oxford and Henley is 186km and 30 locks across seven nights, which Le Boat costs at 24 hours of cruising; Benson to London (Chertsey) is 96km and 22 locks over five nights and 15 hours; the Chertsey three-night loop to Henley-upon-Thames is 75km, 18 locks and 16 hours. The company suggests an average of two to four hours a day, and on those itineraries that is what the lock count allows.

Scotland: twenty-nine locks, a school-run swing bridge and a winter gate

The Caledonian Canal is the more unusual of the two British products, and Scottish Canals publishes the hard numbers. The waterway is roughly 60 miles — 50 nautical miles — of which 22 miles are man-made and the rest is the natural chain of Lochy, Oich and Ness. There are 29 locks in all, the summit at Loch Oich stands at 106ft (32.31m), and the navigation authority puts the minimum sea-to-sea transit at two and a half to three days before weather or traffic. Le Boat's own Laggan itineraries are shorter: 34km and eight locks over three nights, or 186km and ten locks across seven nights and 25 hours of cruising.

Lock and bridge operating hours are seasonal and manned: 9am to 5.30pm from 1 April to 24 May, 8.30am to 6pm from 25 May to 30 August, and 9am to 5.30pm from 31 August to 1 November, seven days a week, with a boat expected to arrive in time for the operation to finish before closing. The swing bridges keep road hours rather than river ones. Banavie at Fort William will not open Monday to Friday between 8.30am and 9am all year, with further closures for the afternoon school run in term time, and Muirtown at Inverness is shut to boats from 8.15am to 9.20am, 12.10pm to 1.20pm and 4.25pm to 5.45pm on weekdays. Lifejackets must be worn in the locks, which are themselves closed for an hour at lunch, and Seaport Marina works VHF channel 74.

Then the canal shuts. Scottish Canals closes it to sea-to-sea transit from 4pm on 1 November 2026 to 9am on 24 March 2027, with local movements possible only by prior agreement and at least two working days' notice. Le Boat's April-to-October hire season sits neatly inside that, so the restriction costs a holidaymaker nothing — but a reader shopping for a February break in the Highlands should know there is no boat to hire.

An ABTA membership that covers conduct, not your money

Le Boat is a member of ABTA under number V6650, and it says so plainly on its own pages. The membership is real and it is worth something: ABTA's Code of Conduct, issued in September 2026, binds members at clause 5F to allow a dispute over an alleged breach of contract or negligence to go to the association's arbitration scheme, and at clause 5G to comply with the dispute resolution schemes ABTA offers. Le Boat's complaints route follows that — raise it at the base first, write to customerservice@leboat.com within 28 days of getting home, then use ABTA's follow-up procedure and its scheme for the resolution of disputes, which is approved by the Chartered Trading Standards Institute.

What the membership does not do is protect the money. The booking conditions state at clause 2a that “as you are booking arrangements other than a package holiday” the arrangements “will not be financially protected”, and the ABTA notice on the same page says in terms that “the travel arrangements you buy from us aren't covered by ABTA's scheme of financial protection”. A boat hire with no flight and no second travel service attached is not a package under the Package Travel Regulations, so there is no ATOL and no bonding behind the 50% a customer pays months in advance.

Le Boat is complying with the rules rather than dodging them — clause 2C of that same ABTA Code requires a member to inform clients about any financial protection arrangements that apply to money it receives, and disclosing that there are none is exactly what the clause asks for. The practical conclusion for a reader is simply that the credit card, not the trade body, is the backstop. Set against that, the liability cap in clause 9c is better than the category norm: three times the cost of the travel arrangements, rather than the price paid.

Two clocks that both run out ninety days before departure

The payment terms are front-loaded. A booking takes a 50% deposit, and the balance falls due at least 90 days before departure. Cancellation is where the terms are hardest: 90 days or more out costs 50% of the total holiday cost, described in the conditions as the loss of the full deposit, and 89 days or less costs 100% of it. There is no sliding scale between the two. The Holiday Flex Plan at £49 for a short break and £139 for a week or more exists to soften that, and travel insurance is the hirer's own responsibility — Le Boat does not sell it.

The same 90-day line governs the Lowest Price Guarantee, which is the detail most worth noticing. For bookings confirmed between 29 April and 16 November 2026 for 2027 departures, a customer can claim the difference if the identical boat, date, duration and route turns up cheaper in the same currency — but only “up to 90 days before your departure date”, and the terms add that “if the price decreases within 89 days of departure, you are not entitled to make a claim”. A bigger percentage discount does not qualify unless the total boat price is lower, extras are excluded, and the cheaper boat must still be bookable on the day of the claim. Validated claims are refunded within 30 days.

So the last 89 days before a Le Boat holiday are a closed room: the customer cannot claim a price drop and cannot cancel for anything less than the whole cost. If Le Boat itself cancels, clause 7d offers a full refund or an alternative holiday of comparable standard, with compensation under clause 7e running from nil at more than 90 days' notice to £20 to £80 per boat as departure gets closer.

A 2024 document and a 2026 one quoting different fuel rates

Two of Le Boat's own pages no longer agree about the cost of diesel, and the reason is the date on each. The booking terms and conditions, last updated on 17 December 2024, estimate the fuel charge at £12 to £23 an hour and the fuel deposit at £260 to £475 for a UK booking. The live extras page and the company's own cost article of 3 September 2026 both give £14 to £28 an hour and deposits of £350, £450 and £525. The newer pair is plainly the current figure and the older one has simply not been revisited — but the terms are the contractual document, and a hirer who budgets from the contract will be out by up to £5 an hour.

The same mild drift shows elsewhere. One Le Boat page claims “over 50 years of experience” and another “over 55 years” in the same navigation, while the about page records a 50th birthday celebrated in 2019 — all three consistent with a 1969 start, and none of them wrong, just written at different times and never squared. On 15 November 2026 the Thames page advertised “Holidays from £819” under a promotion closing the following day, without stating the boat or the number of nights behind it; the same £819 appears on the fleet page as a three-night price for the twelve-berth Horizon 5, which may or may not be the same offer.

None of this is a reason to walk away from the product. It is a reason to take the quote as the price and the extras page as the rate card, and to treat the figures printed in the 2024 terms as history.

Where it wins

  • Prices are per boat rather than per person, so a family of six on a £1,079 week pays the same as a couple
  • River cruisers with up to five en-suite cabins and berths for twelve, which is a different product from the narrowboats most of this category hires
  • No boat licence is required and a pre-departure briefing plus daily technical support are included in the price
  • Three British bases — Benson, London (Chertsey) and Laggan — give access to both the non-tidal Thames and the Caledonian Canal
  • The extras page publishes damage deposits and waiver rates for 2026, 2027 and 2028, which is more forward pricing than most operators in this category show
  • The liability cap is three times the cost of the travel arrangements, which is more generous than the price-paid cap common elsewhere
  • ABTA membership V6650 brings the Code of Conduct and an arbitration scheme approved by the Chartered Trading Standards Institute
  • Compulsory Scottish mooring fees are built into the quoted price rather than collected at the pontoon

  • Cancelling 89 days or fewer before departure costs 100% of the total holiday cost, and cancelling earlier still costs 50% — there is no sliding scale in between
  • The damage deposit at a UK base is £2,445 on a Value boat and up to £3,835 on a Luxury one unless the non-refundable waiver is bought, which is itself £182 to £406 for seven nights
  • Fuel is metered by the engine hour at about £14 to £28, so the week's real cost is unknown at booking — £378 to £756 at the company's own 2025 average of 27 hours
  • The booking conditions state the arrangements are not a package and “will not be financially protected”, and ABTA's financial protection scheme does not cover them either
  • Le Boat publishes the damage waiver by boat group and the deposit by boat range, but nowhere maps a model to a group, so the cost cannot be worked out before taking a quote

Common questions

Where does Le Boat operate in the UK?

From three bases. Benson in Oxfordshire and London (Chertsey) serve the non-tidal Thames, and Laggan, at the top of Laggan Locks, serves the Caledonian Canal and Loch Ness. They are two of Le Boat's 18 destinations; the other 16 are in France, Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium, Italy, Ireland and Canada.

How much does a week on a Le Boat cruiser cost?

Le Boat's own cost guide of 3 September 2026 puts Comfort boats from £1,079 for seven nights in a two-cabin boat before offers. Fuel is extra and metered: at the published £14 to £28 an hour and the company's 2025 average of 27 engine hours a week, that is another £378 to £756. A Scottish week adds £113 of compulsory mooring. Short-break from-prices on the fleet page start at £189 for four nights on a three-berth Town Star.

Why is the damage deposit so large, and can it be reduced?

Without the Damage Risk Reduction waiver, the deposit taken at a UK base for 2026 departures is £2,445 on a Value boat and up to £3,835 on a Luxury one. Buying the waiver cuts that to £240 for 2026 and £250 for 2027 on a Value boat. The waiver is non-refundable and charged daily — £26 to £28 a day in the cheapest of four boat groups for 2027, up to £54 to £58 in the dearest — so £182 to £406 across seven nights. Le Boat does not publish which model falls in which group.

Do I need a licence or any experience?

No licence is required. The hirer must be 18 or over, and a minimum of two adults must be on board — three on the Vision boats. A Captain and a second-in-charge have to be nominated, and a briefing is given before departure. On the Caledonian Canal lifejackets must be worn in the locks; on the Thames navigation after dark is forbidden, the speed limit is 5mph and cruising beyond Teddington Lock is not allowed.

What happens if I cancel?

Cancelling 90 days or more before departure costs 50% of the total holiday cost, which the conditions describe as the loss of the full deposit. Cancelling 89 days or fewer before departure costs 100%. The Holiday Flex Plan, at £49 for a short break or £139 for a week or more, is sold as the way to soften that. Le Boat does not provide travel insurance, so cancellation cover has to be bought separately.

Is my money protected if Le Boat stops trading?

Not by a bonding scheme. Clause 2a of the booking conditions states that because the customer is “booking arrangements other than a package holiday”, the arrangements “will not be financially protected”, and the ABTA notice on the same page confirms the arrangements are not covered by ABTA's scheme of financial protection. Le Boat's ABTA membership, number V6650, brings the Code of Conduct and the arbitration scheme instead. Payment by credit card is the practical fallback.

When can I hire, and is the Caledonian Canal open all year?

Boats are available between April and October. Scottish Canals closes the Caledonian Canal to sea-to-sea transit from 4pm on 1 November 2026 until 9am on 24 March 2027, with local movements only by prior agreement and at least two working days' notice. Lock and bridge hours are seasonal: 9am to 5.30pm from 1 April to 24 May, 8.30am to 6pm from 25 May to 30 August, and 9am to 5.30pm from 31 August to 1 November.

Our verdict

Le Boat is the cruiser option in a category dominated by narrowboats, and on the Thames and the Caledonian Canal it is selling something genuinely different: four or five en-suite cabins, a per-boat price starting at £1,079 for seven nights, and no licence needed to take it out. Read the extras page before booking rather than after. The hire price is a boat price, and the week's real cost is £1,079 plus £378 to £756 of metered diesel, £113 of compulsory Scottish mooring and £182 upwards for the damage waiver that keeps a £2,445 deposit off the card — comfortably over £1,750 for a Laggan week before a bike or a barbecue. The two terms that need a decision rather than a glance are the cancellation scale, which is total inside 89 days, and the clause confirming the booking is not a financially protected package. Neither is hidden; both are in Le Boat's own documents, which is more than can be said for several operators in this category.
