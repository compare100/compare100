L&G Personal Pension Review: Charges, Cash and the Workplace Gap

Rates and terms checked 15 November 2026 · Pensions · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Legal & General's Personal Pension costs 0.56% a year: a 0.25% service charge plus a 0.31% fund management charge, which is £5.60 on every £1,000 invested. You can open one with £100 and add £1 at a time after that. Every figure here was checked on 15 November 2026 against L&G's own key features document, reference Q0059826 04/26, its terms and conditions Q0059984 06/25, and the two Personal Pension pages on legalandgeneral.com.

There is a lot of weight behind it. L&G's half-year results of 5 August 2026 put £1.2 trillion of assets under management across the group, UK defined contribution assets up 23% to £236bn, workplace defined contribution assets under administration up 27% to £128bn, and workplace net flows of £6.2bn to the end of July. Core operating profit was £918m, up 7%, on a Solvency II coverage ratio of 201% against the group's own 160% to 190% operating target. L&G's Independent Governance Committee, which scrutinises the workplace book on behalf of 2.7 million members, rated the firm Excellent for costs and charges and Excellent for member service in its report for 2025.

Two things are worth knowing before you click. The plan is provided by Legal & General (Portfolio Management Services) Limited, company 02457525, Financial Services Register number 146786 — the group's investment company, not the insurer that writes its life policies, which matters for compensation limits later on. And it is a deliberately narrow product: five ready-made funds graded by risk, a Life-Stage default that moves your money as you approach your chosen retirement age, or the self-select route, which L&G describes as picking a single fund from its wider range. None of L&G's own pages for this plan offers individual shares, exchange-traded funds or investment trusts, so a reader shopping for a full self-invested pension should look at that first.

Two charges, two companies, and a third cost nobody totals

The 0.25% service charge is L&G's own, and the terms are unusually clear about it: it covers setting up and administering the plan, it is worked out daily and collected monthly, and it is “taken from any uninvested cash that's available or otherwise by selling units in the Fund you're invested in”. It keeps running until you draw all the benefits or transfer away. On a £50,000 pot that is £125 a year before anything the fund charges.

The 0.31% is not L&G's to set in the same way. It is the fund management charge, levied by the fund manager and shown inside the ongoing charges figure, and the key features document says plainly that it “differs from Fund to Fund”. The 0.31% both consumer pages print is a single worked example: the Multi-Index 3 fund. Put the two together and you get the 0.56% that L&G itself converts into “around £5.60 in fees over a year” on £1,000, or £280 a year on £50,000.

Two things sit outside that sum. The first is transaction costs — the terms say that “in addition to the OCF, transaction costs may also impact Fund values”, and no total for them appears anywhere on the product's own pages. The second is the date. The key features document is dated April 2026 on its cover and carries the reference Q0059826 04/26 LG007720, yet it sources the 0.31% as “an FMC of 0.31% (as at May 2024)”. The two consumer pages reprint 0.31% with no date at all. L&G's own Multi-Index fund factsheet pages returned a server error on two attempts on 15 November 2026, so the current figure could not be read and is not stated here. The honest position is that the headline charge rests on a figure L&G last dated two years before the document it appears in.

What L&G does deserve credit for is publishing both halves at all. Its own guide to pension charges, dated 11 December 2025, says “8 in 10 people have no idea what they're paying in pension fees and charges”, names ten different charge types from inactivity fees to contribution charges, and gives figures for almost none of them. A provider that prints 0.25% and 0.31% side by side and then does the £1,000 sum for you is doing more than most.

The same company charges a workplace member a fraction of this

L&G is overwhelmingly a workplace pension business — £128bn of workplace defined contribution assets against a retail arm the group results do not separate out — and because every workplace provider must fund an Independent Governance Committee, the charges its workplace members pay are published every year in detail. The appendix to the 2025 report, covering the year to 31 December 2025, sets them out fund by fund.

On Generation 3, the current WorkSave platform, the illustrations give a lowest member annual management charge of 0.02% to 0.03%, a median of 0.11% to 0.18% depending on the fund, and a highest of 0.37%. The maximum scheme charge is 0.37% on Generation 3, 0.27% on Generation 25 and 0.50% on Generation 17, the old Group Stakeholder book. The report adds that “nearly three quarters of members pay between 0% and 0.50%”.

Set 0.56% against those numbers and the arithmetic is this page's own: the direct buyer pays roughly three to five times the median Generation 3 workplace member, and about half again as much as the member in the single dearest Generation 3 scheme. That is not a scandal and it is not hidden — an employer buys in bulk and a private individual does not, and L&G is one of the few providers that publishes enough to let the comparison be made. But it is the single most useful thing a reader can know before opening this plan, because for anyone still in work the cheaper L&G pension is very often the one their employer already runs.

One cap does not help here. The 0.75% ceiling on charges, in force since 2015, binds default arrangements inside auto-enrolment schemes, which is why L&G's own guide explains it on a workplace footing. A Personal Pension bought direct sits outside it altogether. At 0.56% the plan would comfortably clear the cap if it applied — 0.19 percentage points under — but nothing obliges it to.

Cash in this pension earns nothing and still pays the service charge

This is the clearest sentence in the whole document set, and it is in the terms and conditions: “The bank account your cash is held in does not attract interest and therefore you won't receive any return on any cash balance you hold in your Pension.” Uninvested money in an L&G Personal Pension earns 0%.

Read it next to the service charge and the two clauses meet. The 0.25% is “taken from any uninvested cash that's available” before L&G sells any units, so cash is the first thing the charge eats. A balance sitting between a contribution and an investment instruction is therefore charged at 0.25% a year and credited at nothing. The terms also say the cash is “held in a bank account in the name of the trustee” and never say who receives the interest that account earns, which is the one question the document does not answer.

The practical consequence is a rule of thumb rather than a scandal: on this plan, do not park money. Cash has a cost here and no offsetting return, which is a different arrangement from platforms that split bank interest with the customer and publish the split. It also means the plan is a poor home for anyone who wants to sit in cash while deciding, and that is worth knowing before the £100 goes in rather than after.

The compensation position differs from the charge position, too. Because the plan is provided by an investment company rather than an insurer, the key features document puts FSCS cover at “up to £85,000 for any loss incurred” — the investment limit per person per firm, not the unlimited protection that applies to money held in an insurer's long-term funds. On a pot built over a working life that ceiling is reached easily.

What L&G's own governance committee marked down for 2025

The Independent Governance Committee's report for the year ending 31 December 2025 was signed in September 2026, and its job is to criticise the firm that pays for it. It rates eight parts of the pension. Three came out Excellent — costs and charges, member service and administration, and environmental, social and governance investing — and the other five Good, with default funds and their investment performance climbing from “Acceptable, with areas for improvement”. The chair's overall view is that “members are receiving good value for money”, and independent benchmarking put L&G “in the top 25% of pension providers in the areas of service levels and member feedback scores”.

The growth funds are why. The 2055-60 target date fund returned 15.37% over one year against a comparator of 8.26%, and 7.77% a year over five against 7.10%. The newer Lifetime Advantage equivalent returned 15.06% against 8.76% in its first full year after launching in September 2024.

The retirement end is a different story, and the committee says so. The 2025-30 target date fund — the one holding money for people retiring now — returned 3.47% a year over five years against a comparator of 6.17%, which is 2.70 percentage points a year behind. The Multi-Asset Fund managed 4.51% against 6.71%. In the committee's own words, “the retirement stage TDFs have lagged behind comparable default strategies” and the underperformance “will have affected outcomes for those retiring”; overall, “the outcomes for members approaching or entering retirement could be improved”, with performance “mixed when compared with other providers, particularly over five years”. It also flagged the performance of Investment Pathway 4.

The list of things it wants fixed is longer than the ratings suggest. The new default funds cost typically 0.15% more than the ones they replaced. Drawdown still asked for a “wet signature” in some circumstances during 2025 and only went fully online in February 2026. The committee wants the £2,000 minimum for a partial cash lump sum reviewed sooner than L&G's 2027 timetable, and it has asked L&G to change its charging so that “those who inherit a drawdown account do not pay higher fees than the original owner”. An external audit found “areas where L&G needs to strengthen controls” in member engagement and service. Members reported “complexities and delays when transferring their pension benefits”, and the replacement process only launched in June 2026. Just 15% of members have nominated a beneficiary and only about 1.2% are identified as vulnerable, which the committee thinks is too few to be credible. Benchmarking also found L&G “offers fewer active ESG investment fund options compared to the other providers”.

One caveat matters more than any of it. That report covers L&G's workplace schemes and their 2.7 million members. Nothing in it covers the Personal Pension bought direct, so a retail buyer reads it as useful evidence about the same administrator and the same fund managers — not as scrutiny of the product they are actually buying.

Three-quarters of pension complaints are upheld, and one return stops in 2024

L&G publishes complaints returns for two entities separately, and the contrast between them is the sharpest documentary point on this page.

Legal and General Assurance Society's return is current. For 1 January to 30 June 2026 it shows 1,261 decumulation and pensions complaints opened and 1,289 closed, a rate of 1.09 per 1,000 policies in force, 19.62% closed within three days and 78.35% after three days but within eight weeks, and 73.85% upheld, with “General Admin / Customer Service” the main cause. The direction of travel is good: the previous half, 1 July to 31 December 2025, had 1,519 complaints at 1.30 per 1,000 and 75.31% upheld, so volumes fell 258 — about 17% — and the rate fell with them.

The return for Legal & General (Portfolio Management Services) Limited, the company that actually provides this Personal Pension, still covers 1 January to 30 June 2024. Two years on, that is the latest figure published. It records 565 decumulation and pensions complaints opened and 590 closed, 0.24 per 1,000 policies in force, 57.11% closed within three days, 74.91% upheld, and the same main cause. The investments line is tiny — six complaints at 1.22 per 1,000 client accounts, 80% upheld. The two entities hold different books and the periods are two years apart, so the rates are not comparable with each other; what is fair to say is that the only published complaints figure for the company selling this plan is two years old, while its sister company's is five months old.

Both uphold rates sit near three in four, and that number needs reading carefully rather than dramatically. A firm's own uphold rate counts complaints it decided in the customer's favour, so a high figure can mean a firm is quick to concede. The Ombudsman's figure is the one that follows a refusal. The Financial Ombudsman Service's annual data, published 21 May 2026 for 2025/26, records 214,649 new complaints across all products at a 30% average uphold rate, with Personal Pensions at 3,777 cases and 49% upheld — well above the average, and above conventional annuities at 360 cases and 39%. Defined benefit transfers not going into a SIPP were upheld in 64% of 311 cases. Those are market figures, not L&G's.

Tax-free cash has to be taken on the way in

Drawdown is available on the same plan from age 55, rising to 57 from April 2028, and the 0.25% service charge carries straight through: L&G's drawdown page repeats “We have a service charge of 0.25% of the value of your Pension per year” and says there is also a fund management charge “which differs from fund to fund”, without giving a figure.

The rule to read twice is on the same page: “To be able to access any tax-free cash, you'll need to do this at outset as you can't take any tax-free cash after you've moved your pension pot into drawdown.” The 25% is not a tap you turn on later. L&G caps it by reference to the £268,275 Lump Sum Allowance, which on this page's own arithmetic means the 25% stops being 25% on any pot above £1,073,100.

One more restriction sits in the terms: “we can only accept full pension transfers into drawdown.” A partial transfer in from another provider will not reach the drawdown side of the plan, and the terms separately require you to leave at least £100 in the Personal Pension if you transfer part of it away. Anybody intending to phase withdrawals across two providers needs to plan around both of those sentences.

£100 in, £1 after that, 30 days to change your mind

The entry terms are among the most generous on the market. The initial minimum lump sum is £100 and £1 thereafter, the minimum transfer in is £1, and the plan is open to customers aged 18 to 74 with payments accepted until 75. Most people can pay in up to the £60,000 annual allowance, and L&G's phrasing on tax relief — “up to 25% added to your contributions” — is the right way round: £80 of your own money becomes £100 in the pension at basic rate, which is a 25% uplift on what left your account.

You have 30 days from the date the pension is opened to cancel, and the cancellation date is stated in the welcome email. Getting out later costs nothing either: L&G says it does not charge “a fee for taking income, lump sums, switching investments, or transferring to another provider”. That is worth setting against the history its own guide describes — exit fees that once ran to 10% before the regulator capped them at 1% for savers over 55 and banned them in new plans.

Transfer speed is published on the workplace side rather than the retail side, and it is a reasonable proxy for the same administrator: an electronic Origo transfer “usually take[s] 5 to 10 working days after we receive the request and all the information we need”, and a transfer outside that system “is typically 15 days or more”. Overseas transfers and transfers of money already in drawdown take longer. The workplace minimum for a partial transfer out is £2,000. L&G's own warning on transferring in is the standard one and still worth heeding: “When you transfer a pension to us you could lose guaranteed benefits from your existing pension provider.”

The market context for all of this comes from L&G's own guide: the average person changes jobs every five years, and there are around three million lost pension pots in the UK. Consolidating is often the right instinct; checking what the old scheme charges before moving it is the part people skip, and on the numbers earlier in this review it is the part that decides whether the move saves money.

Two ombudsmen, eight weeks, and where to take a complaint

Most pension pages name the Financial Ombudsman Service and stop. L&G's terms are more precise, and the distinction decides where a reader actually writes. Complaints about administration that L&G cannot resolve go to The Pensions Ombudsman on 0800 917 4487. Complaints about the sale go to the Financial Ombudsman Service on 0800 023 4567. L&G's complaints page gives no general response deadline, only the escalation point: if no final response has arrived within eight weeks of the complaint being raised, the customer can ask the Ombudsman to look at it. The page also notes that Financial Ombudsman referral rights “may not be offered in all circumstances” for pensions, which is exactly why the two routes are worth separating in advance.

The one independent score available on the product is Smart Money People's, which rates the L&G pension 3.54 out of 5 from 47 individual reviews, with the brand last reviewed on 13 October 2026. The same source has L&G's life insurance at 4.57 from 122 reviews and its annuities at 4.13 from 6, so the pension is the weaker of its money products there. Forty-seven reviews is a small sample and should be read as one, but it is dated, independent and specific to the product, which is more than a star rating on a provider's own page offers.

Common questions

What does the L&G Personal Pension actually cost?

0.56% a year on L&G's own worked example: a 0.25% service charge on the plan value, calculated daily and collected monthly, plus a 0.31% fund management charge for the Multi-Index 3 fund. L&G converts that to “around £5.60 in fees over a year” on £1,000, which is £280 on £50,000. The fund charge differs from fund to fund, and transaction costs are extra and not totalled anywhere.

Is it cheaper to use my workplace L&G pension instead?

Usually, yes. The Independent Governance Committee's 2025 appendix puts the median member charge on the current Generation 3 workplace platform at 0.11% to 0.18%, with the dearest scheme at 0.37%. Against 0.56% on the direct plan, that is roughly three to five times the median — this page's own division of L&G's published figures. An employer negotiates in bulk and an individual cannot, so check what your scheme charges before moving money out of it.

Do I earn interest on cash sitting in the pension?

No. The terms and conditions state that “the bank account your cash is held in does not attract interest and therefore you won't receive any return on any cash balance you hold in your Pension”. The 0.25% service charge is also taken from uninvested cash before L&G sells any fund units, so cash costs 0.25% a year and returns nothing.

When can I take money out, and how much is tax free?

From 55, rising to 57 on 6 April 2028, with exceptions for a protected pension age or retirement through ill health. Up to 25% is normally tax free, limited by the £268,275 Lump Sum Allowance — which means the 25% stops being a full quarter on pots above £1,073,100. L&G's drawdown page is explicit that the tax-free cash has to be taken at outset: once the pot has moved into drawdown, none is available.

Who do I complain to if something goes wrong?

L&G first. If there is no final response within eight weeks, administration complaints go to The Pensions Ombudsman on 0800 917 4487 and complaints about the sale go to the Financial Ombudsman Service on 0800 023 4567. L&G notes that Financial Ombudsman referral rights may not be offered in all circumstances on a pension. For scale, the Ombudsman upheld 49% of 3,777 personal pension complaints in 2025/26 against a 30% average across all products.

Which company am I actually buying from, and what protects the money?

Legal & General (Portfolio Management Services) Limited, company 02457525, Financial Services Register number 146786 — the group's investment company rather than its insurer. The key features document puts FSCS cover at up to £85,000 for any loss incurred, which is the investment limit per person per firm rather than the unlimited cover that applies to money held in an insurer's long-term funds.

Our verdict

A plain, cheap-to-enter pension from a company with £1.2 trillion behind it, and one of the few whose charges you can actually add up: 0.25% to L&G, 0.31% to the fund manager on its own worked example, £5.60 a year per £1,000. For someone with a few old pots and no workplace scheme to use instead, £100 to start, nothing to leave and 30 days to change your mind is a fair offer, and the governance committee's Excellent ratings for cost and service are earned. Three things should decide it. If your employer runs an L&G scheme, the same company will very likely charge you between a third and a fifth of this, so check before consolidating anywhere. If you want to hold cash, hold it elsewhere — this plan pays 0% on it and charges 0.25% for the privilege. And if you are within a few years of drawing the money, read the retirement-stage fund performance and the tax-free-cash-at-outset rule before you move a pot you are about to spend.
