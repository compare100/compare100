Lenovo UK Computers: A One-Year Warranty, a Twelve-Month Battery and the Upgrades In Between

Rates and terms checked 16 November 2026 · Computers · Compare100 editorial team

Lenovo’s UK store sells its computers on a one-year parts-and-labour warranty, and the company behind the checkout holds 24.2% of the world’s PC market on its own August 2026 figures. Standard delivery is free, returns are free through Lenovo’s own process, and anything bought from 1 November 2026 can go back as late as 31 January 2027.

You are buying from Lenovo Technology United Kingdom Limited, Third Floor, 25 Templer Avenue, Farnborough GU14 6FE — a company that has been on the register since 26 September 2003. The UK shop carries seven laptop families, from ThinkPad and ThinkBook at the work end through Yoga, IdeaPad and Chromebooks to Legion and LOQ for gaming, and most of them are built to the specification you pick rather than taken off a shelf. Lenovo will match a price against Currys, Argos, John Lewis and Very before you order, students and teachers get up to an additional 7% off, and payment runs through Klarna, PayPal and Trustly as well as the usual cards.

One structural point is worth having before you start configuring. Buying from the maker means the warranty is the maker’s own and every service call goes to Lenovo rather than to a retailer’s returns desk, which is the simplest arrangement there is. It also means the cover you get is the cover Lenovo publishes: one year, with the battery treated as its own twelve-month item, and the faster service levels sold as separate products on top. Nothing about that is hidden — it is set out in Lenovo’s own warranty and services documents, and the rest of this page works through what each of them says.

One year of parts and labour, and twelve months for the battery

The Lenovo Limited Warranty is the document that decides what you get for nothing, and it starts the clock in the plainest possible place: “The warranty period for the product starts on the original date of purchase as shown on your sales receipt or invoice.” Lenovo’s own laptop pages put the length at one year, with extensions of up to three years available to buy.

The battery is handled separately, and this is the single most useful line on the page. Lenovo’s Personal Computing Devices Services Agreement for the region states that the “Warranty Period for all Lenovo batteries, stylus, and digitizer pens are limited to 12 months unless otherwise specified”. Lenovo’s warranty and protection page says the same thing from the other direction: the Sealed Battery Warranty “extends the one-year base battery warranty to 3 years” and carries a one-time battery replacement. Put the two together and the arithmetic is unavoidable — buy a three-year warranty on a machine with a battery you cannot take out yourself, and in year two the part most likely to wear out is the part no longer covered.

The extension itself is sold in terms of 2, 3 or 4 years. It cannot be bought for a desktop at all, and non-ThinkPad notebooks and ThinkBook models are limited to the three-year version, so the four-year option is in practice a ThinkPad one. One replacement is what the entitlement buys; a second failure in the same term is yours.

What the base warranty does not reach is set out in a list, and it is longer than most people expect. Lenovo does not warrant “uninterrupted or error-free operation of a product”, nor “loss of, or damage to, your data by a product”, nor “any software programs, whether provided with the product or installed subsequently”. Damage from “misuse, abuse, accident, modification, unsuitable physical or operating environment” is out, so is damage caused by a non-authorised repairer, and so is “any technical or other support, such as assistance with ‘how-to’ questions”. A machine with a removed or altered identification label is out as well. The ceiling on all of it is the price on the invoice: liability is capped at actual direct damages, “NOT TO EXCEED THE AMOUNT PAID FOR THE PRODUCT”.

Seven kinds of service exist, and a UK laptop gets two of them

Lenovo defines seven different ways a warranty repair can happen, and knowing which one applies to your machine matters more than the length of the cover. In order: Customer Replaceable Unit service, where “the Service Provider will ship CRUs to you at its cost for installation by you”; on-site service, where a provider “will either repair or exchange the product at your location”; courier or depot service, where the machine “will be repaired or exchanged at a designated service center”; customer carry-in; mail-in; customer two-way mail-in; and product exchange, where “Lenovo will ship a replacement product to your location”.

A consumer notebook sits at the self-service end of that list. On the warranty tables Lenovo publishes for UK notebook models, the entitlement is one year of parts and labour, one year on the battery pack, and service types one and four — ship the part to the customer, or have the customer bring the machine in. Neither of those involves an engineer coming to you, which is what the upgrades are for.

There is a United Kingdom clause in Lenovo’s services agreement that deserves reading before you assume a depot repair is free. Under the UK version of depot service, “You are responsible for disconnecting the product, packing it in the shipping container” and “Shipping expenses will be paid by you.” Lenovo pays the other leg: “The Service Provider will return the repaired or replacement product to you at its expense.” One way out of your pocket, one way out of Lenovo’s.

The on-site levels Lenovo sells are genuinely fast on paper. An eight-hour entitlement means “a service technician will be dispatched to your location within eight hours”, and there is a four-hour version, both available in business-hours and 24x7 forms, with next business day and second business day beneath them. The UK carries its own cut-off: “To meet the response time, support call needs to be received by designated service center by 3:00pm UK time.” Ring at four in the afternoon and the next business day is the one after tomorrow.

One more piece of plumbing is worth knowing. The company you are contracting with for a service is “the Lenovo affiliate in the country of purchase”, and where Lenovo identifies no affiliate, it is Lenovo PC HK Ltd. The warranty text also grants service across the European Economic Area — cover on hardware bought in an EEA country “may be obtained in any EEA country” where Lenovo has announced and made the product available — which is a clause the UK no longer sits inside. For travel beyond the home country Lenovo sells an International Warranty Upgrade, which carries the sealed battery, Keep Your Drive and accidental damage entitlements abroad at the home country’s service level or the closest available.

The upgrade names, and what each one actually adds

Four paid products sit on top of the base warranty and they do different jobs. Accidental Damage Protection is the one most buyers mean when they ask about extra cover: it “covers accidental drops, spills, bumps, and structural failures incurred under normal operating conditions or handling”, it has been available to UK customers since 10 December 2024, and Lenovo’s own estimate is that it saves buyers “typically... between 28%–80%” against the cost of the repair or replacement. The limit is one replacement during the term, so it is insurance against an accident rather than against a clumsy year.

Premier Support is the telephone product — “advanced level phone support for hardware and software issues”, with a dedicated number and a single point of contact running the case from end to end. That is the upgrade that buys back the “how-to” help the base warranty explicitly excludes. Keep Your Drive does one narrow thing well: “if your hard drive fails, you are entitled to keep the defective drive before handing the PC over to Lenovo for repair”, which for anyone carrying client files or medical records is the difference between a repair being possible and not. The warranty upgrade proper is the one that brings an engineer out, with a technician “dispatched to arrive on-site, typically the next day” once a problem is judged not fixable over the phone.

What none of Lenovo’s own pages carry is a price for any of this. The warranty and protection pages describe every product in detail and quote no figure, and the UK support pages that list extended warranty, accidental damage protection and Premier Support name them without costing them. The number only appears once you have a specific machine in a basket, which makes the upgrades impossible to compare between manufacturers before you have half-committed to one.

Fourteen days by law, and a longer window that closes on 31 January

Lenovo’s UK terms of sale give the statutory position without dressing it up: “you may withdraw from (or cancel) this contract within 14 calendar days without giving a reason”. The goods go back “no more than 14 days from the date on which you inform us of your withdrawal”, and the refund follows “no later than 14 days from the day on which we receive your notification of withdrawal”.

Three exceptions in those same terms matter on a Lenovo order more than they would almost anywhere else. There is no right of withdrawal “where the contract involves the delivery of goods that are made to your specifications” or that “are clearly personalised” — and a built-to-order machine, which is most of what the Lenovo store sells, is exactly that. Sealed software loses the right once “the seal is removed or broken after delivery”, and digital content loses it once performance has begun. So on a configured laptop the thing you are actually relying on is not the statutory window at all; it is Lenovo’s own returns policy.

That policy is more generous than the law in two respects, and it is published on the shopping pages rather than in the contract. A return can be requested “within 14 days of delivery”, and “returns are free of charge when using the standard Lenovo return process”, with a drop-off or a collection. Orders placed in the run-up to Christmas get longer: “Products purchased from 01/11/26 can be returned by 31/01/2027 thanks to our extended return period.”

The delivery terms are short and worth knowing. Standard shipping is free on everything, with no spend threshold. Lenovo “do not deliver products to any address outside the United Kingdom or Ireland”. Attempts are made Monday to Friday in business hours, the carrier is not named anywhere, and a multi-item order may arrive in pieces, potentially with “different carriers”. Risk transfers the moment it lands: “The risk of loss of or damage to the product(s) shall pass to you upon delivery.” Shipping estimates are production estimates rather than delivery promises, and once an order is in, Lenovo “cannot guarantee cancellation”. A pricing mistake is handled fairly — you get “the option to either cancel the order at no charge or proceed with the transaction at the corrected price”. Payment runs on Visa, Mastercard, American Express, debit cards, wire transfer, PayPal, Klarna and Trustly, with TreviPay reserved for business accounts.

A record quarter and a reported loss in the same announcement

Lenovo’s results for the quarter ended 30 June 2026, published on 13 August 2026, are the most recent the company has issued, and they explain why its service network is as wide as it is. Group revenue “reached an all-time quarterly high of US$26.9 billion, up 43% year-on-year”. The PC side, which Lenovo calls the Intelligent Devices Group, “increased 27% year-on-year to record first fiscal quarter revenue of US$17.1 billion”, with revenue from PCs and smart devices up nearly 30%. Global PC market share “reached 24.2%”, more than five percentage points clear of the next company, and Lenovo puts its AI PC share at 25.1%. The infrastructure arm nearly doubled to US$8.5 billion and the services arm reached US$2.9 billion, up 28%. Research and development came in at US$682 million, up 30%.

In the same tables the group reports a net loss attributable to equity holders of US$609 million, and the announcement does not say what caused it. The figure Lenovo leads on is an adjusted one — “adjusted net income was up 176% year-on-year to US$1.1 billion” — and the footnote defining it excludes net fair value changes on financial assets, the fair value change on derivative financial liabilities relating to warrants, and notional interest on convertible bonds. Those are the items that typically separate the two numbers, but the announcement does not quantify them against this quarter, so the honest statement is that the reported loss is unexplained in the document that reports it.

For somebody buying one laptop, the scale number is the one that matters and the loss number is not much of a worry: a quarter of the world’s PCs is what keeps spare parts, drivers and depot capacity available for the five or six years a good machine lasts. It is worth printing both all the same, because a page that quotes only the record revenue is quoting the press release rather than the results.

Farnborough, and the two Lenovo companies at one address

The name on a consumer invoice is Lenovo Technology United Kingdom Limited, and the register puts flesh on it: company number 04912819, incorporated 26 September 2003, registered at Third Floor, 25 Templer Avenue, Farnborough GU14 6FE. Twenty-three years of trading history under one number is a reasonable thing to know before handing over a four-figure order.

A second Lenovo company shares the same Farnborough address — Lenovo Global Technology UK Limited, number 10219781, incorporated 7 June 2016 — and it is the arm that sells servers and data centre equipment rather than laptops. Both are legitimate parts of the same group, and the distinction only matters if a dispute ever needs a letter addressed correctly: the one on a consumer order is 04912819.

What Lenovo’s own terms of sale do not carry is either of those numbers. The contract names the entity and prints the Farnborough address, and stops there — no company number, no VAT registration number. Both are free to look up and neither is secret, but a shopper reading the contract on the page cannot tell which of the group’s companies they have just contracted with, and a business buyer reclaiming VAT has to go and find the number somewhere else.

The one number the shop will not print, and a register that holds almost nothing

No machine price is quoted on this page, and that is not an oversight. Lenovo’s UK laptop deals page, its laptops category and an individual IdeaPad product page all return model names, series and specifications with the price fields empty, and the site-wide savings banner comes back as the unfilled template “Save up to {savingPercent}”. The prices are assembled in the browser, one configuration at a time, which means there is no readable UK price list to compare against HP’s or Dell’s, and no published cost for any of the warranty upgrades either. The honest version of this section is a gap rather than a figure.

Lenovo does operate price protection, in three overlapping forms, and one of them is described inconsistently on its own pages. A pre-purchase match will be considered against Currys, Argos, John Lewis and Very. A Lowest Unit Price Match promises that “if, subsequent to your order, the price drops lower we will refund the difference” — but the shopping FAQ states its duration two different ways, one passage tying it to orders placed between 13 October and 31 December 2026 and another saying the guarantee is “valid for 14 days from the date of delivery”. Those are not the same promise, so no duration is quoted here; ask before relying on it. A third scheme, the Price Promise, covered purchases from 22 April to 30 June 2026 with claims closing on 14 October 2026, and is still sitting on the page after expiry.

On safety, the government’s product recalls and alerts register returns two results for “lenovo”, and only one of them is a Lenovo product: a Lenovo USB-C laptop power bank, recalled from end users on 30 November 2023. The other is a counterfeit Type C laptop charger sold through an online marketplace, whose listing was removed on 18 November 2025. Two entries against a company shipping a quarter of the world’s PCs is a short list, and it is worth reading in both directions — the register records what has been reported to it, not everything that has ever gone wrong, and a single power bank is a very different thing from a notebook recall.

Where it wins

  • Buying from the manufacturer means one warranty, one service network and no retailer standing between you and the repair
  • Free standard shipping on everything, with no minimum spend
  • Returns are free through Lenovo’s standard process, and anything bought from 1 November 2026 can go back until 31 January 2027
  • Seven defined service types, with paid on-site response down to four hours and a 24x7 option
  • A sealed-battery extension exists at all, in 2, 3 and 4-year terms, which is more than most makers sell
  • Keep Your Drive lets you retain a failed disk instead of posting your data away with the machine
  • Lenovo will consider a price match against Currys, Argos, John Lewis and Very before you order
  • Students and teachers get up to an additional 7% off, and payment includes Klarna, PayPal and Trustly
  • Scale that keeps parts and drivers alive: 24.2% of global PC shipments and US$682m of quarterly research spending on Lenovo’s own August 2026 figures

Where it falls short

  • The battery, stylus and digitiser pen are capped at 12 months even on a machine with a longer warranty, so on a sealed-battery laptop the part most likely to wear out is uncovered from year two unless you pay for the extension
  • Lenovo’s own United Kingdom clause makes the customer pay the carriage to the repair depot — “Shipping expenses will be paid by you” — with Lenovo paying only the return leg
  • Next-business-day on-site response is measured from a 3:00pm UK cut-off, so an afternoon fault becomes a two-day wait
  • A built-to-order machine is goods “made to your specifications”, which removes the statutory 14-day right of withdrawal and leaves you relying on Lenovo’s voluntary returns policy instead
  • No price for any warranty upgrade appears on Lenovo’s own UK pages, and the store’s machine prices are assembled in the browser, so nothing can be compared before configuring
  • The lowest-price refund is described two ways on the same shopping FAQ — an order window of 13 October to 31 December 2026 in one passage, 14 days from delivery in another — and an expired Price Promise from June 2026 is still published alongside them
  • Accidental damage cover is limited to one replacement during the term, and the warranty excludes data loss, software of any kind and “how-to” support outright
  • Warranty liability is capped at the amount paid for the product, and Lenovo delivers only to UK and Ireland addresses

Common questions

How long is the warranty on a Lenovo bought in the UK?

One year of parts and labour as standard, running from the original date of purchase shown on the sales receipt or invoice. Lenovo sells extensions of up to three years, and the paid upgrades also change how the repair happens rather than only how long you are covered for.

Is the battery covered for the same period as the laptop?

No. Lenovo’s services agreement limits the warranty period for “all Lenovo batteries, stylus, and digitizer pens” to 12 months unless stated otherwise, and its warranty pages describe the Sealed Battery Warranty as extending “the one-year base battery warranty to 3 years”. The extension is sold in 2, 3 and 4-year terms, gives one battery replacement, is not available for desktops, and is capped at three years on non-ThinkPad notebooks and ThinkBook models.

Can I send a Lenovo laptop back if I change my mind?

Lenovo’s shopping pages allow a return request within 14 days of delivery, free through the standard process, and purchases from 1 November 2026 can be returned until 31 January 2027. Note that the statutory 14-day withdrawal right in the terms of sale does not apply to goods “made to your specifications”, which covers a configured machine — so on a built-to-order laptop you are relying on Lenovo’s own policy rather than the law.

Who repairs it, and does it cost anything to send in?

Lenovo’s own service network, through one of seven defined service types. A consumer notebook typically gets parts shipped to you to fit, or a carry-in repair. If it goes to a depot, the UK clause in Lenovo’s services agreement says you pack it and “Shipping expenses will be paid by you”, while Lenovo pays to send it back. An engineer visiting you is a paid upgrade, with response levels of four hours, eight hours, next business day and second business day, and a 3:00pm UK deadline for the call.

Is the extra cover worth buying?

It depends which gap you are filling. Accidental Damage Protection is the only route to a drop or a spill being paid for, and Lenovo estimates it saves buyers “typically... between 28%–80%” of the repair cost, limited to one replacement in the term. The sealed-battery extension is the one that closes a real hole in the standard warranty. Premier Support buys back the “how-to” help the base warranty excludes. None of them is priced on Lenovo’s public UK pages, so get the figures in the basket and compare them against the machine price before deciding.

Which company am I actually buying from?

Lenovo Technology United Kingdom Limited, company number 04912819, incorporated on 26 September 2003 and registered at Third Floor, 25 Templer Avenue, Farnborough GU14 6FE. A second group company, Lenovo Global Technology UK Limited (10219781), shares the address and handles servers and data centre kit. Lenovo’s terms of sale name the entity and the address but print neither the company number nor a VAT number.

Our verdict

Lenovo is the biggest PC maker in the world and it sells in Britain the way a manufacturer does: free delivery, free returns through its own process, a published warranty and a service desk that answers for its own hardware. The one-year parts-and-labour cover is standard for the industry and the returns window running to 31 January 2027 on autumn orders is better than standard. Where the deal needs reading closely is the twelve-month limit on batteries, styluses and pens, the UK depot clause that puts the outbound carriage on the customer, and the 3:00pm cut-off that decides whether next business day means tomorrow. If you want a machine to last four or five years, price the sealed-battery extension and the on-site upgrade at the same time as the laptop, because neither is in the box and neither is costed anywhere you can see before you configure. Buy the hardware on the hardware; buy the support deliberately.
