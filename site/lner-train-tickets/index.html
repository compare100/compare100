LNER Train Tickets: Fares, the 70min Flex and What Changed

Rates and terms checked 15 November 2026 · Train Tickets · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

LNER adds no booking fee to any ticket bought on lner.co.uk or in the LNER app, and in April 2026 the cheapest Advance single from London King's Cross to Newcastle was £27.60 each way against a top Anytime fare of £200.80 on the same route. Every figure here was checked on 15 November 2026 against LNER's own fact sheet, Passenger's Charter and published performance tables.

London North Eastern Railway Limited runs the East Coast Main Line and is one of nine of the Department for Transport's fourteen contracted operators now listed as publicly owned. It carried 26.6 million passenger journeys and 6,154 million passenger kilometres in the year to March 2025, over 1,404 route kilometres, from eleven managed stations, with 3,407 full-time equivalent staff.

One thing to settle before searching for a fare: on LNER's busiest flows the Off-Peak and Super Off-Peak tickets no longer exist. Return fares went first, withdrawn across the network for travel from 11 June 2023. Then from 5 February 2024 the walk-up Off-Peak and Super Off-Peak singles were removed between London King's Cross and Newcastle, Edinburgh and Berwick-upon-Tweed, leaving three fares: Advance, a semi-flexible ticket called 70min Flex, and a capped Anytime single.

What replaced them does several things well. A 70min Flex ticket carries a guaranteed seat reservation and lets you start the journey up to 70 minutes earlier or later than booked at no charge. Book direct and Delay Repay is a single click rather than a form. LNER Perks pays £5 for joining and 2% of every ticket back as credit, and groups of three to nine get 20% off Advance fares without asking.

What a single costs now the return has gone

LNER started pulling returns apart in 2019. Trial sales of single leg pricing opened on 30 November 2019 for travel from 2 January 2020 on three flows out of London King's Cross — Newcastle, Leeds and Edinburgh — with each single priced at roughly half the old return. Sales across nearly all LNER priced flows opened on 14 May 2023 for travel from 11 June 2023, and the return disappeared as a product.

The second change is the one that bites. From 5 February 2024, on journeys between London and Newcastle, Edinburgh and Berwick-upon-Tweed, the Off-Peak and Super Off-Peak singles were withdrawn too. Advance and 70min Flex prices now move with how full the train is, how close departure is and which class you pick, with the Anytime single acting as the ceiling no fare may pass.

LNER publishes three assurances about where that leaves a passenger, and its own April 2026 measurements against them. At least two thirds of Advance fares are meant to cost under half the Anytime price: the figure reported was 94.9%. No more than 10% are meant to sit at 80% or more of the Anytime price: the figure reported was 1.1%. On the worked examples, the best Advance single was £27.60 to Newcastle and £41.40 to Edinburgh, against top Anytime fares of £200.80 and £204.80 — so the ceiling on the Newcastle flow is 7.3 times the floor.

Against the fares that were taken away, LNER counts about 2.0 million Advance tickets priced below the old Super Off-Peak on trains where that fare used to be valid, and 1.3 million of the new semi-flexible tickets priced below it. Just under half of the Standard Class 70min Flex fares sold in the four weeks to that count cost less than the Super Off-Peak single would have. Advance fares usually go on sale 12 or more weeks before travel and can be bought up to five minutes before the train leaves.

The ticket that buys you seventy minutes

70min Flex is the fare LNER invented to fill the hole the Off-Peak left. It is priced like an Advance, it reserves you a seat on the train you pick, and it lets you start the journey up to 70 minutes earlier or later than that train — a window of 140 minutes in total — with no fee and no paperwork. Direct customers swap in My Account or the app; anyone else boards a valid train and finds a seat.

The geography matters because it is not an everywhere ticket. At the northern end it covers Edinburgh, Berwick-upon-Tweed, Newcastle and surrounding stations including Morpeth, Dunbar, Musselburgh and Cramlington. At the southern end it covers London terminals, Alexandra Palace, Stevenage and intermediate stations, all rail stations in Zones 1 to 4 and tube stations in Zones 1 to 6. From August 2025 it was extended to connecting services run by Northern, TransPennine Express, CrossCountry, ScotRail, Govia Thameslink and Transport for London, and from summer 2026 to journeys between London King's Cross and the Northumberland Line stations — Ashington, Bedlington, Blyth Bebside, Newsham, Seaton Delaval and Northumberland Park. LNER has to be the primary operator of the itinerary for the London leg to qualify.

LNER's own usage figures say more about the ticket than the sales copy does. About 75% of 70min Flex buyers travel on the train they booked. Of those who do swap, 75% do it within 24 hours of departure and 50% within two hours. Passengers moving to an earlier train outnumber those moving to a later one by three to one, and most of those swaps are northbound — people going home early rather than staying late.

Four limits are worth knowing before you buy. The ticket is not refundable. Changing the date or time beyond the 70-minute window costs a £10 admin fee plus any fare difference. Travel Centre staff cannot flex a ticket for you, so without a phone or an account you are relying on finding a seat. And the guarantee attaches to the LNER leg only: on a connecting operator's train you may be standing. It is sold through every retail channel except onboard, and it does not always appear next to the Advance fare in a search.

What the Department for Transport found when it measured the trial

On 29 October 2026 the Department for Transport published a 276-page evaluation of both changes, written by SYSTRA, titled Evaluation of single leg pricing and Simpler Fares trial on LNER: final report. It compared a window before each change with an equivalent window after — for single leg pricing, 27 June 2021 to 12 November 2022 against 25 June 2023 to 9 November 2024 — and set the trial flows beside control flows that were left alone.

Single leg pricing did well on the money. Revenue on the London trial flows rose 42%, from £113.9m to £161.8m, journeys rose 19%, and yield per journey rose 19% from £37 to £44. Non-London trial flows took £38.4m against £33.0m, up 16%, with journeys down 2% and yield up 19%. The control flows grew too — non-LNER London flows put on 33% of revenue and 22% of journeys — so the trial's own contribution is smaller than the raw rise, and the modelling puts it at about £16,000 a week with no statistically significant effect on demand.

Simpler Fares reads differently. On Phase 1a — London to Newcastle, Edinburgh and Berwick-upon-Tweed — revenue moved from £177.0m to £179.4m, a rise of 1%, while journeys fell 2% and yield rose 4% from £60 to £62. Over the same period LNER's own control flows grew revenue and journeys by 16% each. Phase 1b was worse: revenue down 5% from £5.5m to £5.3m and journeys down 8%. None of the Phase 1a figures was statistically significant, and the report cautions that some passengers may simply have moved onto routes the trial did not cover.

The passenger findings are the hardest part of the document. By the fourth survey wave, 32% of passengers on the Simpler Fares flows rated the new structure fair — up from 24% — against 47% who rated it unfair. Single leg pricing scored 53% fair, and the system both replaced scored 37%. Awareness was thin: 16% fully aware, 30% partly aware, 54% not aware at all. Asked about a UK-wide rollout of Simpler Fares, 19% said they would travel more by rail and 36% less; for single leg pricing alone the same question returned 49% more and 3% less.

Three rivals on the same tracks, and why the fare changed because of them

In Monitoring open access: 2025 update, published on 12 September 2025, the Office of Rail and Road describes the East Coast Main Line as the only long-distance route in Britain with significant head-to-head competition between a contracted operator and open access ones. LNER shares its tracks with Grand Central, Hull Trains and Lumo, all of whom sell per single and always have.

They are small. Across all domestic open access services there were 9 million passenger journeys in 2024/25 against 1,729 million in Great Britain as a whole, which is 0.5%. They are growing, though: long-distance open access journeys sat about 75% above their 2018/19 level while long-distance contracted operators were back at roughly where they started. In July 2025 the regulator approved extra services for Grand Central and Hull Trains and extensions for Lumo, and rejected a Hull Trains bid for additional Sheffield services on capacity grounds and a low net present adjustment ratio.

The DfT evaluation separated out the flows where LNER has company, and that is where single leg pricing paid best. Revenue on contested London flows rose 72%, from £25.8m to £44.4m, with journeys up 55% — against 42% and 19% on London trial flows taken together. On contested non-London flows revenue rose 34% and journeys 30%. In the modelling, the weekly journey gain only reached statistical significance in the version that accounts for competition, at about 286 journeys a week. Matching a rival's pricing structure, in other words, is where the measurable passengers came from.

Punctuality and cancellations, period by period

LNER publishes its Department for Transport key performance indicators every rail period, target included, which is more than most sellers of anything do. Across the first six periods of 2026/27 the share of station stops reached within three minutes ran 66.4%, 66.2%, 65.0%, 64.8%, 62.8% and 63.4%, against targets of 68.5% in period one and 64.9% thereafter. The rolling average fell from 71.6% to 68.1% over those six periods. Cancelled stops came in at 4.2%, 3.4%, 2.6%, 2.1%, 4.0% and 3.7% against targets of 2.8% and then 3.4%, and cancellations LNER caused itself spiked to 4.0% in period three against a rolling 1.2%.

The full year before that was better than target on both counts. For 2025/26 LNER reports punctuality of 72.40% against a 68.30% target, all-station cancellations of 2.20% against 3.0%, part or full cancellations of its own making at 0.70%, 3.76 delay minutes per 1,000 train miles attributable to LNER, and 0.12% of services running with fewer seats than planned.

For scale, the Office of Rail and Road put Great Britain's three-minute punctuality at 85.0% across 1.9 million planned trains between 1 April and 30 June 2026, with 4.1% of trains cancelled on a score that counts a part cancellation as a half. A long-distance operator will always sit below a national average dominated by short commuter runs, but the gap is large. One caution: the regulator's own LNER key stats sheet records On Time at 56.5% for April 2024 to March 2025 on a measure the sheet does not define, so it cannot be set against the three-minute figures above and is not set against them here. The same sheet gives 55,832 trains planned and 3.8% cancelled that year.

Satisfaction has drifted the wrong way. The moving annual average for overall customer satisfaction fell from 69.1% in period one of 2026/27 to 67.7% in period six, against a DfT target of 69.0% — below target in five of the six. Satisfaction with information during disruption held between 68.6% and 69.3% against a 68.0% target. Complaints ran between 117 and 189 per 100,000 journeys across those periods. LNER's Spring 2026 customer report puts the 2025/26 totals at 33,151 complaints received and 31,263 closed, 109.7 per 100,000 journeys, 97% answered within 20 days, and Passenger Assist bookings of 262,869 of which 18,018 were not fulfilled.

Delay Repay, the seat guarantee and the forty working days

The Passenger's Charter sets compensation against the timetable published on the day you travel, not the one you booked from. Arrive 30 to 59 minutes late and you get at least 50% of the single fare, or 50% of the relevant portion of a return. At 60 to 119 minutes it is 100% of the single. At 120 minutes or more it is 100% of the return. Claims go in within 28 days with the ticket attached, and LNER aims to process them within 10 working days. Book direct and travel as booked and the claim is one click; buy elsewhere and it is a form.

Season ticket holders are paid a fraction of the whole price per qualifying delay: the weekly price divided by 10, a Flexi by 16, a monthly by 40, a quarterly by 120 and an annual by 464. On an annual ticket that is a 464th of what you paid for a delay of half an hour or more, which is worth knowing before anyone assumes a season ticket is compensated like a single.

Two smaller guarantees are worth claiming. Where a reserved seat is not available and no alternative is found, LNER pays 100% of a single ticket's value or 50% of a return, and a First Class reservation honoured only with a Standard seat gets the fare difference back. Where booked assistance is not provided, the same 100% and 50% apply; assistance can be booked up to two hours before departure on a line open 24 hours a day.

The charter also commits to things that are not money. Refreshments are offered on delays of 60 minutes or more while stocks last. Alternative transport is arranged where the next connection is 60 minutes or more later and road would be quicker. Ticket queues are targeted at no more than five minutes at peak times and three at other times. Timetables are published at least 28 days before they take effect, engineering work is summarised 12 weeks out and detailed at least seven days out, and luggage runs to three items a passenger with assisted luggage capped at 23kg. If a complaint is unresolved after 40 working days, or within 12 months of LNER's final answer, it can be taken to the Rail Ombudsman.

Refund rules tightened on 1 April 2026

The date to note is 1 April 2026, because it split LNER's flexible tickets into two cohorts. An Off-Peak or Anytime single bought on or before 31 March 2026 can be refunded up to 28 days after the ticket expires, subject to an admin charge of up to £5 per ticket. One bought on or after 1 April 2026 can only be refunded until 23:59 on the day before it becomes valid, the same charge applies, and a ticket bought on the day of travel cannot be refunded at all. The window went from four weeks after expiry to the night before departure.

Advance tickets have their own clock. They can be refunded through My Account within two hours of booking and not after that. Beyond the two hours the only route is a change, which costs a £10 admin fee plus any difference in fare. 70min Flex cannot be refunded at any point; a change inside its validity window costs £10, and moving to a train outside the 70 minutes costs £10 plus the fare difference if the new ticket is dearer.

Season tickets sit somewhere between. A Flexi Season refund carries an admin fee of up to £10 and needs at least one unused day pass left on it. A changeover needs at least seven days of validity remaining. A partial refund for illness is discretionary and only considered after more than four weeks away. Refunds caused by disruption carry no admin fee at all, and unused tickets have to come back within 28 days of the date you meant to travel.

Perks, group discounts and what 2% is actually worth

LNER Perks pays £5 for joining, once — leave and rejoin and there is no second £5 — and it can only be spent as part payment on an Advance ticket bought in the app and costing more than £5.15. After that the scheme returns 2% of every £1 spent on LNER tickets bought on the website or app, either as account credit or as a donation to a charity LNER nominates.

Run the arithmetic before counting on it. On the £27.60 Advance single to Newcastle, 2% is 55p, so clearing the price of a second single takes roughly £1,380 of ticket spend. Credit expires 24 months after the most recent of your last booking or your last externally added ticket, and LNER can move the expiry date on 30 days' notice. Referring a friend pays £10 once they have spent £30 or more and travelled, capped at five rewards in 24 hours and 25 in a rolling 12 months.

The scheme will credit tickets you did not buy from LNER, which is unusual, but on conditions: the ticket needs a seat reservation made at the time of booking, it has to be valid on an LNER train, and it has to be added no more than seven days after the travel date. The caps are one other person's ticket per travel date, 15 tickets in any seven days and £1,000 of combined value. Anything paid for with a National Rail Travel voucher earns nothing, refunded tickets earn nothing, and child and Privilege season tickets are excluded. Members must be 18 or over and hold one account only.

Group travel is the better discount for most people and needs no membership: three to nine passengers get 20% off Advance fares, applied automatically, and ten or more get 22%. LNER also flags a live fault on its own terms page — some customers trying to redeem credit in the app are being pushed to the website mid-booking and cannot spend it — which it says is a priority to fix.

Where it wins

  • No booking fee on any ticket bought on lner.co.uk or in the LNER app, stated as a flat commitment rather than a promotion.
  • 70min Flex comes with a guaranteed seat reservation and 140 minutes of latitude around the booked train at no charge.
  • LNER beat its own fare assurances comfortably in April 2026: 94.9% of Standard Advance fares cost under half the Anytime price against a two-thirds floor, and 1.1% sat above 80% of it against a 10% ceiling.
  • Delay Repay starts at 30 minutes rather than an hour, pays 100% of the single from 60 minutes, and is a target 10 working days to process.
  • One-click Delay Repay for anyone who booked direct and travelled as booked, instead of a claim form.
  • Punctuality, cancellations, satisfaction, complaints and service quality are all published by rail period against the Department for Transport's own targets, including the periods LNER missed them.
  • Groups of three to nine get 20% off Advance fares automatically, rising to 22% at ten or more passengers.
  • LNER Perks will credit tickets bought from other retailers, provided they carry a seat reservation made at booking and are added within seven days of travel.

Where it falls short

  • Off-Peak and Super Off-Peak singles cannot be bought at any price between London and Newcastle, Edinburgh or Berwick-upon-Tweed, so a passenger hunting the walk-up fare they used to buy on those flows will not find it.
  • The spread is wide. The dearest Anytime single to Newcastle was £200.80 in April 2026 against a £27.60 best Advance, 7.3 times as much, and LNER's own fact sheet confirms that busier trains cost more.
  • Passengers on the affected flows are not persuaded. The Department for Transport's evaluation of 29 October 2026 found 47% rated Simpler Fares unfair against 32% who called it fair, 54% were unaware of it altogether, and on a UK-wide rollout 36% said they would travel less by rail against 19% more.
  • Refund terms were tightened on 1 April 2026. An Off-Peak or Anytime single bought before that date was refundable up to 28 days after it expired; one bought on or after it is refundable only until 23:59 the day before travel, and not at all if bought on the day. The up to £5 per ticket admin charge survived the change.
  • 70min Flex is not refundable under any circumstance, moving it outside its window costs £10 plus any fare difference, Travel Centre staff cannot flex it for you, and the seat guarantee covers only the LNER leg — a connecting train may mean standing.
  • The Passenger's Charter cannot say when it took effect. The cover is dated April 2026 and the final page reads Updated 15 December 2024, with nothing in the document reconciling the two, so a reader cannot tell which version of the promises they are holding.
  • LNER's own customer ratings page contradicts itself on period four of 2026/27: the narrative states that performance was below the DfT target for station cleanliness while the table immediately beside it prints 87.0% against an 85.0% target.
  • The published trend is downward. Overall customer satisfaction fell from 69.1% to 67.7% across the first six periods of 2026/27 against a 69.0% target, missing it in five of six, while three-minute punctuality fell from 66.4% to 63.4% over the same stretch against a national figure of 85.0%.

Common questions

Does LNER charge a booking fee?

No. LNER states that it never adds booking fees to tickets bought on lner.co.uk or in the LNER app, which is where the £0 figure in the comparison above comes from. Fees appear later rather than at the point of sale: changing an Advance or a 70min Flex ticket costs a £10 admin fee plus any fare difference, refunding an Off-Peak or Anytime single costs up to £5 per ticket, and a Flexi Season refund costs up to £10.

Why can I not find an Off-Peak return on LNER?

Because both halves of it have been withdrawn. Return fares were removed across the LNER network for travel from 11 June 2023, so every fare is now priced per single journey at roughly half the old return. Then from 5 February 2024 the Off-Peak and Super Off-Peak singles were removed between London King's Cross and Newcastle, Edinburgh and Berwick-upon-Tweed, leaving Advance, 70min Flex and Anytime on those flows. Off-Peak fares still exist on LNER journeys outside that trial area.

What is a 70min Flex ticket?

It is LNER's semi-flexible fare, introduced with the Simpler Fares trial in February 2024. It reserves you a seat on a named train and lets you start the journey up to 70 minutes earlier or later than that train without paying anything, a window of 140 minutes. It is not refundable. Changing the date or time beyond that window costs a £10 admin fee plus any difference in fare. About 75% of buyers travel on the train they booked, and of the rest, half swap within two hours of departure.

How much does LNER pay if the train is late?

At least 50% of the single fare for a delay of 30 to 59 minutes, 100% of the single at 60 to 119 minutes, and 100% of the return at 120 minutes or more, measured against the timetable published on the day of travel. Claims must be submitted within 28 days with the ticket attached, and LNER aims to process them within 10 working days. Season tickets are compensated as a fraction of the whole price — a 10th for a weekly, a 40th for a monthly, a 464th for an annual.

Can I get a refund on an LNER ticket?

It depends on the fare and, since 1 April 2026, on when you bought it. An Advance can be refunded through My Account within two hours of booking and not afterwards. A 70min Flex cannot be refunded at all. An Off-Peak or Anytime single bought on or before 31 March 2026 is refundable up to 28 days after expiry for up to £5 a ticket; one bought on or after 1 April 2026 is refundable only until 23:59 the day before it becomes valid, and not at all if bought on the day of travel. Refunds caused by disruption carry no admin fee.

Is LNER Perks worth joining?

It pays £5 on joining and 2% of ticket spend back as credit, which on the £27.60 Advance single to Newcastle is 55p a journey — so roughly £1,380 of spend before the credit covers another single. The joining £5 can only be used on an Advance bought in the app costing more than £5.15, and credit expires 24 months after your most recent booking. If you travel in a group of three or more, the automatic 20% Advance discount is worth considerably more than the 2%.

Who owns LNER?

London North Eastern Railway Limited, company number 04659712, is registered at West Offices, Station Rise, York YO1 6GA, and its website terms are governed by the law of England. The Department for Transport's own published table of rail ownership lists LNER as a public sector operator, one of nine of the department's fourteen contracted operators in that position, held through the government's public sector rail owning group ahead of the creation of Great British Railways.

Our verdict

LNER is the operator that rebuilt its own fares, and whether that suits you depends on how you buy. Book an Advance direct, in advance, and you pay no fee, get a seat, and on April 2026 figures have a 94.9% chance of paying under half the Anytime price — £27.60 to Newcastle at the sharp end. Turn up on the day on the London to Newcastle or Edinburgh route and the Off-Peak you wanted has gone, leaving a demand-priced Advance or a £200.80 ceiling. The compensation terms are good and openly published; the refund terms got meaner on 1 April 2026; and the Department for Transport's own passengers, asked in October 2026, called the new structure unfair by 47% to 32%.
