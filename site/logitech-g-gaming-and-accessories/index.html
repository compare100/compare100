Logitech G: What Its Own Shop Charges, Covers and Repairs

Rates and terms checked 15 November 2026 · Gaming and Accessories · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Logitech G lists 142 products in its own British shop and prints a price on every one of them, from an £8.99 G240 cloth mouse pad to a £999.00 three-camera Mevo Start kit. Standard delivery is free above £29.00 and express above £99.00, returns run for 30 days at no charge, and 20 original spare parts are sold separately from £2.00.

Gaming is the largest thing this company does. In the three months to 30 September 2026 it took $349m of Logitech's $1,219m of net sales — 28.6% of the total, ahead of keyboards and combos at $246m and pointing devices at $230m — and the chief executive, Hanneke Faber, named it one of three lines carrying growth that quarter. Every product page also carries a number hardly anyone else in the trade publishes: a full lifecycle carbon figure, 7.1 kg CO2e for the wired G502 X and 8.28 kg for the PRO X SUPERLIGHT 2.

One thing to know before you reach the checkout, because it decides who you deal with if something goes wrong. The shop is run for Logitech by another company: its own help centre calls it “The Logitech B2C online store by Global-e” and says the charge on your card will read “Global-e//Logitech”. The warranty behind the goods is given by Logitech, Inc. of 3930 North First Street, San Jose, California. The trade marks belong to Logitech Europe S.A. in Lausanne. Logitech UK Limited does exist — company 02133170, Morgan House, Madeira Walk, Windsor, on the register since 20 May 1987 — but it is registered for engineering design, advertising and market research, not for retail.

Ten shelves, 142 products, and the price on every one

Counted off the shop's own category pages on 15 November 2026, the range breaks down as 37 racing wheels and pedals, 21 gaming mice, 19 keyboards, 19 headsets and audio products, 12 cameras and lighting items, 11 flight and farm simulation controls, eight microphones, seven mouse pads, four gamepads and four pieces of furniture. Every listing carries a number, which is not something every gaming retailer manages.

The entry prices by shelf are £8.99 for the G240 cloth mouse pad, £18.99 for the F310 wired gamepad, £43.99 for the G402 Hyperion Fury mouse, £44.99 for the G335 headset, £49.00 for the Playseat Trophy gearshift holder, £49.99 for both the G213 Prodigy keyboard and the YETI ORB microphone, £54.99 for the flight sim throttle quadrant and £39.99 for the Litra Glow light. At the other end sit the £999.00 Mevo Start three-pack, the PRO Racing Wheel at £789.99 to £869.99 depending on configuration, the RS50 McLaren Racing bundle at £669.99 to £759.99, and the Playseat Formula Instinct McLaren Racing Edition at £569.99.

The discounting is real and it is dated to the day you look at it. The G920/G29 racing system was £219.99 with “£90.00 off” marked against £309.99; the G502 X PLUS wireless mouse £104.99 against £129.99; the G502 X LIGHTSPEED £89.99 against £109.99; the G515 RAPID TKL keyboard £99.99 against £129.99; the ASTRO A50 Gen 5 headset £209.99 against £259.99; the X56 H.O.T.A.S. £194.99 against £219.99; the Farming Simulator heavy equipment bundle £174.99 against £219.99; and the F310 gamepad £18.99 against £30.99, which the page labels 38% off. Stacked on top of all that the home page was running £30 off orders of two or more items, £50 off three or more, a further £5 off anything that is not black with the code COLORS, 50% off RS pedals bought with an RS50 base or system under RSP50OFF, and a free copy of Call of Duty: MW4 for PC with qualifying G5, ASTRO or PRO Series purchases over £239. No minimum basket value is printed beside the two multi-item offers.

Work out the delivery before you choose. The cheapest thing in the shop costs £8.99 and free standard delivery starts at £29.00, so you have to spend £20.01 more than the item to stop paying for the van — and eleven G240 mouse pads come to £98.89, a penny short of the £99.00 that makes express delivery free.

The carbon number on the product page, and what sits behind it

Logitech prints a lifecycle carbon figure on individual product pages in kilograms of CO2e: 7.1 kg on the £59.99 wired G502 X, 8.28 kg on the £139.99 PRO X SUPERLIGHT 2. The lighter, dearer mouse carries the larger footprint by 1.18 kg, about 17% more, which is the sort of comparison the label exists to make possible and which no retailer's listing lets you make.

The methodology page says the figure represents “the total greenhouse gas emissions across its full lifecycle”, that it “follows international standards ISO 14067 and ISO 14044”, and that four stages are counted: sourcing and manufacturing, transportation and storage, in-use, and end-of-life. It is candid about the weak link — “we can't know exactly how every customer uses their product”, so the in-use share rests on use-case scenarios rather than measurement.

Around it sits the rest of the environmental paperwork: “Today, 81% of Logitech products use Next Life Plastics”, science-based targets validated by the SBTi, an EcoVadis Gold rating, a place on the CDP Climate A List, and a commitment that “by the end of 2030, we will reduce our Scope 1 and 2 emissions to near zero”. Free recycling is offered and the repair partnership with iFixit is set out in terms.

Two things are missing from all of it. The methodology is described as “third-party reviewed” and the third party is never named, there is no uncertainty range on any figure and no schedule for revising one. And the Scope 3 target is written twice without being written the same way: one passage gives a 50% reduction “compared to 2021”, another says “more than half” against no baseline year at all. A reader has to pick.

Twenty spare parts, from £2 mouse feet to a £89.99 brake module

Most makers of gaming hardware will sell you a new one. Logitech G runs a spare parts shop with 20 listings and real prices, which is the most practical thing on the whole site. A set of G502 mouse feet is £2.00 — 3.3% of the £59.99 the mouse itself costs. Mouse feet for the G403, G603 and G703 are £4.00. A 10g weight and weight door for the G403 is £4.00 and a G502 weight box £5.00. Cables run £5.00 for the two-channel input lead, £7.99 to £9.99 for the PC splitter, £8.00 for the 3.5mm PC cable, £8.99 for the 2.5m PRO USB cable and £9.99 for USB-A to USB-C. Wireless receivers are £10.00 for the F710, £15.00 for the USB nano receiver that serves the MK270, MK320, MK330 and MK345 desktop combos, and £24.99 for the PRO LIGHTSPEED receiver.

The racing gear is where this gets genuinely useful, because those are the parts that wear out. A PRO spring set is £8.99, PRO brake elastomers £17.99, a PRO table clamp £17.99, a PRO gas module £59.99, a PRO clutch module £59.99, a PRO load cell brake module £89.99 and a 300W PRO power adapter £89.99. That brake module is 28% of the £319.99 the complete PRO Racing Pedals cost, so a failed load cell is a repair rather than a replacement. The sustainability pages back it up: “original manufacturer spare parts and quality step-by-step instruction guides are available” in more than 60 countries, through the iFixit partnership.

Three qualifications, and they matter if you are buying a £789.99 wheel on the strength of it. Seven of the twenty parts were out of stock on 15 November 2026, including both pedal modules, the power adapter and the PS5 HDMI adapter. No page anywhere says how long parts for a given product will be stocked — the nearest statement is that “making simple repairs with original replacement parts extends your device's life and reduces waste”, which is about the device rather than the supply. And the spare parts tool on Logitech's own support site, which is where a reader starting from a broken product would land, answers “there are no Spare Parts available for this Product” with all three of its dropdown menus empty.

The refurbished shelf, with the new price printed next to it

Eleven refurbished products were listed on 15 November 2026, and the shop deserves credit for putting the comparison on the page itself rather than leaving a shopper to find it. Each unit is “tested to ensure it's 100% functional”, cleaned, inspected, updated to the latest firmware and sent out “in new brown box packaging with all standard accessories and manuals included”. Anything with a tear is thrown out, though minor cosmetic blemishes are expected to remain.

Now the arithmetic, which is where the shelf stops being uniform. A refurbished PRO X SUPERLIGHT 2 mouse is £99.99 against £139.99 new — £40.00 off, 28.6%, and the best saving on the shelf. Refurbished PRO Racing Pedals are £269.99 against £319.99, so £50.00 and 15.6%. A refurbished G923 racing system is £229.00 against a new one starting at £249.99, which is £20.99 and 8.4%. The refurbished PRO Racing Wheel is £749.99 against £789.99 for the cheapest new configuration — £40.00, or 5.1% off a wheel costing the better part of £800. The refurbished ASTRO A50 X is £299.00 and the page itself prints £309.99 beside it, a saving of £10.99 on a £310 headset, which is 3.5%.

And one listing has no saving in it at all. A refurbished PRO X 2 LIGHTSPEED headset is £169.99. A new PRO X 2 LIGHTSPEED headset, on the same site on the same day, is also £169.99. Both pages even show the same £199.99 struck through. The refurbished page states a one-year warranty; the new one states no warranty length at all, which is the next section.

A warranty document that will not say how long it lasts

Logitech's Limited Hardware Warranty is a short, clearly written document, and the one thing it does not contain is a number. The period, it says, is “identified on your product package and/or at www.logitech.com”. It runs “from the date of purchase (or delivery as may be required in certain jurisdictions)”. The remedies are “(1) to repair or replace the hardware, or (2) to refund the price paid”, and a replacement is covered “for the remainder of the original warranty period or thirty (30) days, whichever is longer”. Excluded are “accident, abuse, misapplication, or any unauthorized repair, modification or disassembly”, “normal wear and tear” and “non-Logitech branded parts and accessories even if sold with the Logitech hardware product” — that last one worth reading twice if the box contained a bundled cable or a licence.

The document then does something that reads oddly from Britain in 2026. It writes a floor into the contract for one territory only: “in the European Union any warranty period less than two years shall be increased to two years”. Great Britain is not named in that sentence, in that document, or anywhere near it. The warrantor named is Logitech, Inc. of San Jose, California, with a separate clause naming Logitech Europe S.A. in Lausanne for Australian consumers. There is no effective date and no version number on the page; the only date on it is a “©2026 Logitech” copyright line.

So how long is a new Logitech G mouse covered for in the UK? Neither of the two product pages read for this review mentions a warranty at all — not in the specifications, not under the “Materials and Sustainability” heading, which on the G502 X page has no content beneath it. The order FAQs do not say. The gaming support category links to a page labelled “Warranty Information” and states no terms. The only warranty figure Logitech publishes anywhere a buyer can read before paying is on the refurbished page, which promises “the same 1-year warranty and Logitech support you'd get from one of our new products”. One year, then, on the company's own account — and a European buyer of the identical mouse has two written into the same document.

What does not depend on any of that is the statutory position. Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015 goods have to be of satisfactory quality, and section 20 gives a right to reject with a refund “without undue delay, and in any event within 14 days” of the trader agreeing to one. The warranty says as much itself: “such rights are not affected by the warranties in this Limited Warranty”. A manufacturer's guarantee is extra, not a substitute, and on a £789.99 wheel the difference is worth knowing before you ring anyone.

Delivery and returns: two thresholds, one currency slip

The good part first, because it is genuinely better than the law requires. Returns run for 30 days and the product pages say “free returns within 30 days of your purchase”. Regulation 30(3) of the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 gives 14 days from the day the goods come into your physical possession, so Logitech has more than doubled the window and paid the postage. Buy between 15 November and 23 December and the window stretches to 31 January. In-stock orders usually ship in one to two business days, standard delivery takes four to seven days and express two to three.

Three things about the small print. An in-stock order can be cancelled online “within 30 minutes” of being placed and after that only by contacting an agent, with pre-orders agent-only throughout. Logitech reserves the right to “limit the quantity of product per order, address, and/or individual customer”. And the shipping cut-off is published in Eastern Standard Time — orders placed before 3:00pm EST usually ship the next business day — which for a British buyer is 8pm in winter.

The charge below the two free-delivery thresholds is not published anywhere this review could find: not on the product pages, not on the category pages, not in the order FAQs, and there is no delivery page in the site's own sitemap to look at. The FAQs say only that “shipping rates, available methods, and free-shipping thresholds vary by destination”, which means the number appears for the first time at the checkout. The threshold itself also gets written two ways: the British shop prints “free standard delivery on orders over £29.00” on every page, while the support article sitting behind it says “standard shipping is free for orders over $29 USD”. The same 29, with the currency swapped.

Refunds are the one figure this review will not print, because Logitech publishes two and they do not agree. The order FAQs say the refund credit appears “within 7 business days” of Logitech receiving the returned product. The support article says Logitech will “send it to the credit card-issuing bank within 3 weeks of receipt of the returned product”, after which the bank decides. Those are the two pages; a reader should plan for the longer one. Neither page says who pays the postage on a change of mind as opposed to a fault, which the product pages answer and the FAQs do not.

Four companies, one logo, and a register full of namesakes

Buying from the maker usually means a short chain. Here it has four links. The shop is operated by Global-e, named in Logitech's own help article as the operator of “the Logitech B2C online store” and as the source of the “Global-e//Logitech” descriptor that lands on your statement. No company name, address or registration number for that operator appears anywhere on the Logitech G site, so a shopper who wants to know who holds their money cannot look it up from what is published. The hardware warranty is Logitech, Inc.'s, at 3930 North First Street, San Jose. The brand and the trade marks belong to Logitech Europe S.A., at the Daniel Borel Innovation Center, EPFL, CH-1015 Lausanne. The listed parent reports from both Lausanne and San Jose.

The British company is the odd one out. Logitech UK Limited, company 02133170, has been on the register since 20 May 1987, when it was incorporated as Jewelsabre Limited; it became Logi (U.K) Limited on 22 June 1988, Logi (U.K.) Limited on 24 January 1989 and Logitech UK Limited on 17 February 2003. It is active, registered at Morgan House, Madeira Walk, Windsor SL4 1EP, filed accounts to 31 March 2025 with the next set due by 31 December 2026 — and its four recorded activities are engineering design for industrial process and production (71121), other engineering activities (71129), advertising agencies (73110) and market research and public opinion polling (73200). Not retail, not wholesale. A search of the register for “logitech” returns 46 companies, almost all of them unconnected recruiters, freight agents and IT shops that happen to share the word, so pick the number rather than the name if you ever need to check one.

The only contract the site links from its footer is a Terms of Use at logitech.com, last updated in January 2022. It is “governed by the laws of the United States and by the laws of the State of California”, requires that “all claims and actions must be brought in the appropriate state or federal court located in Santa Clara County, California”, and sets a one-year deadline after a claim arises to file it. There is no terms-of-sale document in the site's own sitemap at all. For a British consumer none of that displaces the Consumer Rights Act, which applies to the sale whatever the website says about California, but it does mean the document on offer is not the one that governs the purchase.

Where the paperwork is unusually good is compliance. Logitech runs a per-model document search at logitech.com/compliance covering RoHS statements, REACH declarations, TSCA Title VI compliance, a product SCIP numbers list, an end-of-life recycling declaration, battery safety and recycling instructions, LED and laser safety information and the India E-Waste Management Rules. Enter a model or part number, choose a country and it returns the documents. That is more than most manufacturers publish. The landing page does not mention UKCA, CE or FCC marking anywhere, and the country selector marks the United States with an asterisk, so a British buyer needs the model number in hand before it tells them anything.

How much of Logitech is actually gaming

The figures come from the company's own quarterly release of 27 October 2026, for the quarter ended 30 September 2026. Net sales were $1.22 billion, up 3% in dollars and 2% in constant currency. GAAP operating income was $201m, up 5%; non-GAAP operating income $231m, up 0.4%. GAAP gross margin was 44.9%, up 150 basis points on the year, and non-GAAP 45.2%, up 140. Guidance for the following quarter is net sales of −5% to 0% year on year in constant currency, with non-GAAP operating income of $220m to $255m.

Inside that, gaming took $349m against $323m a year earlier — $26m more, an increase of 8.0% — and it is the largest of the eight categories the company reports, ahead of keyboards and combos ($246m), pointing devices ($230m), video collaboration ($188m), webcams ($76m), tablet accessories ($58m), headsets ($46m) and everything else ($25m). On the quarter's $1,219m of total net sales that is 28.6%. Two caveats a reader should carry: the company's own footnote says “gaming includes streaming services revenue generated by Streamlabs”, so the line is not purely hardware; and the quarterly series shows how much of this business is Christmas, with the category at $483m in the December 2025 quarter against $292m in the March 2026 quarter that followed — 1.65 times as much in three months.

What that means for a shopper is simple enough. The range is not a sideline the company might quietly drop; it is the biggest thing on the list, which is the best available reason to believe the spare parts shop and the firmware updates will still be there in three years. It also explains the discounting: the quarter you are buying in is the one the whole year turns on.

Common questions

How long is the warranty on Logitech G gear bought in the UK?

Logitech does not publish the figure where a buyer can see it. Its Limited Hardware Warranty says the period is “identified on your product package and/or at www.logitech.com” and gives no number; the two product pages read for this review mention no warranty at all. The only figure the company publishes anywhere is on its refurbished page, which refers to “the same 1-year warranty and Logitech support you'd get from one of our new products”. The same document writes a two-year minimum into the contract for European Union buyers and does not name Great Britain. Separately from any guarantee, the Consumer Rights Act 2015 applies to the sale, and the warranty itself confirms that “such rights are not affected”.

Who am I actually buying from on the Logitech G site?

The shop is operated by Global-e. Logitech's own support article calls it “the Logitech B2C online store by Global-e” and says the charge on your card will show as “Global-e//Logitech”. The warranty is given by Logitech, Inc. of San Jose, California, and the trade marks belong to Logitech Europe S.A. in Lausanne. Logitech UK Limited, company 02133170 in Windsor, is registered for engineering design, advertising and market research rather than retail. No registered name, address or company number for the shop's operator is published on the site.

What does delivery cost?

Nothing above £29.00 for standard and nothing above £99.00 for express — both printed on every product page. Below those thresholds the charge is not published: not on the product pages, not in the order FAQs, and the site has no delivery page in its own sitemap, so the figure first appears at the checkout. Standard delivery is quoted at four to seven days and express at two to three, with in-stock orders shipping in one to two business days. Note that the support article behind the British shop states the same threshold as “$29 USD”.

Can I return something I have already opened?

Yes. The window is 30 days and the product pages say returns are free, which is more than twice the 14 days regulation 30(3) of the 2013 distance selling regulations requires. Anything bought between 15 November and 23 December can go back until 31 January. On the refund timescale Logitech publishes two different answers — “within 7 business days” of receipt in its order FAQs, and sent to the card issuer “within 3 weeks of receipt” in its support article — so plan for the longer one. An order itself can only be cancelled online within 30 minutes of being placed; after that you have to contact an agent.

Are the refurbished Logitech G products worth buying?

It depends entirely on which one. The refurbished PRO X SUPERLIGHT 2 at £99.99 against £139.99 new is £40.00 off, 28.6%, and the PRO Racing Pedals at £269.99 against £319.99 save £50.00. At the other end the ASTRO A50 X at £299.00 saves £10.99 on £309.99 — 3.5% — the PRO Racing Wheel saves 5.1%, and a refurbished PRO X 2 headset at £169.99 costs exactly what the new one costs on the same day. Every unit is tested, cleaned and firmware-updated and arrives in brown box packaging with the standard accessories, and the page states a one-year warranty. Do the subtraction on the specific product before ordering.

Can I buy spare parts and repair Logitech G hardware myself?

For some products, yes, and the prices are modest: G502 mouse feet £2.00, a PRO spring set £8.99, brake elastomers £17.99, a clutch or gas module £59.99 each, a load cell brake module £89.99, receivers £10.00 to £24.99 and cables £5.00 to £9.99. Logitech works with iFixit on step-by-step guides and says original parts are available in more than 60 countries. Two warnings: seven of the twenty listings were out of stock when checked, and no page states how long parts for any product will be stocked. The spare parts tool on Logitech's support site is not the place to start — it returns “there are no Spare Parts available for this Product” with its menus empty.

What is the carbon figure on the product page?

It is the product's full lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions in kilograms of CO2e — 7.1 kg for the wired G502 X, 8.28 kg for the PRO X SUPERLIGHT 2 — counting sourcing and manufacturing, transportation and storage, use, and end-of-life. Logitech says the method follows ISO 14067 and ISO 14044 and has been “third-party reviewed”, without naming the reviewer, and is open that the in-use share rests on use-case scenarios because “we can't know exactly how every customer uses their product”. There is no uncertainty range published and no schedule for revising a figure, so treat it as a comparison between Logitech products rather than an audited absolute.

What is the cheapest thing in the shop?

The G240 cloth mouse pad at £8.99, the lowest price of the 142 products across the ten category pages. The cheapest gamepad is the F310 at £18.99, the cheapest mouse the G402 Hyperion Fury at £43.99, the cheapest headset the G335 at £44.99 and the cheapest keyboard the G213 Prodigy at £49.99. Spare parts start lower still, at £2.00 for a set of mouse feet. Bear in mind that free standard delivery starts at £29.00, so on an £8.99 order you pay a delivery charge the site does not publish until the checkout.

Our verdict

Buying from the company that designed the thing is worth doing here for two reasons that have nothing to do with price: the £2.00 to £89.99 spare parts shelf, which turns a failed pedal or a worn mouse foot into a repair, and the carbon figure printed on the product page, which no retailer gives you. The range is wide and fully priced, 142 products from £8.99 to £999.00, the discounts are real on the day you look at them, and 30 days of free returns beats the 14 the law asks for. What Logitech G does not do well is tell you the terms before you commit: no warranty length on any product page, a two-year floor written for the European Union and not for Britain, a delivery charge that first appears at the checkout, two different refund timescales on its own pages, and a shop operated by a company it never identifies. Price the racing and simulation gear here against a retailer, because that is where the spare parts matter most and where this shop earns its margin; for a £44.99 headset or a £49.99 keyboard, check whether a shop with a named warranty and a published delivery charge is any dearer first.
