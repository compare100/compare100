Lookfantastic Review: 416 Brands, a £5.95 Delivery Year and the Value on the Box

Rates and terms checked 15 November 2026 · Health and Beauty · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Lookfantastic’s own A to Z carried 416 brands on 15 November 2026, from 111SKIN and Aesop at one end to Zelens and Zooki at the other. UK delivery is free once an order passes £25; a 12-month Premier Delivery subscription costs £5.95 and lowers that bar to £10; and an order placed before 1am still goes out for next-day delivery. Unwanted items have 30 days to come back.

The shop is older than most of the brands it sells. The company behind the name, LOOKFANTASTIC.COM.LTD, was incorporated on 2 March 1998 and has carried that name since 19 April 1999; it is still active, registered at Icon 1, 7-9 Sunbank Lane, Ringway, Altrincham WA15 0AF. Its owner, the listed group THG, calls it “the world’s number one online pure-play retailer for prestige beauty products” and puts its beauty division’s reach at “more than 1,300 premium brands” sold “into 195 territories, with leading positions in the UK, US, and Europe”. A shopper can read the audited accounts of the company taking the money, which is not true of most beauty websites.

One rule decides what a basket can be undone. Nothing goes back once it has been opened unless it is faulty: the returns policy asks for items in “pristine condition”, unopened and unused with seals intact, and no exchanges are offered at all. The 30-day window on sealed goods is better than the law requires. What the policy does not print is the price of a UK return label, and that is the one figure a reader looking at a £12 mascara would want before buying it.

What £5.95 a year actually moves

Premier Delivery is the cheapest thing on the site and the easiest to work out. It costs £5.95 for 12 months and, in the shop’s own words, gives “12 months of unlimited FREE delivery on orders over £10”. Standard free delivery starts at £25. So the subscription does one thing: it takes £15 off the basket you need to build before postage stops being charged, every time you order, for a year. At under 50p a month that is a small bet.

It is a bet with one figure missing, though. Whether £5.95 pays for itself depends on what a single delivery costs without it, and that price is not published anywhere this review could read. Three of the shop’s own delivery pages were opened on 15 November 2026 and not one carried a delivery table; two of the three answered with nothing but the promotional banner and a meta description reading “Free delivery available!”. The break-even therefore has to stay a conditional: a single order under £25 whose postage would have cost more than £5.95 pays for the whole year, and any smaller charge needs two or more such orders before the subscription earns its keep. A shopper who places one large order a quarter should not buy it.

One thing a reader should check for themselves at the basket. Every Lookfantastic page read on 15 November 2026 carried the same banner price of £5.95, but search engine listings of those same pages still show the subscription at £9.90 and at £6.50, and the index also holds a test path for those Premier Delivery pages. That pattern is consistent with the price being varied between visitors rather than with any of the three being wrong, but this review could only verify the £5.95 it was shown, so treat the number on your own screen as the one that counts.

416 brands, counted by hand, and the price that will not load

The brand list is the product here, so it was counted rather than estimated: 416 entries on the A to Z on 15 November 2026. The spread is what the count is worth. At the prestige end are La Mer, Clé de Peau Beauté, TOM FORD, Augustinus Bader, Natura Bissé and Noble Panacea. The pharmacy shelf is there too — CeraVe, La Roche-Posay, Avène, Eucerin, Neutrogena, Acnecide and Regaine. And the Korean range is deep rather than token: Beauty of Joseon, CosRX, Anua, Numbuzin, SKIN1004, TIRTIR, Mixsoon, Medicube, Round Lab, ISNTREE, Celimax, HaruHaru and Dr. Althea all have their own listings.

Two details in the list are worth noticing. The letter M alone carries 37 of the 416, and it is where the professional haircare sits — Matrix, Moroccanoil, Mielle Organics, Mylee, MAKE UP FOR EVER. And one entry in the alphabet is not a brand at all: “Our Brands” sits between ORDO and Osmology by Aery, which is the shop’s own label hub filed alphabetically among the names it competes with.

What could not be read is any price. Fifteen Lookfantastic addresses were opened on 15 November 2026 — the home page, the brand index, the returns policy, four terms pages, three delivery pages and two Beauty Box pages among them — and not one of them carried a price. Navigation, menus, banners and meta descriptions all arrive; the product page for the November box returned its title and “Worth over £60” and no amount payable at all. The prices are fetched by the browser afterwards. For a shopper that is invisible, because a browser is what they are using. For this page it means one honest limitation: no item price here is quoted from the shop, because none could be read, and the figures that are quoted are the ones the shop puts in its own banners, policies and product titles.

“Worth over £60” is a claim with rules behind it

The Beauty Box is the subscription Lookfantastic is best known for, and it is sold on a value claim rather than a price. The November edition is titled “The November Beauty Box (Worth Over £60)”. Elsewhere in the same navigation sit “The Mask Edit (Worth Over £85)” and “The Beauty Vault (Worth Over £760)”. Those are the shop’s own words, read on 15 November 2026, and they are the whole proposition: pay a monthly amount, receive products said to be worth several times more.

Claims of that shape are not unregulated. The Chartered Trading Standards Institute’s Guidance for Traders on Pricing Practices, last reviewed in April 2026 and free to read, deals with the comparison a “worth” figure depends on. At 17.3 it says a recommended retail price “must not be false; it must not be created purely in order to present the appearance of a discount”, and it expects a trader to hold substantiation from the supplier or manufacturer behind it. Section 20 adds that pre-printed value claims on a pack “should be objectively accurate and justifiable”. Section 17 also treats a comparison as less likely to comply where it “is made for a materially longer period than the higher price was offered”.

None of that makes a £60 claim wrong, and a box of six deluxe minis from brands charging £30 for a full size can genuinely add up. It does give a reader a test that takes thirty seconds once the box arrives. Take each item, find the full-size price and volume on the same website, and work out the pro-rata value of the millilitres actually in the box. A 10ml decant of a £60 50ml serum is £12 of product, not £60 of it. Add the results and compare the total with the word “worth”. If the sum works, the claim is doing exactly what it says.

What this review cannot tell you is what the box costs. The monthly price, the upfront alternative, the date payment is taken and the notice needed to stop it are all behind the same unreadable layer as every other price on the site, and the FAQ pages for the box returned no figures either. The navigation offers “Pay Monthly” and “Pay Upfront” and no amounts. Anyone signing up to a recurring payment should read the renewal terms on the checkout page itself, because this page could not.

Three sunscreens in the whole history of the register, and one brand this shop sells

Beauty has a regulator with a public scoreboard, and it is the most useful thing a shopper in this category can be shown. The Office for Product Safety and Standards publishes every cosmetic alert, report and recall at gov.uk, searchable by keyword. Searched for sunscreen on 15 November 2026 it returned exactly 3 results in its entire history — which says more about how rarely a sunscreen is formally recalled in Great Britain than about any one brand.

The most serious of the three is the one worth reading. Ultra Violette Lean Screen SPF50+, reference 2510-0095, alert date 15 October 2025, risk recorded as serious. The reason is one sentence: “The product has failed SPF testing with variable results, placing consumers at risk of sunburn.” Country of origin Australia, notified by Local Authority Trading Standards, and the corrective action is that “the product has been recalled from end users”, with owners told to stop using it immediately and to register for a refund through the manufacturer’s own website. The record lists 22 affected batch codes, from A1399 to A2550, which is the level of detail that lets somebody check the tube in their bathroom rather than guess.

The other two are Bondi Sands Zinc Mineral Sunscreen SPF50+ in the body and face lotions, references 2512-0024 and 2602-0295, alert date 18 March 2026, with the risk level left as “Not provided”; and a Bubble Cosmetics Sunscreen Pack, reference 2607-0138, dated 17 August 2026 and rated high risk. Bondi Sands is on Lookfantastic’s A to Z, read the same day. Ultra Violette is not stocked, and nor is Bubble. A brand appearing on a shop’s shelf and on the register is not a mark against the shop — the register exists because recalls work, and a retailer the size of this one will be on it again.

It matters here for a practical reason. A recalled product is a faulty product, and the returns policy’s own carve-out for faulty goods is the one route that beats the unopened-only rule. A sunscreen half used in August goes back under a recall when the same bottle, simply unwanted, would not. Checking the register before accepting that an opened product cannot be returned is free, and nobody does it.

The 30 days, the 14 days and the seal

The statutory floor for anything bought online is the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, which give 14 days from delivery to cancel for any reason. Lookfantastic’s 30 days is more than twice that, and the shop deserves the credit for it: it is a real improvement on the law, not a restatement of it.

The seal is where the two rulebooks meet, and the law is narrower than most beauty policies imply. Regulation 28(1)(e) removes the cancellation right for sealed goods that are not suitable for return on health protection or hygiene grounds — but only where they have been unsealed after delivery. An unopened foundation with its seal intact therefore keeps its full cancellation right, sale item or not, and that is precisely the case Lookfantastic’s own policy accepts. Where the two part company is the opened item: the law lets the shop refuse it, and the shop does.

Three practical details sit around that. Refunds are processed in 3-5 working days from the date the return is received, the money takes up to 5 working days more to reach an account, and a customer who has waited 10 working days after the confirmation email is told to contact customer service. No exchanges are offered on any item, so a wrong shade means a refund and a second order rather than a swap. And the policy page carries no price for a UK return label at all, which leaves a shopper unable to work out whether returning a £10 item is worth the postage.

If a refund goes wrong, the route out is thinner than in financial services. Retail has no ombudsman. The nearest thing is RetailADR, approved under the ADR for Consumer Disputes (Competent Authorities and Information) Regulations 2015 and free to consumers — and its published member list carried no Lookfantastic, THG, Cult Beauty or Glossybox entry on 15 November 2026. That leaves the card scheme’s chargeback rules, or Citizens Advice and Trading Standards, and on a basket under £100 it also rules out a section 75 claim, since that section requires a cash price above £100.

The change worth knowing about is to the oldest name in the group. On 30 October 2025 it was renamed FIC SHARECO LIMITED. It is still active, still at the same address, and still filing — last accounts to 31 December 2025, confirmation statement dated 19 March 2026 — but the name a shopper might have seen at the bottom of a beauty checkout no longer exists as such. None of that affects a delivery. It does mean the entity name in the footer is worth a glance at the checkout rather than an assumption, because in this group the names move and the register is the only place that records when.

An upheld ruling about an Instagram story, and what it was about

The clearest independent judgement on this retailer is an Advertising Standards Authority ruling of 25 September 2024 against “THG Beauty Ltd t/a Look Fantastic”, and it was upheld. The advertisement was an Instagram story posted on 5 November 2023 on the account of the influencer Erim Kaur, labelled “Fragrance”, reading “4 long-lasting perfumes for Broke Girlies *all under £50”, with a clickable link captioned “CHEAP & LONG LASTING PERFUMES” that led to her affiliate shop page.

The ASA challenged whether the story was obviously identifiable as a marketing communication. It found that it was a marketing communication for the purposes of the Code, and that it was not obviously identifiable as one: the “Paid links” disclosure appeared only after a reader had already engaged with the link, and “in the absence of a clear and prominent identifier such as ‘ad’” the commercial intent was not clear from the outset. The ruling records a breach of CAP Code (Edition 12) rules 2.1, 2.3 and 2.4, recognition of marketing communications. The advertisement “must not appear again in the form investigated”, and the retailer, a second company named alongside it and the influencer were all told to make future advertisements obviously identifiable.

It is a disclosure failure rather than a safety or a pricing one, and the perfumes were presumably exactly as described. It is still the only adjudication of its kind on this shop, and it is a fair thing to carry in mind on a site that sells heavily through affiliates and creators: a recommendation reaching you through an influencer’s story may be paid for, and the rules say you should be able to tell before you click, not after.

Common questions

Is delivery free at Lookfantastic?

Yes, over £25. The banner carried on every page read on 15 November 2026 offers free UK delivery on orders over £25. A 12-month Premier Delivery subscription at £5.95 lowers that threshold to £10 for the year. The charge on a smaller order without the subscription is not published anywhere the site serves to a plain request, so check it at the basket.

Can I return a beauty product after I have opened it?

Not unless it is faulty. The returns policy asks for items unopened and in pristine condition within 30 days of receipt, and offers refunds rather than exchanges. Two things are worth knowing. An unopened item keeps the full cancellation right the Consumer Contracts Regulations give, because regulation 28(1)(e) only removes it for sealed hygiene goods once they have been unsealed. And a product subject to a safety recall counts as faulty, so it can go back even if it is half used.

What does Premier Delivery include?

In the shop’s own words, “12 months of unlimited FREE delivery on orders over £10 for just £5.95”. Standard free delivery begins at £25, so the subscription takes £15 off the basket needed to avoid postage on every order for a year. Orders placed before 1am still qualify for next-day delivery. Whether it pays for itself depends on the standard delivery charge, which the site does not publish in a readable form.

Who do I complain to if a refund goes wrong?

Customer service first, through the account, and the policy tells a customer to make contact if a refund has not arrived 10 working days after the confirmation email. Beyond that, retail has no ombudsman. RetailADR is the approved scheme for retail disputes, but its member list carried no Lookfantastic or THG entry on 15 November 2026. That leaves a chargeback request through the card issuer, a report to Citizens Advice, which passes complaints to Trading Standards, and the Office for Product Safety and Standards for anything about the product itself.

Is the Beauty Box really worth over £60?

That is the claim on the November 2026 edition, and it can be tested once the box arrives. Price each item pro rata: a 10ml sample of a serum sold at £60 for 50ml is £12 of product. Add the results and compare. The Chartered Trading Standards Institute’s pricing guidance, last reviewed in April 2026, says a recommended retail price “must not be false; it must not be created purely in order to present the appearance of a discount” and that value claims “should be objectively accurate and justifiable”. The subscription price itself could not be read from the site’s own pages.

Our verdict

A deep, well-stocked beauty shop with a genuinely cheap delivery subscription and a returns window twice the length the law demands. The 416 brands are real, counted on the shop’s own A to Z, and the mix of prestige, pharmacy and Korean skincare in one basket is the reason to use it rather than a department store. £5.95 for a year of delivery on orders over £10 is the best-value thing on the site and the easiest decision on this page. Where it falls short is disclosure rather than substance: no price can be read without a browser, the cost of a return label is not published, the “worth over” arithmetic behind the Beauty Box is never shown, and an upheld 2024 ruling on influencer advertising is a reminder to check whether a recommendation is paid for. Buy sealed, keep the box until you are sure, and check the product safety register before accepting that an opened item cannot go back.
