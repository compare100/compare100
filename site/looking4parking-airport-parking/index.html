Looking4.com Airport Parking: Prices, Fees and the Company Behind It

Rates and terms checked 20 November 2026 · Airport Parking · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Eight days of park and ride at Gatwick came out at £47.99 on Looking4’s own price table on 20 November 2026 — £6.00 a day. The same stay at Manchester was £49.73 and at Heathrow £85.99. Ten car parks are priced at each of those airports, and the site says it sells on behalf of “more than 1,200 car parks around the world, at more than 200 airports” in “over 15 countries”.

What sits behind it is unusual for a comparison site, and worth knowing before you book: Looking4.com is the ecommerce business of Manchester Airports Group, which owns Manchester, London Stansted and East Midlands airports. Its registered office is 6th Floor Olympic House, Manchester Airport, M90 1QX — inside the airport. MAG itself is owned by Manchester City Council (35.5%), the IFM Global Infrastructure Fund (35.5%) and the nine other Greater Manchester local authorities (29%) between them, so the money ends up with ten councils and an infrastructure fund rather than a private equity house.

It runs none of the car parks it sells. Its own help pages put it plainly: “Looking4Parking.com does not own or operate any of the car parks that advertise through our website.” The group it belongs to turned over £1,514.7m in the year to 31 March 2026 and handled 66.3m passengers, and the digital division Looking4 sits in grew its revenue from £228.5m to £288.7m across that same year.

Eight days at Gatwick, Heathrow and Manchester

The site publishes a price table on each airport page rather than making you search first, and on 20 November 2026 those tables were priced on a four-day and an eight-day booking in March 2027. At Gatwick the ten listed products ran from £47.99 for eight days at the Cambridge Hotel park and ride up to £81.99 for ACE meet and greet, with Cophall at £48.93 on its Super Saver and £51.95 on its standard park and ride, and Purple Parking at £57.88 and £60.98.

Heathrow is the expensive one. Nothing there came in under £85.99 for eight days, which was Concierge uncovered park and ride; Maple’s Express park and ride for Terminals 2 and 3 was £92.94, Kwik Park meet and greet £92.98, Triangle £92.99 and MBW £132.89. At Manchester the floor was £49.73 on the JetParks Ringway Earlybird, with Airport Parking park and ride at £56.87, Maple uncovered at £52.98 and NCP Flightpath at £58.47.

The “from” prices in the page titles are honest, which is worth saying because they often are not in this trade. Heathrow advertises “from only £10.75 per day” and that is exactly £85.99 divided by eight. Manchester advertises “from only £6.22 per day”, which is £49.73 divided by eight. Gatwick advertises “from £47.99 for 8 days”, which is the row itself. Divide before you accuse: the arithmetic ties up on all three.

One practical gap: these are totals, not daily rates, and a four-day stay is not half the price of an eight-day one. The Cambridge Hotel was £35.99 for four days and £47.99 for eight, so the second half of the fortnight costs £12.00 while the first costs £35.99. JetParks at Manchester is sharper still — £42.93 for four days against £49.73 for eight, which is £6.80 for the extra four days. If your dates are flexible by a day or two, price both.

Manchester's own car parks come sixth on Manchester's own site

This is the test that matters on a comparison site owned by an airport operator, and Looking4 passes it. At Manchester, four products carry the word Official in their names — the airport’s own car parks, owned by the same group that owns the website. They sit sixth, seventh, eighth and ninth of the ten products listed, underneath five cheaper rows.

The gap is not trivial. Official T3 Mid Stay was £71.99 for eight days and Official T3 Multi Storey £81.99, against JetParks at £49.73 and Maple at £52.98. A reader who takes the top of the list over the parent company’s own product saves £22.26. Official T2 West Multi Storey at £107.99 was the most expensive row on the eight-day table. One caveat on the counting: the discount pages call one Manchester product “Official Drop & Go P16” while the results table lists a Drop & Go Express at £65.98 without the word Official, so whether that fifth row is also the airport’s own could not be established.

Gatwick repeats the pattern with the site’s own badge rather than its owner’s. The Looking4 Meet & Greet Super Saver is listed sixth of ten at £66.19 for eight days, below five cheaper rows from Purple Parking, the Cambridge Hotel and Cophall. An agent that puts a rival above its own margin is arguing against itself, and it is the single strongest reason to trust the ordering.

The qualifier is that meet and greet and park and ride are different products, so a straight ranking flatters the cheaper format, and Looking4 does not split the two formats into separate tables. Read the product type in each row before you read the price.

A parking site owned by three airports, and what that pays for

Manchester Airports Holdings Limited reported revenue of £1,514.7m for the year ended 31 March 2026, up 12.8% on £1,342.5m, with operating profit of £303.9m (up 15.5% from £263.2m) and adjusted EBITDA of £614.4m. Passengers were a record 66.3m, up 1.9%. Car park income grew 11.6% and retail income 14.3%. The digital arm, trading as Cavu, went from £228.5m of revenue to £288.7m, and it bought the Dutch parking comparison site Parkos the year before, which is why the group now describes itself as operating in the UK and the Netherlands.

Manchester Airport’s own website sends readers to Looking4 and sweetens it: a page headed “Save money when parking at other airports with Looking4” lists twelve airports — Gatwick, Glasgow, Edinburgh, Liverpool, Luton, Birmingham, Heathrow, Bristol, Leeds Bradford and its own three — and says “They can save you up to 60% if you book in advance and, by clicking the links below, we’ll save you an extra 5%.” The links carry the tracking string code=magsave. That extra 5% is real money and it is the cheapest thing on this page.

What neither MAG page does is say that Looking4 is MAG. A second page names “our trusted UK airport parking partners” as JetParks, Looking4Parking and SkyParkSecure — three brands presented alike, one of which is the airport’s own subsidiary. Nothing on either page discloses the relationship, and both carry only “©2026 Manchester Airport plc” in the footer.

The traffic also runs the other way and goes equally unlabelled. Cavu’s own site lists Cavu Commerce, Cavu Connect and Cavu Lounges, says it was “Founded by a major airport group”, and never names Looking4.com at all; MAG’s page about Cavu names Escape Lounges and the three Cavu products and does not name it either. Looking4’s footer claims the parent — “Looking4.com is part of Manchester Airport Group” — while the parent’s own brand pages do not claim the child. Both statements are the same group’s; the reader is left to join them up.

MAG’s public pages do not agree with each other on scale either. Its home page gives 61.3m passengers, 261 destinations, 58 airlines and £10.6bn contributed to the UK economy, all labelled FY24; its group overview page says “Last year 54m passengers flew through our airports, and we contributed more than £8bn to the UK economy” and dates nothing. The audited figure for the year to 31 March 2026 is 66.3m. Take the annual report and ignore the marketing pages.

The name in the footer left the register in March 2022

Every page on looking4.com ends with the line “© 2026 Looking4Parking Limited. Incorporated in England and Wales. Company Number: 7107772”. Company 07107772 is real, active and filing: it was incorporated on 17 December 2009 as Looking4parking Ltd. It has not been called that since 17 March 2022, when the change-of-name certificate was issued and it became CAVU Ecommerce (EMEA) Limited. The register has carried the new name for four and a half years; the website still prints the old one, and prints it with “Limited” where the register said “Ltd”.

Nothing about this costs the reader anything at the point of booking. It costs them later. If a car comes back scratched and the money has to be chased, the company named in the small print cannot be found on the register under that name, and the entity that took the payment is one the customer has never seen written down. The same pattern on other parking brands has been a pre-pack administration; here it is simply a rebrand into the parent that has not reached the footer.

The rest of the file is orderly. The person with significant control is Travel Parking Group Limited (07235776), holding 75% or more of the shares, notified 6 April 2016 — the date the founders, David Hirst Greenbrown and Martin Mansell, were also first recorded, both ceasing on 21 June 2018. The sole SIC code is 52290, other transportation support activities. Accounts are made up to 31 March 2026, with the next due by 31 December 2027, and the last confirmation statement was dated 30 September 2026.

The board turns over quickly. Nineteen officers have been appointed and seventeen have resigned. Only two directors are in post — Nicholas Graham Woods from 31 December 2025 and Andrew Peter David Withington from 5 May 2026 — and a third, Caroline Anne Exley, was appointed on 31 December 2025 and resigned on 28 April 2026, four months later. For a business taking money for a service delivered months ahead, two directors appointed inside the last twelve months is worth noticing.

Flexible, Supersaver, and the £9.50 only one of them pays

The product name carries the cancellation right, and the cheap ones have none. In the site’s words: “Supersaver, Non-Flexible and Advance Purchase products cannot be amended or cancelled as they are sold at a discounted price to the standard rate for the car park”, and “If your booking states 'NON-FLEX' then your booking CANNOT BE AMENDED OR CANCELLED”. Three of the ten Gatwick products on the page carried NON FLEX in the name, including the cheapest one and the site’s own meet and greet.

Where a booking is flexible, “all flexible bookings can be cancelled up until 48 hours before your drop off time” — and cancelling costs £9.50 unless you bought the optional cancellation cover, which exists to waive that fee. So the choice at checkout is between paying for cover you may not use and paying £9.50 if you do cancel, on a £47.99 booking where £9.50 is a fifth of the price.

Two charges are named but never quantified. The booking fee is “a small charge that contributes towards the operational costs of bringing you our website”, it is non-refundable, and no amount appears anywhere on the site; there is no credit card surcharge on top of it. The levy fee is defined as “a fee levied by the airport to car park operators to access the airport”, also without a figure. Both are the practices the Competition and Markets Authority has been writing to this sector about, so the absence of a number is the criticism, not the fee itself.

Some car parks add their own charges at the barrier, and those are not in the headline price either. The Airtime meet and greet at Heathrow states a £5.30 entry and £5.30 exit fee at airport — £10.60 paid on the day, on top of the booking. Overstaying is charged by the car park on return rather than by the website: “You will be charged a daily rate by the car park on your return”, and “Onsite car parks will charge more than offsite car parks”. Changing the registration number is free.

One line in the help pages closes a door most readers would try first: “Cancellations made with the car park direct, cannot be considered.” You booked through the agent, so you cancel through the agent, inside the 48 hours and on the product you happened to buy. And on the Gatwick product page for the site’s own Super Saver meet and greet, Cancellation cover appears in the feature list while the terms immediately below it say “No cancellations are allowed, and dates can not be amended”. Those are the same page.

Vehicle rules are stricter than the price table suggests. That Gatwick meet and greet takes cars only, the car must fit a “standard sized parking space (2.4m wide x 4.8m long)”, and minibuses or anything with more than 8 passenger seats are refused. Heathrow’s short stay decks are capped at 2.2m at Terminals 2, 3 and 5 and 2.0m at Terminal 4. A roof box or a long wheelbase van decides the product for you.

The clauses the help pages send you to cannot be read

Asked where its cancellation policy lives, the site answers precisely: “Our cancellation policy can be located on our terms and conditions page point 5.1 to 5.7.” Those clauses could not be read. The UK terms page returns a page with navigation and a footer and no terms in it, and so do five of the company’s other national storefronts — Ireland, Cyprus, Greece, New Zealand and South Africa — all returning the same empty template with the same title. Six attempts on six of its own sites produced no contract text at all.

That is a real problem rather than a presentational one, because the contract is the only place the cancellation tiers, the liability position and the fee schedule are written down in full. The help pages give the 48-hour rule and the £9.50; the terms are where clauses 5.1 to 5.7 would say what happens to the booking fee, what the car park is liable for and which law governs the booking. A reader who wants to know what they have agreed to before paying cannot find out.

There is also no telephone. “As we are an online only booking agent, we do not have telephone lines to be able to make your booking over the telephone”, and the office is open Monday to Friday 09:00 to 17:00, excluding bank holidays — so nobody is there at the weekend, which is when most people fly. The reservations team aims to reply within 48 hours, a VAT receipt takes up to 5 working days, and the only phone number a customer gets is the car park’s, printed on the confirmation email. No postal address appears on the help or contact pages.

Damage goes down the same channel as everything else: asked what to do when a car comes back scratched, the answer is “All feedback must be made in writing to us via our contact form.” There is no separate claims route, no published timescale for a damage claim and no escalation step. There is no financial regulator here, no ombudsman and no alternative dispute resolution scheme named anywhere on the site, so what remains is the Consumer Rights Act 2015 duty to provide a service with reasonable care and skill, the common law of bailment from the moment a valet takes the keys, and the county court.

What the independent survey scores, and what it leaves out

Which? surveys this trade properly and published its latest results on 25 June 2026, from an online survey of 5,030 panel members and members of the public carried out in February and March 2026, covering 78 airport car parks. Its Recommended Providers were Sentinel at Leeds Bradford on 86%, APH on 80% at Gatwick and Manchester, Secure Airparks at Edinburgh on 76% and Cophall at Gatwick on 75% for park and ride, with Maple on 76% and Purple Parking on 75% for meet and greet. The worst read was Bristol’s multi-storey at 60%. Which? notes the data is unweighted and may not be representative.

Looking4 is not in it, and that is not a mark against it: Which? scores the car park you actually park in, not the website that sold you the space. Several of its scored names are on the price tables above, so the survey and the ladder can be read together, which is the useful way round: Cophall at 75% appears at Gatwick, Maple at 70% for park and ride appears at Heathrow and Manchester, and JetParks — the Manchester floor price — scores 70%. Purple Parking’s 75% is its meet and greet score, while the Gatwick table lists its park and ride, so read the format as well as the name.

The survey also explains why the agent question matters. In Which?’s own words, “When we investigated rogue airport parking operators, we found agents stealing from vehicles, cars abandoned in fields and companies refusing to take responsibility for damage.” Looking4 says cars are “safe & secure within pre-checked car parks” but does not say what the check is, and does not state whether any listed car park holds the police-backed Safer Parking award, which is the accreditation that attaches to the site rather than to the seller.

The site’s own numbers are respectable and dated. It reports 94% of customers would use the service again and carries a Feefo score of 4.7 out of 5 from 331,473 reviews, with the 2025 Feefo Trusted Service and Gold Trusted Service awards and a bronze in Best Company for Airport Parking at the British Travel Awards 2025. One product, the Airtime meet and greet at Heathrow, carries a separate Reevoo score of 7.8 out of 10 from 19,000 reviews. The Feefo count read 331,420, 331,459 and 331,473 on three pages opened minutes apart, which is a live counter rather than a contradiction.

Five things this review could not settle

The booking fee. It is charged on every booking and it is non-refundable, and no page on the site states how much it is. It cannot be compared against Holiday Extras’ published “no hidden booking fees” position without a figure, and it should be visible in the basket before you pay. Check it there.

The levy fee. Defined, named, and never quantified. Whether it applies to every airport or only to those that charge operators for access is not stated.

Clauses 5.1 to 5.7. The cancellation policy the help pages cite could not be read on six of the company’s own storefronts. Ask for the terms by email before buying a non-refundable product.

The Safer Parking position. No car park in any of the three price tables showed an accreditation, and the site’s “pre-checked” claim is not defined. Ask for the award by name for the specific car park, not for the brand.

What the extra 5% is. Manchester Airport offers 5% off Looking4 bookings made through its own links, and neither company’s page says the two businesses are in the same group. Whether the discount comes out of the airport’s commission or the car park’s price is not published.

Common questions

Who do you actually contract with when you book through Looking4.com?

Not Looking4. Its own help pages say “Looking4Parking.com does not own or operate any of the car parks that advertise through our website”, so the parking is supplied by the operator and Looking4 takes the booking. The company behind the website is CAVU Ecommerce (EMEA) Limited, company 07107772, registered at 6th Floor Olympic House, Manchester Airport M90 1QX, though the site’s footer still prints its former name, Looking4Parking Limited.

Can you cancel a Looking4 booking, and what does it cost?

It depends on the product. “All flexible bookings can be cancelled up until 48 hours before your drop off time”, and doing so costs £9.50 unless you bought the optional cancellation cover that waives the fee. Supersaver, Non-Flexible and Advance Purchase bookings cannot be cancelled or amended at all. Cancelling with the car park instead of with Looking4 does not count: “Cancellations made with the car park direct, cannot be considered.”

Is Looking4 part of Manchester Airport?

Yes. Its footer states “Looking4.com is part of Manchester Airport Group”, it is registered inside Manchester Airport, and its person with significant control is Travel Parking Group Limited (07235776) at 75% or more. Manchester Airports Group owns Manchester, London Stansted and East Midlands airports and is itself owned by Manchester City Council (35.5%), the IFM Global Infrastructure Fund (35.5%) and nine other Greater Manchester local authorities (29%). Its digital division turned over £288.7m in the year to 31 March 2026.

Does Looking4 favour Manchester, Stansted and East Midlands because its owner owns them?

Not in the ordering tested on 20 November 2026. The four products named Official at Manchester — the airport’s own car parks — were sixth, seventh, eighth and ninth of ten, priced from £71.99 for eight days against £49.73 at the top of the list. The site’s own branded meet and greet at Gatwick was likewise sixth of ten. Read the product type in each row, though: park and ride and meet and greet are listed in one table.

What extra charges are not in the price you see?

A non-refundable booking fee of an amount the site does not publish; a “levy fee” it defines but does not quantify; and, at some car parks, charges collected at the barrier — the Airtime meet and greet at Heathrow states £5.30 on entry and £5.30 on exit, a further £10.60 on the day. Returning later than booked is charged at the car park’s daily rate on the way out, and the site notes onsite car parks charge more than offsite ones. There is no credit card surcharge.

What happens if your car is damaged while it is parked?

You write to Looking4 through its web contact form, which is the same route as any other feedback, and there is no published timescale for a damage claim. There is no telephone line, the office runs Monday to Friday 09:00 to 17:00, and no ombudsman or alternative dispute resolution scheme is named anywhere on the site. That leaves the Consumer Rights Act 2015, the law of bailment once a valet has the keys, and the county court. Photograph the car on arrival and on collection.

Our verdict

On price and on ordering, Looking4 did well. Eight days of park and ride came out at £47.99 at Gatwick, £49.73 at Manchester and £85.99 at Heathrow, the “from” figure in each page title turned out to be the cheapest row divided by the nights, and at Manchester the four car parks belonging to the company’s own owner were listed sixth to ninth of ten, £22.26 above the cheapest alternative. An agent owned by an airport group that ranks its parent’s products below cheaper rivals is doing the job it claims to do, and Manchester Airport’s extra 5% makes that route cheaper again.

Two things decide whether it suits you. The first is the product tier: the cheap rows are Supersaver and NON FLEX, which cannot be changed or cancelled at all, and the flexible ones cost £9.50 to cancel inside a 48-hour deadline. The second is what happens if something goes wrong. There is no phone number, no weekend cover, no ombudsman, a non-refundable booking fee of an unpublished size, and a set of terms that would not open on six of the company’s own websites — under a corporate name the register retired in 2022. If you are booking a flexible product well ahead and reading the product type before the price, it is a strong place to buy. If you want a contract you can read and somebody to ring on a Sunday, buy from an operator direct.
