Marks & Spencer Children's Fashion: Uniform, Prices and Delivery

Two M&S school polo shirts cost £4.50 in June 2025, and on 26 October 2026 the company said it will not raise school uniform prices in 2027 — holding them for what it calls “a market-leading seventh year in a row”, unchanged since 2021. Every piece of M&S kidswear also carries a one-year promise: “If any item doesn’t live up to our high standards within 365 days, we’ll make it right with a full refund.” That is a dated, published commitment with a number attached to it, and M&S says it was “the first major UK high street retailer to offer this”.

Something has changed in the background that matters more to a uniform list than any of it. From September 2026, under section 35 of the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026, a school in England may not require a primary pupil to have more than three different branded items of uniform in a school year, and a secondary pupil no more than three — or four if one of them is a tie. The Department for Education’s own guidance spells out what falls outside that count: “a navy skirt or a grey cardigan that can be bought from a range of retailers would not be considered a branded item.” Most of a uniform list is now generic, and generic is what a high street shop sells.

On delivery, the figures on M&S’s own site are £75 and nothing. A banner read on two separate help pages on 20 November 2026 gives “Free delivery when you spend over £75*”, “Free next-day Click and Collect*” and “Free returns for online orders”. The £75 is the highest free-delivery basket of the four children’s clothing retailers covered on this site, and the free collection applies at any order size.

What a uniform list looks like after September 2026

The rule is short and it is worth reading in its own words. Section 35 of the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 (2026 c. 21, the date printed under its chapter line being 29 April 2026) inserts a new section into the Education Act 1996 saying that the appropriate authority of a relevant school in England “may not require a primary pupil at the school to have more than three different branded items of school uniform for use during a school year”, and the same for a secondary pupil, “(or more than four different branded items of school uniform if one of those items is a tie)”. Where the school is a middle school, the limits “apply in relation to all pupils at that school”.

The definitions do the real work. “School uniform” is defined as “a bag and any clothing required for school”, so a PE bag counts. An item is branded if “it has the school name or school logo”, or if, “as a result of its colour, design, fabric or other distinctive characteristic”, “it is only available from particular suppliers”. The Department for Education’s statutory guidance, last updated 6 July 2026, puts the consequence plainly: “a navy skirt or a grey cardigan that can be bought from a range of retailers would not be considered a branded item.”

That guidance states the requirement as a cap “to 3 or fewer”, with the four-item version “if one is a tie” for secondary and middle schools, and says the legal basis is “the Education Act 1996, as amended by the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026”. It also tells schools that “Parents should be able to purchase generic items of uniform from a range of retailers”, that they “should avoid single-supplier contracts unless regular tendering competitions are run”, that “The contract should be re-tendered at least every 5 years”, and that opportunities to buy second-hand uniform must be “freely accessible to all parents” — specifically not only “via paid for events”.

One caveat on the dates. The guidance and the government’s own announcement of 30 April 2026 both put the cap at “From September 2026” and “From this September”, and the announcement words it as a cap “to three (excluding ties)”. The commencement provision inside the Act itself was not read on this check, so the September 2026 date here is the Department’s, not the statute’s. The guidance is issued under a separate and older law, the Education (Guidance about Costs of School Uniforms) Act 2021 (2021 c. 20, dated 29 April 2021), whose section 2(2) reads “This Act comes into force at the end of the period of two months beginning with the day on which it is passed.”

£343 for a primary child, £442 for a secondary one

The government has measured what this costs. The Cost of School Uniforms Survey 2023, carried out by BMG Research and published in September 2024, interviewed “a representative sample of 1,207 parents/carers of children aged 4 to 16 attending state funded schools in England”, by telephone, with main fieldwork “between 27th November 2023 and 8th January 2024 inclusive” and a two-day pilot on 24 and 25 November 2023.

Its headline figure is that “The average total expenditure on school uniform and PE kit combined based on the items required in 2023 was £381.92”, and that “There were higher levels of expenditure for children in secondary schools than in primary schools (£442.25 v £343.28)”. A footnote warns that the combined figure “is not calculated by summing the individual average expenditure on school uniforms and PE kit” and “is calculated at a respondent case level”, which is why the parts below do not add up to it.

Uniform alone came to £249.58, against £279.51 on the earlier comparison — £217.65 for primary boys and £246.80 for primary girls, £266.14 and £289.04 for secondary. PE kit alone was £139.70: £120.10 and £117.09 at primary, £172.03 and £169.06 at secondary. On a like-for-like basis the survey gives £373.24 against an inflation-adjusted £387.44 in 2015, with secondary falling from £460.05 to £432.12 and primary flat at £334.80 to £335.51.

The branding figures are the ones the new cap was built on. “Most parents/carers (86%) reported that at least one item of uniform required branding”, rising to “98% of parents/carers whose child was in secondary school” against “78% of parents/carers of children in primary school”, and “Seven in ten parents/carers (71%) reported that at least one item of PE kit required branding.” The report carries its own disclaimer — “This research report was written before the new UK Government took office on 5 July 2024. As a result, the content may not reflect current Government policy” — and it is three academic years old, so treat it as the scale of the bill rather than this year’s bill.

Seven years of the same price, and what the price actually is

M&S has announced this four times and the four announcements line up, which is rarer than it sounds. On 5 July 2022 it “committed to hold prices on its market leading quality schoolwear”, with “Prices in the M&S range start from just £7 for a multipack of three 100% responsibly sourced cotton unisex polo shirts, featuring StayNEW™ technology to reduce colour loss and bobbling”, and an extra 20% off “for early bird shoppers until 25th July”, bringing the opening price to £5.60. On 1 June 2023 it was “protecting the price of its market leading quality school uniform for a third year”, with the same £7 three-pack and the same £5.60. On 18 June 2025 it was the fifth year, with “Prices in the range start from just £4.50 for a 2 pack of 100% responsibly sourced cotton unisex polo shirts (2-18 years), featuring stain resistant technology.” On 26 October 2026 it became “a market-leading seventh year in a row”. Counting 2021 to 2027 inclusive gives seven years, and third in 2023 and fifth in 2025 both fall where they should.

The pack size changed between those releases, so the obvious suspicion is that the pack shrank while the headline price stayed put. Do the division before making the accusation. £7 for three shirts is £2.33 each; £4.50 for two is £2.25 each. Per shirt the opening price went down by about 8p, not up. The honest caveat is that the two releases describe different fabric treatments — StayNEW™ in 2022 and 2023, “stain resistant technology” in 2025 — so this is not a like-for-like product, and the comparison is approximate rather than exact.

The 2026 release does the same thing deliberately and says so: “Following a 30% increase in online sales of five-pack value lines last year, M&S has expanded its focus on multipacks”, and “Stretch trousers and girls’ jersey trousers have moved into two-packs reducing the cost per item by £2.” It prints no garment prices at all. On fit it gives “up to 3cm of extra length in trouser hems”, “Expandicuff™ sleeves and an easy-close top button on shirts up to age eight”, “an exclusive durable twill fabric” for trousers, skirts and pinafores, and “Extended sizes are also available in trousers and shirts across length, waist and body fit.” Charlotte Davies, Director of Kidswear at M&S, is quoted saying “School uniform is an essential purchase, and we know families are looking closely at every pound they spend.”

What none of the four releases carries is a number behind “market leading”. The 2022 release footnotes a value ranking to YouGov — “M&S schoolwear is ranked #1 by consumers for value for money**”, “**Source: YouGov” — and cites Mintel for the size of the market rather than M&S’s share of it: “Counting the cost: Brits spend £1.2 billion sending kids Back to School”. The 2023 release quotes the company’s own Family Matters Index, that “the cost of living remains a concern for 81% of families”, with “62% of customers placing value as the most important priority”. The 2025 and 2026 releases name no external source for the market-leadership claim at all.

The guarantee that runs for 365 days

The most useful thing M&S publishes about children’s clothes is not in its terms and conditions. It is in a kidswear blog bylined “By Alex Dimitriu, Kidswear Director”, listed on the newsroom index as 29 July 2025 while the page’s own metadata reads 28 July 2025 — both are printed here because neither settles it. The blog states that “every piece of M&S kidswear comes with a one-year quality guarantee”, and that “If any item doesn’t live up to our high standards within 365 days, we’ll make it right with a full refund.” It adds: “We’re proud to be the first major UK high street retailer to offer this.”

A year on a child’s jumper is a long time, and it is the only claim on the shop’s side of the argument with a number and a remedy in it. Set it against the durability language that surrounds it, which has neither: the blog says only that “every item is made well and made to last, tested for durability, and offered at a price parents can feel good about”. No number of washes, no number of wears, no named test standard, nothing a reader could check. On a page about children’s clothes that is the right way round — the testable promise is the refund, and the untestable one is the marketing.

The same blog records that M&S “reduced prices by up to 20% on over 100 products which we consider everyday essentials”, that it “now add[s] special labels in our kids’ jackets with space for three names”, and that the company has seen “children’s jackets dating all the way back to the 1960s” come back to it. The hand-me-down framing runs through all of this material, and the 2025 press release puts it as a test rather than a slogan: uniform “designed to be durable and pass the ‘hand-me-down’ quality test”.

Seventy-five pounds, or nothing at a counter

The delivery figures were read twice, on two different M&S help pages, on 20 November 2026, and both carried the same banner strip: “Free delivery when you spend over £75*”, “Free next-day Click and Collect*” and “Free returns for online orders”. Neither asterisk has a footnote on any page that was read. The next-day promise appears elsewhere as a separate banner — “Order by 10pm, get it tomorrow. Love that.” — again without a term behind it.

Against the rest of this category, £75 is a high bar and the collection offer is a generous one. Amazon’s equivalent is a £15 minimum on Haul or £95 a year for Prime, JoJo Maman Bébé’s is a £60 basket, and Gap Kids charges £4.95 for home delivery however much you spend while collecting free at its pick-up points. M&S sits at the top of that range for home delivery and at the bottom of it for collection, which suits a school-uniform shop with stores in most towns and suits a parent ordering one replacement jumper rather less.

Two numbers a UK shopper would want are published by M&S — on other countries’ versions of the same site. The Irish pages state it in readable sentences: “Standard Delivery costs €4.50, or is free on orders over €75. Delivery within 4 days, excluding weekends”, “Store collection is free on all fashion, home and beauty orders”, and “Stores will hold your order for 5 days from your arranged collection date.” The Jersey pages give a tiered version in sterling: “Free home delivery over £50”, standard tracked delivery at £3.50 taking 5-6 working days, express at £5.99 taking 3-4, and store collection at £3.50 or free over £25, with a 5% GST fee on orders over £60.

So the price of standard UK delivery below £75, and the number of days it takes, exist as published M&S policy in euro and in Jersey sterling, and could not be found in any readable UK page. That is a gap rather than a concealment, and the reason is the next paragraph.

Six weeks in 2025 when none of this could be bought online

M&S stopped taking online orders in late April 2025 after a cyber incident, and its own results documents are the only source worth using on what that cost. The interim results for the 26 weeks to 27 September 2025, issued 5 November 2025, record that “the site was only available for browsing from late April to early June”, and that “Home delivery resumed first, followed by click and collect in early August.”

The numbers in that half were severe. “Fashion, Home & Beauty sales decreased 16.4%”, from £2,030.5m to £1,697.6m. “UK online sales declined 42.9% to £382.3m” while “Store sales decreased 3.4% to £1,315.4m”. Operating profit before adjusting items in the segment fell from £243.4m to £46.1m, down 81.1%, taking the adjusted operating margin from 12.0% to 2.7% — a drop of 930 basis points. Group adjusted profit before tax came in at £184.1m against £413.1m.

By the full year to 28 March 2026, reported on 20 May 2026, the segment’s sales were £3,915.5m against £4,243.4m, down 7.7%, with “Online sales decreased by 18.4% to £1,165.7m” and “Store sales decreased by 2.3% to £2,749.8m”. Segment operating profit before adjusting items was £213.4m against £478.0m. The incident itself shows up as “Adjusting items of £292.1m includes £131.3m of incident-related costs”, of which “£109.3m of these costs are related to immediate incident systems response and recovery”, partly met by “Insurance proceeds of £100.0m”. Group statutory profit before tax was £364.6m against £511.8m, a fall of 28.8%; group revenue rose 25.0% to £17,273.6m, but that number is inflated by consolidating Ocado Retail for the first time — sales excluding Ocado Retail grew 1.9%, to £14,178.1m.

Two things in those documents bear directly on children’s clothes. The segment is called “Fashion, Home & Beauty”, its definition includes kidswear, and neither document breaks out a childrenswear or schoolwear figure of any kind — so there is no published number for how big the kids business is. And on the range itself the company is blunt: the half-year commentary says “Kids lagged the market. As we invest in value, daywear prices have been reduced by more than 20%”, and the full-year version says “Kidswear opening price points were reduced as the business seeks to reposition its ranges.” The seven-year price freeze is a schoolwear promise. Everyday kids’ clothes went the other way, and got cheaper.

Two recalls in thirteen months, and both were babywear

The GOV.UK product safety register is free, searchable and almost never quoted, and it holds two Marks & Spencer children’s recalls in the thirteen months to November 2025. The newer one, reference 2511-0077, published 11 November 2025, is headed “Product Recall: Marks and Spencer Stripe 2.5 Tog Baby Sleepbag” and describes a “Striped 2.5 tog blue baby sleeping bag. The product was sold on M&S.com, and in Clothing & Home stores.” The hazard is specific: “The product presents a risk of strangulation as the neck measurement does not fall within the parameters specified in the relevant standard, BS EN 16781.” The notice records that “The product has been recalled from end users”, tells owners to “Stop using the product immediately”, and states that “The product does not meet the requirements of the General Product Safety Regulations 2005.” The brand is given as “Marks and Spencer PLC”, the product code as T92/4648S, the country of origin as Sri Lanka and the lead authority as Local Authority Trading Standards. One oddity: the notice’s own Type field reads “Childcare articles and children’s equipment – Swaddle Blanket” while its description calls the item a sleeping bag.

The older one, 2503-0117, published 8 April 2025, covers “Marks and Spencer PLC Children’s Fisherman’s Coats for 0-3 Months and 3-6 Months”, described as “Water-resistant children’s jackets sold from 9th January 2025.” The hazard reads: “The product presents a risk of suffocation because it includes a hood and is available in sizes suitable for babies”, with the mechanism spelled out — if a baby is left unattended inside a hooded product, its own movement may move the hood. The models named are “Ditsy Print Fisherman Coat, Printed Fisherman Coat, Hooded Fisherman Jacket”, the origin is China, and the corrective action is to “return them to their nearest Marks and Spencer store for a full refund”. Again the breach cited is the General Product Safety Regulations 2005, and again Trading Standards notified it.

Read both ways. Two recalls from end users in just over a year, on babywear, is two more than a parent would like, and the register grades neither — the risk level on both reads “Not provided”. It is also what a traceable business on a public register looks like: a named company, a product code, a refund at any store, and an authority that made it public. A shop with no recalls on the register is not necessarily safer; it may simply be smaller or harder to trace.

A practical note for anyone repeating the search. Querying the register for “marks and spencer” returned “1,161 reports” across 24 pages on 20 November 2026, and only the first five headlines named the retailer at all — the keyword ranks results rather than filtering them. Searching the product name instead returned each alert directly.

Which company sells the jumper

Two Marks & Spencer companies sit on the public register and they are 75 years apart. MARKS AND SPENCER P.L.C. is company 00214436, incorporated on 17 June 1926, active, a public limited company at Waterside House, 35 North Wharf Road, London W2 1NW, with a single SIC code of 47190, “Other retail sale in non-specialised stores”. MARKS AND SPENCER GROUP P.L.C. is company 04256886, incorporated on 23 July 2001 at the same address and under the same SIC code, with its last accounts made up to 28 March 2026 and its last confirmation statement dated 23 July 2026.

The 2001 company has two previous names on its record, and together they show what it was for: it was TRUSHELFCO (NO.2827) LIMITED from 23 July to 7 August 2001, then CONKER RETAIL LIMITED until 24 January 2002. A shelf company bought off the peg and renamed twice is the ordinary machinery of putting a holding company above a trading one, and it is the holding company that files the group accounts quoted earlier.

Which of the two sells the clothes is a question the shop’s own paperwork should answer, and on 20 November 2026 it could not be reached. The website’s terms and conditions were requested at four separate addresses and every one failed at the host with a client error, returning nothing of the page. The only identification available was the footer of the shop itself, which reads “© 2026 Marks and Spencer plc (UK)” — a name but not a company number, no registered office, no VAT number and no clause saying when the contract is formed. The 1926 company is the obvious candidate on the strength of that footer, but it is an inference and it is recorded here as one.

What the shop would not show, and what somebody else measured

Which? is the independent measure worth having here, and it runs a baby and child shops survey with real fieldwork behind it: July and August 2025, “an online survey of 2,317 members of the public” reporting “about 6,099 experiences”, with a category average customer score of 80%. Marks & Spencer scores 82% from 270 responses, which is above average and on one of the larger samples in the table. Next is 83% from 276 and John Lewis 82% from 271, and both carry the “RECOMMENDED PROVIDER” label; Amazon is 81% from 532 and JoJo Maman Bébé 76% from 125. Which? says “This year, we’ve named seven retailers WRPs for baby equipment”, and M&S is not among them — it scores level with a recommended retailer without being one, which is a fine distinction but it is Which?’s to draw.

The rest of this section is a list of things that could not be read. Not one garment price could be found on any M&S product or category page. Three of them were fetched in full — between 70,979 and 78,623 characters each, read from start to finish — and the only pound signs in any of them were navigation labels such as “Gifts Under £20” and “Flowers under £35”. The prices are drawn in by script after the page loads, which means a reader without JavaScript, and any tool reading the page as text, sees a shop with nothing in it. Every price on this page therefore comes from the company’s own dated press releases instead.

The kids size chart could not be read either: three different size-guide addresses were tried and all three failed at the host. So the one check worth doing on any children’s clothing range — whether the age bands step evenly and whether any age is missing — cannot be done from the outside. The same goes for the returns window in days, the delivery price below £75, the small print behind both asterisks, the fibre percentages (a product page describes “pure cotton fabric” without saying how much), and any complaints or alternative dispute resolution route.

Two smaller things are worth a line. The link to a polo shirt reads “3pk-unisex-pure-cotton-school-polo-shirts” while the page it opens is titled “3 Pack Unisex Stain Resist School Polo Shirts (2-18 Yrs)”, and a pinafore reached at “girls-pleated-school-pinafore-2-12-yrs-” declares its canonical address as “girls-permanent-pleats-pinafore” — the name in the link is not always the name on the page. And the biggest omission is in the announcement itself: the 26 October 2026 release, written for parents restocking at October half term, does not mention the branded-item cap, second-hand uniform or the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act anywhere. The single legal change that pushes a uniform list towards the generic items M&S sells had already taken effect, by the Department for Education’s own date, and the back-to-school announcement said nothing about it.

Common questions

How much does delivery cost at M&S, and when is it free?

M&S’s own banner, read on two of its help pages on 20 November 2026, states “Free delivery when you spend over £75*”, “Free next-day Click and Collect*” and “Free returns for online orders”. The asterisks have no published footnote, and the price of standard home delivery below £75 is not stated on any UK page that could be read. For comparison, M&S’s Irish site publishes “Standard Delivery costs €4.50, or is free on orders over €75. Delivery within 4 days, excluding weekends”, and its Jersey site charges £3.50 for standard tracked delivery, free over £50.

How many branded uniform items can my child’s school insist on?

Three. Section 35 of the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 says a school in England “may not require a primary pupil at the school to have more than three different branded items of school uniform for use during a school year”, and the same for a secondary pupil “(or more than four different branded items of school uniform if one of those items is a tie)”. The Department for Education says the limit applies from September 2026 and covers PE kit as well as uniform. An item is branded if it carries the school name or logo, or if its colour, design or fabric means “it is only available from particular suppliers”.

Is M&S school uniform actually the same price as last year?

On its own account, yes, and the announcements check out against each other. M&S called it the third year in June 2023, the fifth in June 2025, and on 26 October 2026 said it would not raise prices in 2027, “a market-leading seventh year in a row”. Counting 2021 to 2027 inclusive gives seven. The pack sizes have changed — a three-pack of polo shirts at £7 in 2022 and 2023 became a two-pack at £4.50 in 2025 — but that works out at £2.25 a shirt against £2.33, so the price per item fell. The two releases describe different fabric treatments, so it is not an exact like-for-like.

What is the M&S kidswear guarantee?

A year. M&S states that “every piece of M&S kidswear comes with a one-year quality guarantee”, and that “If any item doesn’t live up to our high standards within 365 days, we’ll make it right with a full refund.” It also claims to be “the first major UK high street retailer to offer this”. The claim is published in a kidswear blog listed 29 July 2025 rather than in the site’s terms and conditions, which could not be opened on 20 November 2026 — so the guarantee’s exact conditions could not be read alongside it.

Have any M&S children’s clothes been recalled?

Two in thirteen months, both babywear, both on the GOV.UK product safety register. A striped 2.5 tog baby sleepbag (reference 2511-0077, 11 November 2025) was recalled from end users because “the neck measurement does not fall within the parameters specified in the relevant standard, BS EN 16781”. Children’s fisherman’s coats in 0-3 and 3-6 months (2503-0117, 8 April 2025) were recalled over a hood suffocation risk, with owners told to “return them to their nearest Marks and Spencer store for a full refund”. Both were recorded as breaching the General Product Safety Regulations 2005, and the register gives no risk grade for either.

Why can I not see any prices when I look at the M&S site?

The prices are added by script after the page loads. Three M&S product and category pages were fetched in full on 20 November 2026 — between 70,979 and 78,623 characters each — and not one garment price appeared in the page text; the only pound signs were navigation labels such as “Gifts Under £20”. The same is true of the delivery, returns and size-guide pages, which return navigation and no policy text. Every price on this page therefore comes from M&S’s own dated press releases rather than from its shop.

Our verdict

For school uniform, M&S has the strongest documented position of any children’s clothing retailer on this site: prices unchanged since 2021 across four dated announcements, an opening price of £2.25 a polo shirt, a 365-day refund guarantee on every piece of kidswear, and a legal cap on branded items from September 2026 that moves most of a uniform list towards exactly the generic garments it sells. The weak side is everything a shopper would want to read before ordering. The £75 free-delivery basket is the highest in the category and sits in a banner with an unpublished asterisk; no price, size chart, returns window or set of terms could be read on the site at all, while the Irish and Jersey versions of the same business publish their delivery charges plainly. Buy the uniform in a store or by Click and Collect, which is free at any size, and treat the guarantee as the thing that makes the price worth paying.
