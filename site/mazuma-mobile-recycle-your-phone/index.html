Mazuma Mobile Recycle Your Phone

Rates and terms checked 20 November 2026 · Recycle Your Phone · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Mazuma Mobile has bought and sold over 5 million phones since it was founded in 2006, and says it has paid out more than £300 million to the people who sent them in. It lists 39 iPhone models, from the iPhone 17e down to the iPhone 7, and it is the one recycler in this trade that will not print an “up to” price against any of them. Terms, promises and company records read from Mazuma’s own pages and Companies House on 20 November 2026.

What you get instead is a quote that does not move. Mazuma’s selling line is that its prices are “upfront, fixed and guaranteed”, that there are “no nasty inflated ’up to’ prices that change when your device arrives at Mazuma”, and that it will pay “100% of the price quoted or we’ll send you your mobile back free of charge”. The quote is held for 14 days, which is also how long you have to post the handset, and payment goes out the same day the parcel lands — Monday to Friday, on orders received before 2pm, public holidays excepted. Faulty and water-damaged phones are bought as well as working ones, and a network-locked handset is paid “the full value”.

One thing to know before you click: the figure is not on the website. No page on www.mazumamobile.com, m.mazumamobile.com or buy.mazumamobile.com carries a pound sign against any handset. Every model page says the same thing — “Please select Gigabyte (GB) size of your iPhone model to see what it’s worth” — so finding out what Mazuma will pay means beginning a sale rather than reading a list.

No pound sign anywhere on the site, and that is deliberate

The iPhone list runs ten years deep: iPhone 17e, 17 Pro Max, 17 Pro, Air and 17; 16e, 16 Pro Max, 16 Pro, 16 Plus and 16; 15 Pro Max, 15 Pro, 15 Plus and 15; 14 Pro, 14 Pro Max, 14 Plus and 14; SE 2022; 13 Pro Max, 13 Pro, 13 Mini and 13; 12 Mini, 12 Pro Max, 12 Pro and 12; SE 2020; 11 Pro Max, 11 Pro and 11; XR, XS Max, XS and X; 8 Plus and 8; and 7 Plus and 7. Thirty-nine models, the oldest of them a handset Apple launched in 2016. Not one of the thirty-nine carries a price, and nor does the Samsung page, the broken-phone page or the home page.

Mazuma says plainly why. Its iPhone page promises “None of those disappointing ’up to’ inflated prices, no hidden costs or catches”, and its main sell-my-mobile page goes further: “Unlike other mobile phone recyclers, we guarantee our prices are fixed” and “Price guarantee for 14 days — The price we quote is the price you get”. Most of this trade advertises a ceiling that only the newest handset in the best grade can reach. Mazuma has decided not to have a ceiling, which means there is nothing to be discounted from when the parcel is opened.

That is a real advantage and it has a real cost, and the cost falls entirely on anyone trying to shop around. A recycler that publishes a list can be compared from the sofa. Mazuma can only be compared by picking a model, choosing a storage size, selecting a condition and taking the quote — a process the site reckons “Takes less than 5 mins”, which is true, and which still has to be done once per handset per competitor.

The two halves of the site also disagree about what is on sale. The desktop iPhone page still carries a banner reading “COMING SOON! Introducing the new iPhone 17 Series!”, while the mobile site already lists five iPhone 17 models and will quote for them. One of those two pages has not been touched since the 17 went on sale.

Fixed and guaranteed, with a clause about revised offers underneath

The price promise is set out on Mazuma’s own promises page, and it is conditional in a way the marketing is not. “Our price promise guarantees you receive the full price quoted for your sale”, it says, “provided it meets our Terms & Conditions of Supply”. Same-day payment carries the same rider: it “is subject to orders meeting our Terms & Conditions of Supply”. And then the mechanism: “Once we receive your phone or device, if we find they do not meet our Terms & Conditions of Supply and the order value needs to be amended”, Mazuma says, “we will email you a revised offer for your approval”.

So revised offers exist on a site whose whole proposition is that prices do not change when the device arrives. That is not dishonest — every recycler has to price a phone that turns out to be worse than described, and Mazuma is more generous than most about what happens next: “If you are not completely satisfied with the revised offer, you can request to have the revised phone or device returned to you FREE of charge”. The home page FAQ says the same in friendlier words: “If you don’t want to accept the adjusted offer, that’s perfectly okay, and we will send your device straight back to you”.

The sentence the FAQ leaves out is on the promises page. “Revised orders will be automatically processed for payment if you do not reply to our revised offer email within 14 days”. Saying nothing is therefore accepting the lower figure, not declining it. Both statements are Mazuma’s own and they are not strictly in conflict — the promises page supplies the deadline the FAQ omits — but a seller who reads only the FAQ would come away with the opposite impression. The practical advice is short: answer the email.

What triggers a revision is the device not matching what was selected, which puts a lot of weight on the grade the seller picks. Mazuma describes that choice four different ways. The home page says the phone is classed as “Good”, “Poor” or “Faulty”; its own FAQ offers “Excellent, Good and Faulty”; the Samsung page offers “excellent”, “good” or “faulty”; and the iPhone instructions ask only whether the handset is “working” or “faulty”. Four vocabularies for the one choice that sets the price.

The promise has one stated exclusion and it is a fair one: “Price promise does not apply to blocked (registered lost/stolen) phones or devices”. Mazuma checks every handset against “CheckMEND, a crime protection database” and says “We do not purchase blocked handsets”. It also refuses counterfeits — “We do not accept any non-genuine products”.

The document all of that hangs on is the Terms & Conditions of Supply, linked from the footer of every page as “T&C’s”. It returned a client error on three separate attempts across this research, so the contract that governs the price promise, the same-day payment promise and the revision trigger could not be read. Nothing suggests the terms are unreasonable. They simply could not be checked, and the pages that cite them do not reproduce them.

Fourteen days does three different jobs

The number 14 appears three times in Mazuma’s process and means something different each time. It is the price lock — “Price guarantee for 14 days”. It is the posting window — “Post us your device within 14 days”, and “Your order will be valid for 14 days from the date it is processed”. And it is the deadline on a revised offer, after which silence is payment.

The first two protect the seller. The third binds them. The same fortnight is a guarantee if you post promptly and a trap if you stop opening your email, and only one of the three is spelled out on the page a seller is most likely to read.

The clock is tighter than it looks once the post is in it. Mazuma promises a sales pack “within 24hrs” on its how-it-works page and “within 48hrs” on its sending-your-mobile page — two pages, two promises. Delivery is then “2-3 working days” by Freepost or “3-4 working days” by DPD, and the same page warns that “On some occasions the Royal Mail can unfortunately take up to 15 working days to deliver”, with “If we have not received your phone within 15 working days then it may possibly be lost in the post”. Take the slower pack promise and the worst delivery and the posting obligation is still met — the duty is to post inside 14 days, not to have the parcel arrive. Whether a quote survives a parcel that then sits in the network for three weeks is exactly the sort of question the Terms & Conditions of Supply would answer, and that is the document that would not open.

Getting it there: free postage, £150 of cover, and a £750 line

Postage is genuinely free and there are two ways to do it. “Sending your device via DPD local drop off shop is FREE”, and “Print your own label is also free of charge”. The free Tracked Returns route costs “FREE”, carries Royal Mail cover of “Up to £150”, takes “1-3 Working days” and allows a “Maximum number of phones per parcel: 2”. Trade in ten or more and Mazuma says “you will be given a free courier drop-off or collection service”. Free postage and collection are “only available from the UK”.

Above that there is a line, and Mazuma draws it at £750: “If your device is valued at or worth more than £750 please post Special Delivery to comply with Royal Mail’s T&Cs”. Special Delivery is listed at “Cost: £9.35” with cover “Up to £750” and delivery in “1 working day”. That £9.35 comes out of the seller’s pocket, not Mazuma’s, and the proportion it costs depends entirely on the handset: on a £750 payout it is 1.25% of the money, on a £160 payout it is 5.8%.

The real gap sits between the two options. A handset worth anything from £150 to £750 travels free on a route insured to £150, which leaves a £400 phone underinsured by £250 unless the seller volunteers to pay. Mazuma does advise upgrading — “We recommend that you send any high value units via Special Delivery to ensure successful delivery” — but its FAQ prices that advice at “approximately £3 — £6” while its how-it-works page prices the same service at £9.35. Two of Mazuma’s own pages, two numbers for one recommendation.

Who carries the risk is stated without euphemism, and it is the seller: “Unfortunately, we cannot accept responsibility for non-delivery of phones”. Two smaller warnings are worth heeding. “Please make sure your SIM card is removed”, because “We accept no liability in the event that any phone that has been sent with its SIM card and charges are then incurred. You shall continue to be responsible for such charges”. And “PLEASE DO NOT SEND accessories or the original box as we will not be able to return them” — Mazuma wants “the handset with its battery” and nothing else.

Same-day payment, or a cheque in the post

Mazuma pays fast and says so without hedging: “Mazuma will make payment on the very same day* they receive your device!”. The asterisk is honest and it is three conditions — “Monday to Friday, excluding public holidays”, plus “Same day payment does not apply to orders received after 2pm and in events that are out of our control”. A parcel arriving at three o’clock on a Friday is a Monday payment.

There are three ways to take the money: “Cash (Cheque), Cash (Bank Transfer) or Gift e-card”. Bank transfer is the one to pick. On the drop-off route Mazuma lists it as “Bank Transfer - made same day”, against “Cheque - posted same day” — posted, not received. Cheques go out “via 1st class post”, “can be banked into any UK bank that you hold an account with” and then “should normally take 3 working days to clear”.

A cheque also carries a charge that bank transfer does not. “We post all payments via 1st class post. If your payment has not arrived within 5 days, it may have been lost in the post”, and replacing one is not free: “a £6.00 charge will apply and will be deducted from the cheque value”. On an older handset at the bottom of a recycler’s range that can be a fifth of the proceeds, and the seller pays it for a failure of the post rather than anything they did.

The counter route is worth knowing if the handset is valuable enough to make the postal cover a worry. Mazuma takes drop-offs “between the hours of 8:45am to 5pm Mon-Fri”, the order is processed while you wait — “(takes about 15mins)” — and the bank transfer is made the same day against a receipt. The one rule is that “we cannot accept any handsets without a pre registered order”, so the online sale has to be done first.

Four answers to who owns Mazuma, and the register settles it

The contracting company is Mazuma Mobile Limited, number 05909550, incorporated on 17 August 2006 and shown as “Active” at Companies House. Its SIC code is “61900 - Other telecommunications activities” and its registered office is Suites 5a and 5b, Office Building 11, 2 Mannin Way, Caton Road, Lancaster LA1 3SU. Eighteen officers have passed through it since 2006, fifteen of them resigned; the three current directors are Craig Richard Hewitt Smith, appointed 20 December 2017, Sarah Fort, appointed 21 September 2020, and Tomas Conway Hill, appointed 19 June 2024.

The site gives four answers to who owns it. Its about-us page says “Mazuma Mobile is part of the Lancaster Group” and the privacy notice on the main site says “Mazuma Mobile Limited (Part of Lancaster Group Holdings Limited)”. But a second about page says “Mazuma Mobile is part of the EcoRenew Group”, and the privacy notice served on the mobile site says “Mazuma Mobile Limited (Part of Eco Renew UK)”.

Companies House settles it, and the answer is that both names were right once. Mazuma’s controlling shareholder is Lancaster Group Holdings Limited, company 11190451, which holds “Ownership of shares — 75% or more” and “Ownership of voting rights - 75% or more”, notified on 31 March 2018 in place of Ventura Telecom Holdings Limited (05870393), which ceased the same day. That parent was incorporated on 6 February 2018 under the name ECORENEW HOLDINGS (UK) LIMITED and kept it until 26 July 2024. So two of Mazuma’s own pages are more than two years behind their parent’s own change of name. Its filing history shows the handover: a PSC05 on 19 August 2024 recording a “Change of details for Ecorenew Holdings (Uk) Limited as a person with significant control on 24 July 2024”, and a second on 6 March 2026 for Lancaster Group Holdings Limited.

One level up, the parent is controlled by three people rather than a company, each on more than 25% but not more than 50% of the shares and votes: Craig Richard Hewitt Smith, notified 1 September 2022 and also holding the right to appoint or remove directors, and Gary Lau and Paul Smith, both notified on 6 February 2018. This is an owner-managed business, not a subsidiary of anything larger.

The accounts are the most useful thing in the file, and not because of the figures. Mazuma filed “Accounts for a small company” for the years to 31 March 2022 and 31 March 2023, then “Full accounts” for 31 March 2024, 2025 and 2026 — the newest filed on 26 October 2026 and running to 30 pages. A company that moves off the small-company exemption is a company that has grown past a statutory threshold, which is a firmer scale signal than any claim on the website. The figures inside could not be read on this run: the Companies House document viewer returned a client error. The next accounts, to 31 March 2027, are not due until 31 December 2027.

Two smaller points of housekeeping. The address Mazuma gives for post and for data requests is “Unit 2, Southgate Trade Park, Southgate, White Lund Industrial Estate, Morecambe, LA3 3PB” — which was its registered office until it moved to Lancaster on 2 June 2023. Send the handset there by all means; just do not mistake it for where the company is registered. And the two charges visible in its filing history have both been satisfied in full, the later of them on 7 September 2026.

Where the handsets go, and who wipes them

Mazuma is clearer than most about the destination, and it is reuse rather than recycling. It “re-home, on average, in excess of 15,000 mobiles per month” and aims “to reuse over 95% of the mobile phones received in various developing markets where the cost of new mobile phones is sometimes unaffordable”. Its FAQ names them: “Most of the phones are sent for reuse in markets such as China, Africa, Pakistan and India”. Where a handset cannot be used again, “we promise to recycle them in the most environmentally friendly way”.

The credentials claimed for that are specific: “We are a Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment (WEEE) Authorised Treatment Facility and are ISO 14001 Environmental Management System Accredited”. Both are real schemes and both leave a paper trail. An approved authorised treatment facility holds a permit from its environment agency, and that permit has a number. An ISO 14001 certificate names a certification body and carries a reference and an expiry. Neither number appears on any page read for this review, and nor does the date of the last audit, so the claims have to be taken on trust.

On data, the honest reading is that the wipe is the seller’s job. Mazuma offers a free Mobile Data Delete Tool, which it calls “the 1st of its kind in Europe” and which gives “step-by-step instructions on how to remove personal information from your used mobile phone or device”. Instructions are not an erasure service, and no page says Mazuma erases a consumer trade-in itself or names a standard it erases to. The privacy notice does mention erasure, but as a separate commercial service: “Unknown Data includes data on devices received for erasure and destruction services, where we do not access or record the data and simply erasure/destroy in accordance with the service agreement”. That is a business contract, not the consumer route. Wipe the phone before it goes, and take the SIM out.

The CheckMEND screening is the genuine safeguard in this part of the process, and it is a good one, but it establishes whether a phone was stolen rather than whether the last owner’s photographs are still on it. On records, Mazuma says “By law we have to keep basic information about our customers” for “six years after they cease being customers for tax purposes” and that it tries “to respond to all legitimate requests within one month”.

One register entry needs a question put to it. Mazuma Mobile Limited appears on the Information Commissioner’s register at reference Z1088307, registered on 22 October 2007 at payment tier Tier 2 and at the Lancaster address. Read on 20 November 2026, that entry gives a registration expiry of 21 October 2026, a month earlier. On a trading company this is more likely an entry that has not been refreshed than a company that has stopped registering, and the difference matters enough to be worth confirming with the ICO rather than assumed either way — particularly as the privacy notice tells customers the ICO is where to complain.

A note on the old end of any recycler’s range, because it does not bite here. Ofcom’s consumer advice on the 2G and 3G switch-off, published 3 August 2022 and carrying no later date, says “All the mobile providers have confirmed to the Government that they do not plan to offer their 2G (and 3G) services beyond 2033” and, more immediately, “Three doesn’t have a 2G network”. The operator timings on that page are from 2022 and have been overtaken by events. What it means for a seller is simple enough: every one of the 39 iPhones Mazuma lists is a 4G handset or better, so none of them is affected. The switch-off reaches the feature phones and the pre-2012 handsets, which is also the end of the range where any recycler’s prices fall away to almost nothing.

No outside rating, and no adjudicator either

There is one independent measure in this trade and it does not cover Mazuma. Which? published its assessment of second-hand tech retailers on 22 October 2026, built from 2,495 panel members and members of the public reporting about 4,104 experiences of buying second-hand devices since June 2023, with fieldwork in June and July 2025 and the next update due in September 2027. It rates 18 brands. Mazuma is not one of them.

Two of its direct rivals are. Music Magpie scores 81% and Envirofone 80%, and Envirofone is one of four Which? Recommended Providers alongside Samsung Certified Re-Newed at 83%, The iOutlet at 83% and Dell at 81%. The threshold for that badge is “a customer score of at least 80%” plus “at least four stars for customer service” and “at least four stars for value for money”. Being absent from the table is not a mark against Mazuma — a brand has to reach a minimum sample before Which? can score it at all — but it does mean a seller has nothing outside the company’s own words to weigh.

Worth knowing even for the brands that are in it: the survey measures buying a second-hand device, not selling one. Which? describes its sample as “experiences of buying second-hand devices since June 2023”, so it is a rating of these firms as shops rather than as buyers, and the service this page is about is the other direction. Defaqto and Fairer Finance do not reach retail at all.

Against that, Mazuma’s own claims are unsourced and they do not agree with each other. It calls itself “the UK’s best rated & largest mobile phone recycling service” and “UK’s biggest” without citing anything. The home page says “Over 5 million devices bought and sold” while an iPhone 15 Plus page says “With over 8 million satisfied customers” — more customers than devices, which cannot be right both ways round. A “Mobile News Award Winner 2024” logo appears with no category and no link.

The last absence is the one that would matter in a dispute. No consumer complaints procedure, no escalation route and no alternative dispute resolution scheme appears anywhere on the pages read. The only adjudicator named anywhere is the Information Commissioner, and that office handles what happens to a seller’s data, not what happens to their money. A revised offer that a seller thinks is wrong has one route: reply to the email inside 14 days and ask for the phone back.

Common questions

How much does Mazuma Mobile pay for an iPhone?

Mazuma does not publish a figure. It lists 39 iPhone models, from the iPhone 17e down to the iPhone 7, and none of them carries a price until you have chosen the model, the storage size and the condition. That is deliberate rather than an oversight: the site says it has “None of those disappointing ’up to’ inflated prices” and guarantees that “The price we quote is the price you get” for 14 days. The trade-off is that a quote takes a few minutes to obtain and cannot be compared from a list.

Is the quote really fixed?

For 14 days, and subject to the handset matching what you selected. Mazuma promises “100% of the price quoted or we’ll send you your mobile back free of charge”, and the one stated exclusion is a handset registered lost or stolen. If the device does not meet its Terms & Conditions of Supply, Mazuma emails a revised offer instead, and you can ask for the phone back free of charge.

What happens if Mazuma offers less after inspecting the phone?

Reply to the email. Mazuma’s promises page says “Revised orders will be automatically processed for payment if you do not reply to our revised offer email within 14 days”, so saying nothing accepts the lower figure rather than declining it. If you do reply, “you can request to have the revised phone or device returned to you FREE of charge”.

When and how does Mazuma pay?

The same day your device arrives, with three conditions: Monday to Friday, before 2pm, and excluding public holidays. There are three methods — bank transfer, cheque or gift e-card. Bank transfer is made the same day; a cheque is only posted the same day, goes 1st class, takes about 3 working days to clear, and costs £6 out of the payment to replace if it goes missing.

Is my phone insured while it is in the post?

Up to £150 on the free tracked route, and up to £750 by Special Delivery, which costs £9.35 and is what Mazuma asks you to use for a handset worth more than £750. Anything valued between those two figures travels on £150 of cover unless you upgrade at your own expense, and Mazuma states that it “cannot accept responsibility for non-delivery of phones”. Remove the SIM card first: the company accepts no liability for call charges run up on a SIM that travels with the handset.

Who owns Mazuma Mobile?

Mazuma Mobile Limited, company 05909550, incorporated on 17 August 2006 and registered in Lancaster. Lancaster Group Holdings Limited (11190451) holds 75% or more of the shares and votes, and that company was itself named Ecorenew Holdings (UK) Limited until 26 July 2024 — which is why two of Mazuma’s own pages still describe it as part of the EcoRenew group. Above the holding company are three individual shareholders, each on more than 25% but not more than 50%.

Does Mazuma wipe the data from my phone?

Not on the consumer route, on the evidence of its own pages. It provides a free Mobile Data Delete Tool giving “step-by-step instructions on how to remove personal information”, which puts the wipe in the seller’s hands, and no page names an erasure standard or certificate for a trade-in. Its privacy notice does describe erasure and destruction as a service, but under a separate service agreement rather than as part of selling a handset. Wipe and factory-reset the phone before posting it.

Our verdict

Mazuma has built its business on the one thing that annoys people most about selling a phone, and it has done it properly. There is no ceiling price to be talked down from, the quote is fixed for 14 days, postage is free both ways, a network-locked handset is paid in full, and the money goes out the day the parcel lands if it arrives on a weekday before two o’clock. Twenty years of trading, a move from small-company accounts to full accounts, over 5 million devices through the door and more than £300 million paid out are the marks of a business that works, and the CheckMEND screening and the WEEE and ISO 14001 claims put it at the serious end of the trade. Two things temper that. The first is practical: because nothing is priced on the site, Mazuma is the one recycler a seller cannot check without committing to a sale, so the fixed quote has to be taken on trust until it appears. The second is the small print. A revised offer paid automatically after 14 days of silence is the opposite of what the marketing implies, and the contract that governs it would not open on three attempts. Take the bank transfer rather than the cheque, pick the grade carefully, wipe the handset and remove the SIM before it goes, and read the email if one arrives. On those terms it is a straightforward way to turn an old phone into money, and the absence of an “up to” price is a feature rather than a gap.
