Mercedes-Benz New Cars Review: What the Range Costs in 2026

Rates and terms checked 20 November 2026 · New Cars · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Mercedes-Benz UK publishes an on-the-road price for every car in its range on a single page: 62 priced models running from £31,870 for an A-Class Hatchback to £243,450 for a Maybach SL Monogram Series. Figures checked 20 November 2026 against Mercedes-Benz UK’s own model pages, which label each one “OTR price starting from”.

That is worth saying plainly, because it is not how every marque does it. The price is printed rather than quoted on request, the same number appears wherever you look it up, and the page header counts 66 models in all. Four of them — the V-Class, the Marco Polo, a new petrol GLA and the VLE Grand Limousine — carry no price yet, two of those marked “Coming soon”.

The car itself comes from Mercedes-Benz UK Limited, company number 02448457, incorporated on 1 December 1989 and registered at Delaware Drive, Tongwell, Milton Keynes MK15 8BA. Its single SIC code is 45111, sale of new cars and light motor vehicles. Every new car carries a three-year warranty and three years of breakdown cover, and the company is accredited to The Motor Ombudsman’s Motor Industry Codes of Practice, which puts an eight-week deadline on a final response to a complaint and an independent decision after it.

What 62 published prices actually look like

The range page is the whole price list, and it is unusually frank for a premium marque. Reading up from the bottom on 20 November 2026: the A-Class Hatchback hybrid at £31,870, the CLA Coupé hybrid at £33,100, the B-Class at £36,235, the CLA Shooting Brake hybrid at £36,660 and the GLA hybrid at £37,225. The CLA Electric arrives at £39,150 and the CLA Shooting Brake Petrol at £39,715. Those eight cars are the only ones Mercedes sells for less than £40,000.

Above them the ladder climbs steeply. The EQA is £49,760, the GLC Electric £55,495, the E-Class Saloon £57,170, the EQE Saloon £69,365, the EQS Saloon £91,765, the S-Class £103,450 and the EQS SUV £129,480. The G-Class runs from £142,635 in petrol and diesel form to £190,975, the Maybach EQS SUV reaches £200,870, and the Maybach SL Monogram Series tops the list at £243,450. The median published price sits well above £55,000, which tells you more about the shape of the range than any single figure.

Two things about those numbers are worth holding on to. OTR is never expanded anywhere on the page, and no footnote says what it includes — on most marques an on-the-road price covers delivery, number plates, the first year of vehicle tax and the £55 first registration fee, but Mercedes does not say so here. And several models appear twice with different prices and no explanation of what separates them: the EQE Saloon is listed at both £69,365 and £105,995, the EQE SUV at £76,870 and £115,495, the CLA Shooting Brake Petrol at £39,715 and £53,330. The cheaper figure is presumably the base car and the dearer one an AMG derivative, but the page does not say that, so a reader comparing two tiles is comparing two unlabelled things.

Fifty of the 62 pay £440 a year in extra tax

This is the arithmetic that matters most on a Mercedes, and it is not on the Mercedes website at all. Under the vehicle tax tables at gov.uk, a car with a list price above £40,000 attracts an extra £440 a year for five years from the second time it is taxed — £2,200 in total. For an electric car the threshold is £50,000 instead, and zero-emission cars registered before 1 April 2025 are exempt altogether.

Run that against the 62 published prices. Seven petrol, diesel and hybrid Mercedes sit under £40,000, and five electric ones sit under £50,000: the CLA Electric at £39,150, the CLA Shooting Brake Electric at £40,150, the GLA Electric at £45,400, the GLB Electric at £46,100 and the EQA at £49,760. Twelve cars out of 62. The other 50 pay the supplement, which means that on four Mercedes in every five the advertised price understates five years of ownership by £2,200.

The near-misses are where it gets uncomfortable. The EQA clears the £50,000 electric threshold by £240, and the CLA Shooting Brake Petrol clears the £40,000 one by £285. Both margins are thinner than the gap between two definitions of price: gov.uk defines list price as “the published price of the vehicle before it’s registered for the first time” and “before any discounts are applied”, while Mercedes publishes an on-the-road figure, which normally sits several hundred pounds above the list price because it includes registration and delivery. On a £240 margin that difference decides the answer. Neither Mercedes nor the tax tables resolve which number DVLA takes, so get it confirmed in writing before you order either car on the strength of the threshold.

No Mercedes is on the Electric Car Grant list

The Electric Car Grant is applied as a discount at the point of sale rather than claimed by the buyer — gov.uk puts it flatly: “You do not apply for the grant. The seller includes it as a discount in the purchase price.” Band 1 is worth up to £3,750 and Band 2 up to £1,500.

On 20 November 2026 the eligible list held 21 entries in Band 1 and 48 lines in Band 2, covering around 50 models because some lines name several. Not one of them is a Mercedes-Benz. The brands in Band 1 are Abarth, Alpine, BMW, Citroën, Fiat, Ford, Hyundai, Kia, MINI, Nissan and Renault; Band 2 adds Alfa Romeo, Audi, Cupra, DS, Jeep, KGM, Peugeot, Skoda, Toyota, Vauxhall and Volkswagen. Mercedes appears in neither.

The published criteria are an M1 passenger vehicle producing 0gCO2/km at the tailpipe, a minimum battery range of 100 miles (160km), a three-year or 60,000-mile vehicle warranty, an eight-year or 100,000-mile battery warranty and “minimum sustainability criteria”. The grant pages name no body that assesses cars and set out no price ceiling at all, so nothing published explains the absence, and this page makes no claim about why. What can be said is the consequence: the cheapest electric Mercedes costs £39,150, and a buyer choosing between it and a grant-eligible rival is comparing a car with £1,500 or £3,750 already deducted against one with nothing deducted. Mercedes’ own pages do not mention the scheme.

The battery certificate is a nine-step ladder, and the newest electric cars are missing from it

Mercedes-Benz UK Limited issues a separate document called the Battery Certificate for Plug-in Hybrid and Electric Drive Cars, and it is the most substantial thing the company publishes about owning one of its electric cars. It is also the clearest problem on this page.

There is no single battery term. A plug-in hybrid gets 62,000 miles (100,000 km) or six years from first registration, whichever comes sooner, and the 70% capacity floor does not apply to it at all. Battery electric cars are graded by model: the EQE, EQE SUV, EQS, EQS SUV and Maybach EQS SUV get 155,000 miles (250,000 km) or ten years; the EQA, EQB, EQC, EQV and the G 580 with EQ Technology get 100,000 miles (160,000 km) or eight years; the electric B-Class and the older smart models get 62,000 miles (100,000 km) or eight years. A battery falling below 70% of its rated capacity as measured when it was produced can be replaced inside the period — except on the EQC, EQV, B-Class and smart, where the test is an absolute figure instead, such as 162 Ah or 155 Ah on the EQC depending on battery type, 120 Ah or 180 Ah on the EQV, and 19.6 kWh on the B-Class. Nine different answers to one question, in one document.

Now compare that list against what Mercedes is selling today. The current version of the certificate was “updated on 14 October 2024”, more than two years ago. It still sets out terms for the EQC, the EQV and smart. It does not name the CLA Electric, the CLA Shooting Brake Electric, the GLA Electric, the GLB Electric, the GLC Electric, the C-Class Electric, the electric G-Class or the AMG GT Electric — every one of which has a published price on the range page. For a car not on the list the document says only that “the generic 70% capacity threshold for the HVB does not apply but the individual Warranty period rules listed below are applicable”, and then states no period for it. So a buyer ordering the cheapest electric Mercedes in the range cannot establish from Mercedes’ own document how long its battery is covered for, or to what capacity. That is the single most consequential gap on this page, and it needs resolving in writing before any electric Mercedes order is signed.

The conditions attached are strict and specific, and two of them catch people out. The battery must be recharged within 14 days of its charge level reaching zero per cent, only “the approved/recommended charging cable for the vehicle” may be used, and “A full digital service history report is required” before a claim. Excluded are damage from motor sport or performance tuning, non-genuine parts, work by a non-authorised independent repairer, and external causes including “severe weather conditions”, flooding, saltwater ingress, accident, vandalism or war. A repair claim is “limited to the current value of the vehicle at the time the malfunction occurred”, Mercedes may fit “new or recondition or remanufactured parts” at its discretion, removed parts become its property, and “The Mercedes-Benz Battery Certificate does not provide for any further entitlements”. The document contains no provision transferring it to a later owner, and says the terms “may be amended at any time by Mercedes-Benz UK Limited without notice”. Taxi and hackney carriage use is carved out separately, at three years.

A three-year warranty with no mileage, no paint term and no corrosion term

The warranty page gives a reader one number and then stops. It says “every new Mercedes-Benz car sold through our network also comes with a comprehensive three-year warranty” and adds “*Terms and conditions apply”. There is no mileage limit, no paintwork term and no anti-corrosion or bodywork term printed anywhere on it — all three are the sort of thing a premium marque usually advertises, and all three sit behind a warranty handbook PDF the page links rather than summarises. The battery certificate is the same: the page says only that electric and plug-in hybrid cars come with “a High Voltage Battery Certificate”, with no duration, mileage or capacity figure next to it.

Breakdown cover is the better product and the better disclosure. Three years comes free with any new Mercedes and one year with an approved used car, covering technical breakdown, punctures, lost keys, running out of fuel, accident recovery “regardless of fault”, vandalism and theft, 24 hours a day across the UK and Europe. After that it renews annually at no charge for up to 30 years — but only on one condition, which the page states openly: “To maintain Roadside Assistance cover after the first 3 years, simply service your car at a Mercedes-Benz Authorised Repairer.” Independent servicing ends it. Cars registered between 24 October 1998 and 31 March 2008 are covered for technical breakdown only.

The paid extension is a separate insurance product and worth reading before buying. Mercedes-Benz Extended Warranty is underwritten by AWP P&C S.A., a French company registered as 519490080 RCS Bobigny, operating through UK branch BR015275 at 102 George Street, Croydon CR9 6HD, and regulated by the FCA under firm reference 534384. It is administered by Mercedes-Benz Warranty Services and Allianz, a trading name of AWP Assistance UK Ltd, company 1710361, FCA firm reference 311909. Excess options are £0, £100 or £250, a transfer to a new owner costs £25, the car must be under nine years old with fewer than 110,000 miles at the start date, cover runs to a maximum of 155,000 miles, and a 24-month policy needs a car under eight years old. Wear and tear is excluded, as are cosmetic damage, serviceable items, roadside assistance and a replacement car, and the total claim limit is “the current market value of your car”. No price is published: the cost “depends on a number of factors, including your car’s model and mileage”. Neither the main page nor the FAQ mentions the high-voltage battery or hybrid components at all, which on a range this electrified is a conspicuous silence.

Euro NCAP changed its scoring, and the one Mercedes tested under it scores 28% for cyclists

Euro NCAP rebuilt its assessment for 2026, and this matters for anyone comparing crash results across marques: the familiar four percentages for adult occupant, child occupant, vulnerable road users and safety assist have been replaced by four different stages. The only Mercedes assessed under the new protocol is the GLE 450 d 4MATIC, left-hand drive, kerb weight 2,480 kg, and it scores five stars on Safe Driving 74%, Crash Avoidance 87%, Crash Protection 90% and Post Crash Safety 92%. Frontal impact 91%, side impact 100%, rear impact 88%.

Underneath that five-star result sit two findings the summary does not carry. Protection of people outside the car was, in Euro NCAP’s words, “generally good or adequate for the head of a struck pedestrian but poor for the head of a struck cyclist”, and the sub-scores put numbers on it: adult pedestrian head 81%, child pedestrian head 62%, cyclist head 28%, pelvis 47%. And the assessment adds that analysis “revealed that the Mercedes-Benz GLE would be a somewhat aggressive impact partner in a frontal collision” — a judgement about what the car does to the people it hits, which no star rating conveys. Distraction and fatigue detection are both fitted as standard, scoring 55% and 80% respectively, with driver engagement at 71%.

One caution on using these results at all. A five-star rating from the 2026 protocol is not comparable with a five-star rating from 2022 or 2024, because the test tightens between cycles, so always check which year a score comes from. And Euro NCAP has no published assessment for the A-Class, the CLA or the EQA, which between them are the cheapest Mercedes you can buy — the car with the best-documented safety performance in the range costs £79,995 and up.

A van buyer gets a representative APR. A car buyer does not.

The credit comes from Mercedes-Benz Financial Services UK Limited, company number 02472364, incorporated on 21 February 1990, registered at the same Delaware Drive address as the carmaker under SIC code 64999, financial intermediation. A third company, Mercedes-Benz Financial Services UK (Trustees) Limited, company 03180691 and incorporated 1 April 1996, shares the address too. The finance pages say “Credit provided subject to status by Mercedes-Benz Financial Services UK LTD, MK15 8BA”, with “Over 18s only” and “UK residents only”.

What is missing is the number. Mercedes-Benz UK’s own passenger car finance pages carry no representative APR at all — no rate, no term, no deposit, no monthly payment, no optional final payment, no total amount payable and no mileage allowance. The van side of the same business publishes two: 3.9% APR representative on eVito and eSprinter panel vans for registrations between 1 October 2026 and 3 January 2027, and 10.9% APR representative on qualifying approved used vehicles, with the offer ending 3 January 2027 and prices “correct at time of going to press 10/2026”. A reader financing a van can see what it costs; a reader financing a £31,870 A-Class on the same lender’s paper cannot, and has to reach a retailer to find out.

Two smaller points of regulatory housekeeping. The pages state that Mercedes-Benz Financial Services UK Ltd “is authorised and regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority” but print no firm reference number anywhere, which makes checking the permission harder than it needs to be. And the two ombudsmen do not overlap: The Motor Ombudsman handles the car and the Financial Ombudsman Service handles the credit, and neither can look at the other’s half of the same transaction.

The segments Mercedes sells into have shrunk harder than the market

Context from SMMT’s Motor Industry Facts 2026, published February 2026. The UK registered 2,020,520 new cars in 2025, average CO2 fell 10.1% to 91.8g/km against an EU target of 95g/km, and petrol accounted for 938,073 registrations, electrified 978,676 and diesel 103,771. The document carries no table of registrations by marque, so no Mercedes volume or market share can honestly be quoted from it — and the figures it does give for battery electric share do not reconcile with each other, 19.2% sitting alongside a count that works out closer to 14%, so neither is used here.

What the facts book does show is segment-level, and it is not flattering to the part of the market Mercedes occupies. The executive segment has fallen 80.1% since 2015, from 127,666 registrations to 25,367, and the luxury saloon segment is down 52.3%, from 9,061 to 4,318. Mercedes leads the executive table with the C-Class on 4,610 and also places the EQE on 2,749; in luxury saloons the S-Class managed 697, behind the BMW i7 on 959 and the 7 Series on 794. The specialist sports segment, where the AMG GT and SL compete, is down 72.3% to 13,618.

No Mercedes model appears in the 2025 top ten, which is led by the Ford Puma on 55,488 and the Kia Sportage on 47,788. None of that says the cars are worse; it says the segments have thinned, which is worth knowing if residual values matter to you, because a shrinking segment is a thinner used market in three years’ time. It is also worth noting that no ratings agency scores a car marque as a whole, so there is no independent brand rating to report here — Euro NCAP works model by model, and that is the only independent assessment available.

Where it wins

  • An on-the-road price is published for 62 of the 66 models, on one page, rather than quoted on request
  • The cheapest car in the range, the A-Class Hatchback at £31,870, carries the same price wherever it is listed
  • Three years of breakdown cover is included, renewable free for up to 30 years on a full service, and covers accident recovery regardless of fault
  • Accredited to The Motor Ombudsman, giving an eight-week final response deadline and a decision that binds Mercedes once the customer accepts it
  • The battery certificate on the EQE, EQE SUV, EQS, EQS SUV and Maybach EQS SUV runs to ten years or 155,000 miles, which is generous by class standards
  • Ten years of spare parts availability after a model leaves production, under clause 6.7 of the New Car Code
  • The GLE scores five stars and 90% for crash protection under Euro NCAP's tougher 2026 protocol

Where it falls short

  • Mercedes' own battery certificate, last updated 14 October 2024, states no term for the CLA Electric, GLC Electric, C-Class Electric, GLA Electric, GLB Electric or electric G-Class, all of which are on sale now with published prices
  • Fifty of the 62 published prices sit above the expensive car supplement threshold, adding £440 a year for five years, or £2,200, to a cost the advertised price does not mention
  • No Mercedes-Benz model appears in either band of the Electric Car Grant, so its cheapest electric car at £39,150 competes against rivals with up to £3,750 already deducted
  • The warranty page states three years and gives no mileage limit, no paintwork term and no anti-corrosion term at all, leaving all three in a PDF rather than on the page
  • No representative APR is published for any passenger car, while the same lender publishes 3.9% and 10.9% on the van side of the business
  • Free breakdown cover beyond year three is conditional on servicing at a Mercedes-Benz Authorised Repairer, so using an independent garage ends it
  • The extended warranty never mentions the high-voltage battery or hybrid components, publishes no price, and caps a claim at the car's current market value
  • The battery certificate contains no provision transferring it to a second owner, and says its terms may be amended at any time without notice
  • Several models are listed twice at very different prices with nothing on the page explaining what separates them, such as the EQE Saloon at £69,365 and £105,995

Common questions

What is the cheapest new Mercedes-Benz in 2026?

The A-Class Hatchback hybrid, at £31,870 on the road, checked 20 November 2026 against Mercedes-Benz UK’s own model pages. Next up are the CLA Coupé hybrid at £33,100, the B-Class at £36,235, the CLA Shooting Brake hybrid at £36,660 and the GLA hybrid at £37,225. Only eight Mercedes models are priced below £40,000.

Does any Mercedes qualify for the Electric Car Grant?

No. On 20 November 2026 the gov.uk eligible list held 21 entries in Band 1, worth up to £3,750, and 48 lines in Band 2, worth up to £1,500, and no Mercedes-Benz model appeared in either. The grant is applied as a discount by the seller rather than claimed by the buyer, so there is nothing a Mercedes buyer can apply for. The published criteria require 0gCO2/km at the tailpipe, at least 100 miles of range, a three-year or 60,000-mile vehicle warranty, an eight-year or 100,000-mile battery warranty and minimum sustainability criteria; the pages give no reason for any individual absence.

Will I pay the £440 expensive car tax on a Mercedes?

On four out of five, yes. The supplement is £440 a year for five years from the second time the car is taxed, on a list price above £40,000, or above £50,000 for an electric car. Twelve of the 62 published Mercedes prices fall below the relevant threshold: seven petrol, diesel or hybrid models under £40,000, plus the CLA Electric at £39,150, CLA Shooting Brake Electric at £40,150, GLA Electric at £45,400, GLB Electric at £46,100 and EQA at £49,760. Be careful with the EQA, which is only £240 under the electric threshold, and the CLA Shooting Brake Petrol, £285 under the petrol one — gov.uk measures the supplement on list price before discounts, and Mercedes publishes an on-the-road figure, so on margins that thin you need the retailer to confirm which number DVLA will use.

How long is the battery covered on an electric Mercedes?

It depends entirely on which model, and for several current cars Mercedes does not say. The battery certificate gives the EQE, EQE SUV, EQS, EQS SUV and Maybach EQS SUV ten years or 155,000 miles; the EQA, EQB, EQC, EQV and G 580 eight years or 100,000 miles; the electric B-Class and older smart models eight years or 62,000 miles; and plug-in hybrids six years or 62,000 miles. Cover is against capacity falling below 70% of the rated figure measured at production, except on four models where an absolute amp-hour or kWh figure applies instead. The document was last updated on 14 October 2024 and does not list the CLA Electric, GLA Electric, GLB Electric, GLC Electric, C-Class Electric or electric G-Class, so ask for the term in writing for the specific car you are ordering.

What does the three-year Mercedes warranty actually cover?

The warranty page states three years and no more than that. It gives no mileage limit, no paintwork term and no anti-corrosion term, and refers to a warranty handbook PDF for the conditions. Separately, every new car gets three years of breakdown cover including technical breakdown, punctures, lost keys, fuel, accident recovery regardless of fault, vandalism and theft, across the UK and Europe. That cover then renews free of charge for up to 30 years, but only if the car is serviced at a Mercedes-Benz Authorised Repairer each time.

Is the Mercedes-Benz Extended Warranty worth buying?

You cannot tell from the published material, because no price appears anywhere — the cost “depends on a number of factors, including your car’s model and mileage”. What is published: it is insurance underwritten by AWP P&C S.A. under FCA firm reference 534384 and administered by AWP Assistance UK Ltd under firm reference 311909, with excess options of £0, £100 or £250 and a £25 fee to transfer it. The car must be under nine years old with fewer than 110,000 miles when cover starts, and cover runs to 155,000 miles. Wear and tear, cosmetic damage, serviceable items, roadside assistance and a replacement car are all excluded, and the claim limit is your car’s current market value. Neither the main page nor the FAQ mentions the high-voltage battery or hybrid components, so on an electric or hybrid Mercedes, ask what is covered before buying.

Who do I complain to about a new Mercedes?

Start with Mercedes-Benz UK Ltd at Delaware Drive, Tongwell, Milton Keynes MK15 8BA. The company is accredited to The Motor Ombudsman’s Motor Industry Codes of Practice, under which it must give a final response within eight weeks, with guidance that it should acknowledge the complaint within ten working days. If you are still unhappy you have one year from the complaint or the final response, whichever is later, to refer it to The Motor Ombudsman, and its final decision becomes legally binding on all parties once you accept it. Note the split: that route covers the car, while anything about the finance agreement goes to the Financial Ombudsman Service instead.

How safe is a Mercedes in Euro NCAP tests?

Only one Mercedes has been assessed under Euro NCAP’s rebuilt 2026 protocol: the GLE 450 d 4MATIC, which took five stars on Safe Driving 74%, Crash Avoidance 87%, Crash Protection 90% and Post Crash Safety 92%. Within that, protection for people outside the car was mixed — 81% for an adult pedestrian’s head, 62% for a child’s and 28% for a cyclist’s — and the assessment notes the GLE “would be a somewhat aggressive impact partner in a frontal collision”. There is no published Euro NCAP assessment for the A-Class, CLA or EQA. Ratings from different protocol years are not directly comparable, because the tests tighten between cycles.

Our verdict

Mercedes-Benz UK does the hard part well: it prints a real on-the-road price for 62 cars, from £31,870 to £243,450, and the number holds wherever you check it. The problem is everything downstream of the price. The warranty page gives a figure and no terms, no passenger car carries a representative APR, and the battery certificate that governs the most expensive component in an electric Mercedes was last updated in October 2024 and does not name half the electric cars now on sale. Then there is the tax: 50 of the 62 published prices clear the supplement threshold, which is £2,200 over five years that the advertised figure does not show. None of that makes these bad cars, and the ten-year battery term on the EQS and EQE range is genuinely strong. It does mean a buyer has to get three things confirmed in writing before signing — the battery term for the specific model, whether DVLA will read the supplement off the list price or the on-the-road price on a car within a few hundred pounds of the threshold, and the APR. Ask for all three at the same time.
