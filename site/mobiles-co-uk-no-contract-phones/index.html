Mobiles.co.uk SIM-Free Phones: Prices, Warranty and the Small Print

Rates and terms checked 20 November 2026 · Just Phones · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Mobiles.co.uk sells handsets outright from £99.00, delivers every one of them free by DPD on next-day service if you order before 8pm, and prices five iPhones at exactly what Apple charges for them — £699, £799, £899, £1,099 and £1,199. Prices and wording checked on 20 November 2026 against the shop's own pages, Apple's UK store and Companies House.

The shop is older than almost anything else selling phones online here. Its own pages claim “25+ Years Online” and that it was the first website in the UK to sell mobile phones, and the copyright line on its help pages reads “© 1995 - 2026 Currys PLC”. The buying power behind it is real: the help page footer states that “Mobiles.co.uk is a trading name of Currys Retail Limited”, registered in England as company 2142673 at Currys Newark Campus, Long Hollow Way, Newark NG24 2NH — the same company that traded as The Carphone Warehouse Limited from 22 January 1991 until 4 October 2021, and that sits inside a group turning over £9,254m a year from 691 stores in six countries.

One thing is worth knowing before you click, because it decides who you deal with afterwards rather than what you pay. This is a contract shop, and the no-contract range runs on the contract shop's machinery. Every iPhone tile on the SIM-free listing is labelled “£0.00 Monthly Cost”, and the small print directly beneath the heading still sets out an annual increase to monthly prices each April. The handset you buy here has no monthly price at all.

Five iPhones cost what Apple charges, and seven cost less than Apple sells anything for

The Apple listing carried 43 priced tiles on 20 November 2026, at £309.00, £339.00, £379.00, £429.00, £449.00, £499.00, £549.00, £699.00, £799.00, £899.00, £999.00, £1,099.00 and £1,199.00. Set those against Apple's own UK store on the same day, which prices the iPhone 17e from £699, the iPhone 16 from £799, the iPhone 17 from £899, the iPhone Air from £1,099, the iPhone 18 Pro and 18 Pro Max from £1,199 and the iPhone Duo from £1,999.

Five of the shop's price points land on Apple's own figures to the pound. That is worth saying plainly, because a shopper's instinct is that a reseller adds a margin: on current iPhones this one does not, and the comparison takes one minute to run yourself. The £999.00 tier matches nothing on Apple's listing page, which is most likely a larger storage tier rather than a different model, and the £1,999 iPhone Duo does not appear on the shop's readable Apple page at all.

Below £699 the ranges part company, because Apple no longer sells anything cheaper. Seven of the shop's tiers — £549.00 down to £309.00 — sit under Apple's floor, the lowest of them £390 below it, on tiles describing a 6.1-inch LCD screen and wireless fast charging. Nothing on those tiles says whether the handset is older new stock or a refurbished unit, and the shop publishes no grading scale for SIM-free phones, so that is the question to put to the sales line on 0330 678 1450 before paying. Samsung's range runs £169.00 to £879.00 across 37 prices and Motorola's £99.00 to £1,399.00 across 14 priced tiles, so the shop's own claim to stock “over 100 different phones” is plausible on volume alone, though many tiles repeat for colour and capacity.

An April price increase, printed on a page where nothing is monthly

Directly under the heading “Sim Free Apple iPhone Deals”, and printed twice on the same page, sits this note: “*Each April: Vodafone monthly prices rise by £1.80 (£1 for Basics), Vodafone Out of Bundle & iD Mobile” — the sentence running on to say those prices rise by the Consumer Price Index rate plus 3.9%. Every product tile beneath it reads “£0.00 Monthly Cost”. It is a site-wide footnote that belongs on the contract listings, and on this page it is describing an increase to a price of zero.

Two of its figures do not match what the two networks publish themselves. Vodafone's own price-changes page, read the same day, says: “The monthly cost will increase each year on 1 April by £2.50 for Pay monthly plans with Airtime/Data, and £3.50 for Home Broadband plans. This doesn't affect Device Plans.” That is £2.50, not £1.80. The £1.80 is iD Mobile's number: iD's price-increases page gives “A £1.50 price increase each April” for a 24-month phone plan bought between 15/01/2025 and 10/03/2026 and “A £1.80 price increase each April” for one bought on or after 11/03/2026. The note has given one network the other's figure.

The second mismatch is the index. The shop's note groups iD Mobile with Vodafone's out-of-bundle charges under CPI plus 3.9%. Vodafone does say exactly that about out-of-bundle charges — “Out of bundle charges will increase each April by Consumer Price Index rate published in January of that year + 3.9%” — but iD Mobile's own page names no index at all, and states in terms that the increase “doesn't apply if: You are a SIM Only customer. You are a Pay as You Go customer. You purchased a 24 month phone plan before 15/01/2025.” iD also promises “a reminder SMS one month before your price increase is applied”.

The reason the wording matters beyond pedantry is that Ofcom outlawed this shape of term. Its statement of 19 July 2024 says that “From 17 January 2025, phone, broadband and pay TV providers will be prohibited from including inflation-linked, or percentage-based, price rise terms in all new contracts”, and that any rise written into a contract from that date “will need to be set out in pounds and pence, prominently and transparently, at the point of sale”. The prohibition bites on terms applying to the core subscription price, which leaves out-of-bundle charges outside it — so Vodafone's CPI-plus-3.9% line is lawful where it sits and the shop's version of it is not where the shop has put it. Ofcom's reason for acting is the useful part for a reader: it found 55% of broadband customers and 58% of pay-monthly mobile customers did not know what CPI and RPI measure, only 16% and 12% respectively were both aware of a rise and able to identify it as inflation-linked, and around six in ten customers were on such contracts as at April 2024, typically at 3.9% above inflation.

What £963 buys over two years, and what £99 buys once

The home page advertises three monthly deals: 30GB with unlimited minutes and texts at £75.00 up front and £37.00 a month, 60GB at £75.00 and £40.00, and 100GB at £150.00 and £47.00. No contract length is printed beside any of them. Taken over 24 months, which is the standard term these are sold on, they come to £963.00, £1,035.00 and £1,278.00 at the advertised price — and a plan bought in November 2026 passes two Aprils before it ends, so two annual increases land inside the term whichever network it runs on.

The no-contract route is the same shop's answer to that. Buy the £99.00 Motorola outright and add a SIM separately, and Ofcom's pricing report of 26 February 2026 puts average monthly in-bundle mobile spend at £14.59 and average pay-monthly SIM-only spend at about £18. At the £14.59 average, two years of airtime is £350.16, so handset and SIM together come to £449.16 — £513.84 less than the cheapest advertised contract.

That is not a like-for-like swap and the page should say so: the contract bundles a handset the shop does not name, and the data allowances are not the same. The honest version of the comparison needs the shop's own SIM-only prices alongside its handset prices, which is exactly what could not be done here, because the SIM-only listing refused to open. What the arithmetic does establish is the direction, and it is the reason a SIM-free page exists at all: paying once removes the two April steps, the early-exit charge and the question of what the airtime is worth, and leaves a single number you can check against the manufacturer.

The warranty line says twelve months. Samsung says twenty-four

Under the heading “Do SIM free phones have a warranty?”, the SIM-free page answers: “All SIM free phones come with the same 12 month warranty as contract phones..” — the double full stop is on the page. It adds that “If your handset develops a fault within the first month, we'll offer a free exchange”, with a free repair available after that.

Samsung's own UK warranty page, carrying a date of 10 November 2026, says something different about the same handsets. It states that “All Samsung products come with a standard manufacturer's warranty” and sets the period for a smartphone, including a Certified Re-Newed model, at 24 months. Tablets and Galaxy Watch are also 24 months; Galaxy Buds and wireless headphones are 12. So the shop's blanket twelve months matches Samsung's accessory term rather than its phone term, on a listing where Samsung handsets run from £169.00 to £879.00.

Nothing the retailer writes shortens the maker's warranty — that is a separate promise from Samsung to the buyer, and it survives whatever a retailer's FAQ says. The practical consequence is narrower and still real: a buyer who reads only this page will think cover ends at twelve months and may pay for a repair in month eighteen that Samsung would have covered. On top of either, the Consumer Rights Act 2015 gives a 30-day short-term right to reject goods that are faulty on arrival and, under section 23, a right to repair or replacement after that — rights against the seller, not the maker, and ones the shop's own pages never mention.

Two of the shop's pages send a broken phone to two different shops

Past the first month, the SIM-free page says a faulty handset can be booked in for a free repair “at your local Carphone Warehouse store”. The help pages on the same website say something else: “Within the first 12 months, you are entitled to a warranty fix through Currys”, and “To get your handset fixed, you just need to pop in to your local Currys store and they will be happy to help.”

Only one of those addresses exists. Carphone Warehouse's 531 standalone shops closed in 2020, with about 2,900 jobs going, and the company that held the name became Currys Retail Limited on 4 October 2021. Currys plc now reports 691 stores across six countries, 296 of them in the UK and Ireland block of its annual report for the year to 2 May 2026. A buyer following the SIM-free page's instruction in November 2026 is being sent to a shop estate that was wound up six years ago; a buyer following the help page is being sent somewhere real.

It is stale copy rather than a trap, and the fix is a sentence: take a faulty handset to a Currys store, or call customer services on 0330 678 0520. For an iD Mobile customer the help page gives a third route — “iD customers can go into a Currys store or call iD customer services on 0333 003 7777” — and Vodafone's own line is 191 or 03333 040 191. That three-way split is worth knowing because a dispute about a handset and a dispute about airtime go to different places, and only the airtime one has an adjudicator behind it.

Fourteen days, unused, in the original box — and what the regulations actually allow

The help pages set the change-of-mind window at fourteen days and say so three times over: “Please note: Return period is within 14 calendar days”; you must “inform us within 14 calendar days”; and you must “send the item back to our warehouse within 14 calendar days from the date you request the return”. A faulty exchange runs on a longer clock — “If you require a faulty exchange, you need to inform us within 30 days” — which lines up neatly with the statutory 30 days and is a point in the shop's favour.

The change-of-mind condition is the one to read twice: “We will only be able to offer a refund if the goods being returned are unused and in their original packaging”, with everything to be “returned undamaged and in the same condition they were first supplied”. Measure that against the rules that govern an online sale. Regulation 34 of the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, current to 17 November 2026, requires the trader to “reimburse all payments, other than payments for delivery”, forbids any charge for making the refund — “The trader must not impose any fee on the consumer in respect of the reimbursement” — and sets the deadline at 14 days from receiving the goods back or from being shown evidence they were sent.

What the regulations permit is a deduction, not a refusal. Regulation 34(9) lets a trader recover the amount by which value is “diminished by any amount as a result of handling of the goods by the consumer beyond what is necessary to establish the nature, characteristics and functioning of the goods”, up to the contract price, and regulation 34(12) defines the test as handling that “goes beyond the sort of handling that might reasonably be allowed in a shop”. Switching a phone on and looking at it is inside that test. Regulation 34(11) goes further and disapplies the deduction entirely where the trader has not given the required cancellation information. The shop's pages never name the regulations, never name the Consumer Rights Act, and describe the fourteen days as a “returns period” rather than a statutory right — and both of the returns-policy documents linked from the help page, one on media.mobiles.co.uk and one on media.secure-mobiles.com dated July 2022, refused to open, so who pays the return postage could not be established from any readable source.

A trading name on a 1987 company, and a namesake dissolved in 2013

The corporate picture is unusually tangled for a shop this plain, and it is all on the public record. Currys Retail Limited, company 02142673, was incorporated on 26 June 1987 as Notchwave Limited, became Professional Cellular Services Limited on 3 April 1989, became The Carphone Warehouse Limited on 22 January 1991 and took its present name on 4 October 2021. It is active, registered under SIC codes 47421 for retail sale of mobile telephones and 61900, filed accounts to 3 May 2025 with the next due by 31 January 2027, and confirmed its details on 19 August 2026.

There is also a company called Mobiles.co.uk Limited, number 06320783. It was incorporated on 23 July 2007 as Meaujo (736) Limited, renamed the following day, registered at 1 Portal Way, London W3 6RS, filed accounts to 3 April 2010 and was dissolved on 16 February 2013. So the brand's own namesake company has not existed for almost fourteen years, while the website trades under the name as a style of a different company — which is why the claim to “25+ Years Online” and to be the UK's first website selling mobile phones cannot be checked against any one company's incorporation date. The brand may well be that old; the corporate trail does not show it.

The group behind it publishes everything. Currys plc's annual report for the year to 2 May 2026, with statements dated 1 July 2026, gives revenue of £9,254m against £8,706m and £8,476m in the two prior years, adjusted profit before tax of £191m, statutory profit before tax of £153m, free cash flow up 5% to £157m, year-end net cash of £176m, a full-year dividend of 3.0p including a 2.25p final, £74m returned during the year through £50m of buybacks and £24m of dividends, and a further £50m buyback announced. UK and Ireland revenue is £5.4bn at a 17.5% market share on NielsenIQ and GfK point-of-sale data, the Nordics £3.8bn at 28.0%, and more than 24,000 colleagues were employed as at 2 May 2026 excluding fixed-term contracts. The group's own mobile network, iD Mobile, grew subscribers 18% to 2.6m, ahead of a 2.5m target. What the report does not do is name Mobiles.co.uk, and nor does the “At a Glance” page on the plc website, which lists three brands: Currys, iD Mobile and Elkjøp.

Free delivery by DPD, no charge at any basket size, and not to Jersey

Delivery is the cleanest part of the offer and the shop states it without hedging: “ALWAYS FREE - another of our promises to you. Regardless of what you order we won't charge you to receive it”, and “We've teamed up with DPD to deliver all our handsets.” There is no minimum spend, which matters on a £99.00 phone — at a rival charging £3.99 under £40, carriage on a cheap handset is a real fraction of the price. Orders placed before 8pm are quoted for next-day delivery and after 8pm for two-day. SIM-only orders are different: “Orders are delivered by Royal Mail within 3-5 working days”, with “No delivery tracking available”.

The edges are published too. Northern Scotland and UK offshore locations may take up to 5 working days, as may all GY and JE postcodes in the Channel Islands, and the page says flatly “Unfortunately we are unable to delivery to Jersey” — the spelling is the shop's. The address cannot be altered once an order is processed, dispatch can only go to a billing or registered work address, and an order can be cancelled before dispatch on 0330 678 0520. DPD's own customer service line, 08445 560 560, is printed on the page.

Two smaller things sit alongside that. The shop's contact details disagree with themselves: the help page gives customer service as 0330 678 0520 open Monday to Friday 9.00am to 7.00pm and Saturday to 5.30pm, while the home page gives 01509 615 474 open to 6.00pm on Saturday and 10.00am to 5.00pm on Sunday, with sales hours an hour longer than the help page allows. And the shop is right that its stock is unlocked — “the SIM free phones sold here at Mobiles.co.uk are all unlocked” — though that has been the law since Ofcom banned the sale of locked handsets on 17 December 2021, so it is compliance rather than a feature.

Pages that would not open, and the questions they leave

Eleven of the shop's addresses refused an automated read on 20 November 2026, and the pattern matters because of which ones they were. The terms and conditions, the returns page, the about page, the contact page, the privacy policy, the complaints page and the SIM-only listing all failed, as did both returns-policy PDFs and the site's own sitemap. Eight brand pages under the SIM-free section — Google, Nokia, HMD, Honor, Oppo, Xiaomi, Sony, OnePlus, Doro, TCL and Alcatel among the paths tried — failed as well, while Apple, Samsung and Motorola opened and printed their prices. The help page and the delivery page opened and carry most of what a buyer actually needs.

So the £99.00 floor is the cheapest price on the pages that opened, not a verified floor across the whole range, and the main SIM-free listing returned guide text with no product grid at all. Nor could the tiles be matched to model names: each one printed its features and its price but not its model, so the cheapest handset is identifiable as a Motorola with a 5,200 mAh battery and nothing more precise. That is a reading limitation rather than a fault in the shop, and the fix for a buyer is the same as for any phone purchase — get the exact model name and storage size in writing before paying, then price it at the manufacturer.

Two other things could not be established. There is no complaints procedure, alternative dispute resolution scheme or ombudsman named anywhere on the readable pages; Ofcom approves two telecoms schemes, CISAS and the Communications Ombudsman, and shortened the wait to use them from eight weeks to six on 8 April 2026, but a handset bought outright is a goods purchase and neither scheme reaches it, so a dispute here ends in the county court rather than with an adjudicator. And no independent rating of handset retail exists from anybody: Defaqto and Fairer Finance do not cover shops, Smart Money People does not cover retail, and Ofcom's complaints tables measure networks rather than the places that sell their phones. The shop's own claim to be “Winner of the Best Onlline Retailer 2019 at the Mobile Industry Awards and through to finalists for the 2023 awards” — the misspelling is on the page — could not be confirmed, because the awards site publishes only its 2026 winners and keeps no archive a visitor can reach.

Common questions

Who actually sells the phone when I buy from Mobiles.co.uk?

The help page footer states that “Mobiles.co.uk is a trading name of Currys Retail Limited”, registered in England as company 2142673 at Currys Newark Campus, Long Hollow Way, Newark NG24 2NH. That is the same company incorporated on 26 June 1987 as Notchwave Limited, which traded as The Carphone Warehouse Limited from 22 January 1991 and took its present name on 4 October 2021. A separate company called Mobiles.co.uk Limited, number 06320783, was dissolved on 16 February 2013 and has nothing to do with the current sale.

Is a SIM-free phone here cheaper than buying the same phone from Apple or Samsung?

On current iPhones it is the same price. Five of the shop's price points — £699.00, £799.00, £899.00, £1,099.00 and £1,199.00 — match Apple's own UK store exactly for the iPhone 17e, iPhone 16, iPhone 17, iPhone Air and iPhone 18 Pro. Below that the shop lists seven tiers down to £309.00, which is £390 under anything Apple sells. Samsung's range here runs £169.00 to £879.00, and Samsung's own UK prices for its cheapest handsets could not be read on 20 November 2026, so that comparison is not settled.

How long is the warranty on a phone bought outright?

The shop's SIM-free page says “All SIM free phones come with the same 12 month warranty as contract phones”. The manufacturer's own term can be longer and is not reduced by what a retailer writes: Samsung's UK warranty page, dated 10 November 2026, gives 24 months for a smartphone including its Certified Re-Newed models. Separately, the Consumer Rights Act 2015 gives you 30 days to reject a handset that is faulty on arrival and, under section 23, a right to repair or replacement after that — those are claims against the shop rather than the maker.

Why does a page selling phones outright mention an April price increase?

Because it is a site-wide footnote written for the contract listings. The note sits twice under the SIM-free Apple heading and sets out an annual April increase, while every tile beneath it reads “£0.00 Monthly Cost”. It is not a charge on a handset you buy outright. It is worth reading anyway, because two of its figures do not match the networks' own pages — it gives Vodafone £1.80 where Vodafone publishes £2.50, and puts iD Mobile under CPI plus 3.9% where iD names no index and excludes SIM-only customers.

Can I send a SIM-free phone back if I change my mind?

The help page sets the window at 14 calendar days to tell them and a further 14 calendar days to get the handset to the warehouse, and says a refund is available “only” where the goods are “unused and in their original packaging”. The regulations are more generous than that reads: regulation 34 of the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 lets a trader deduct for handling that “goes beyond the sort of handling that might reasonably be allowed in a shop”, rather than refuse the refund outright, and it forbids any fee for making the refund. Neither of the shop's two linked returns-policy documents would open, so who pays return postage is not published anywhere a buyer can read.

Where do I go if the phone develops a fault?

Take it to a Currys store. The help page says “Within the first 12 months, you are entitled to a warranty fix through Currys” and tells you to “pop in to your local Currys store”, and Currys plc reports 296 stores across the UK and Ireland. Ignore the SIM-free page's instruction to visit “your local Carphone Warehouse store” — that chain's 531 standalone shops closed in 2020. Customer services is 0330 678 0520, and an iD Mobile customer can also call 0333 003 7777.

Is there an ombudsman if the shop will not help?

Not for the handset. No complaints procedure, ADR scheme or ombudsman is named on any readable page of the site. Ofcom approves two telecoms schemes, CISAS and the Communications Ombudsman, and since 8 April 2026 you can escalate to them after six weeks rather than eight — but those cover a communications service, and a phone bought outright is a goods purchase, so a dispute about the handset ends in the county court instead. A dispute about airtime goes to whichever scheme your network belongs to.

Our verdict

On price, this is a better shop than its plain pages suggest: five iPhone tiers match Apple's own UK prices to the pound, the range runs from £99.00 to £1,399.00, delivery is free by DPD with no minimum spend, and a fault in the first 30 days is handled on the statutory clock rather than inside it. The seller is named outright on its own help page — a trading name of Currys Retail Limited, company 2142673 — and the group behind it publishes £9,254m of revenue and 691 stores. The weakness is not the pricing, it is the paperwork. A no-contract page that carries an annual monthly-increase schedule, attributes one network's figure to another, promises twelve months of warranty where Samsung gives twenty-four and sends a broken phone to a shop estate that closed in 2020 is a contract shop's machinery left running over a cash sale. None of that costs you money on the day; all of it costs you time if something goes wrong. Buy here for the price, get the exact model and storage in writing, keep the box, and go to the manufacturer rather than the FAQ when you need the warranty.
