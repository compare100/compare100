momondo Review: Free to Search, and the Date Under Its Advice

Rates and terms checked 20 November 2026 · Cheap Flights · Compare100 editorial team

momondo costs £0 to use: no subscription, no account and no momondo booking fee, and the travel site you click through to pays momondo for sending you — on its own page, “Prices come straight from the travel sites to your screen, with no extra fee from momondo”, which “makes our service totally free for our users”. A single search scans “hundreds of travel sites in seconds”, drawn from the 700+ provider sites behind the KAYAK group of search engines momondo joined in 2017. Figures checked 20 November 2026 against momondo's own pages.

It is a Danish brand with a long reach. Momondo A/S of Farvergade 10, Copenhagen operates the site, and the imprint gives a staffed phone number, +45 33378080, which is more than most flight search engines publish anywhere. The results cover flights, hotels, car hire, packages, trains and buses, and momondo tells you plainly which of those it ranks on price and which it ranks on something else.

Two things are worth knowing before you search. momondo is not the seller — the booking is made with the airline or agency named on the booking page, and momondo's own help pages say so in one line: “We are not a travel agency, and we don't sell tickets.” And its own guide to the cheapest time to book, which is the best-known thing on the site, rests on fares collected in August 2017.

Nothing to pay momondo, and the travel sites know why

The £0 holds up in two directions. momondo's summary of itself reads “We're completely free to use – no hidden charges or fees”, and its longer explanation says that when you take a deal, “the provider of that deal simply pays us for either redirecting you to their site or helping you to book with them”. There is a second income line as well: “Another way we earn money is by displaying travel-related advertisements on our site”, and “Some companies even pay us when you click on an advert that interests you”. On the packages tab each supplier “will pay for clicks or bookings that they get via momondo”.

So the fare on the screen is the airline's or the agency's, not a marked-up version of it. What the fare does not always include is set out in the terms: “additional charges (e.g., payment fees, services charges, checked-in luggage fees, local taxes and fees) may apply”. On the train and bus tab momondo goes further and says of its partners that “Some partners may charge for an additional service fee”. Neither of those is momondo taking money from you; both are reasons the number you end up paying can be higher than the one you clicked.

The coverage claim is honest about its own limits, which is unusual. momondo says it searches “hundreds of travel sites” and then adds “While we do search as many sites as we can, we're still not able to search them all” and “our results may not always reflect all available deals”. The terms put the same point in colder words: “We usually have many search results, but we are not comprehensive and do not display all available providers and offers.” One detail on that page is easy to miss and tells you something about where momondo now sits: the sentence about partnerships with most of the 700+ provider sites in the results is written about KAYAK, not about momondo.

Sixty days ahead — and the date underneath that figure

momondo's UK guide to finding cheap flights is specific in a way almost nothing else in flight search is. It breaks the advice down by region and attaches a percentage to every recommendation. For flights within Europe, “The best time to book is 60 days in advance, which could save you up to 30%”; the best time of day to fly is the evening, worth “up to 8%”, and “the best day of the week to fly is on Tuesday, saving you up to 12%”.

The same shape repeats across five more regions. Europe to North America: 60 days ahead saves “up to 26%”, evening flights “11%”, Tuesday “up to 10%”. Europe to Africa: 60 days, “up to 29%”, evening “up to 11%”, Tuesday “up to 9%”. Europe to South America: 60 days, “up to 25%”, noon “up to 6%”, Tuesday “up to 7%”. Europe to Asia: “Book 60 days in advance to save up to 24%”, morning “up to 8%”, Tuesday “up to 8%”. Europe to Oceania is the one exception to the round number — “59 days before your trip”, for “up to 21%”, with morning flights at “up to 6%” and Tuesday at “up to 7%”. Tuesday is the cheapest day on all six routes, which is the single most reusable line on the page.

Then read the footnote. The numbers are “Based on an analysis of more than 100 million fares from searches done on 1704 routes across all momondo domains” “between 06/08/16 – 07/08/17”, and “Data collection took place on 08/08/17”. The sample is enormous and the dates are stated honestly, which is better than most published travel advice manages. It is also a search year that ended in August 2017, and the page's own metadata carries a last-modified date of 24 January 2026. Airline pricing has been through a pandemic, a fuel shock and a capacity squeeze since those fares were captured, and momondo does not say how the percentages were worked out — only how many fares went in.

One thing that looks like a contradiction is not one. momondo's international version of the same guide gives different figures — 60 days ahead for “up to 34%”, booking on the day of travel costing “up to 36% more”, Tuesday worth “up to 11%”, Saturdays adding “up to 10%” and evening departures saving “up to 9%”. Those are global averages across every route in the study; the UK page's 12% is the Europe-to-Europe subset of the same data. Different scopes, same study, and the two are not in conflict.

Two addresses for one brand: Copenhagen and Connecticut

The imprint is unambiguous about who runs the site. “Momondo A/S is a public limited company under Danish law with headquarters in Copenhagen”, at Farvergade 10, 1 DK-1463 Copenhagen K, “registered in the Danish commercial register under CVR no. 20181397”, with “VAT identification number: DK 20181397” and a telephone number, +45 33378080. The contacts page says the same thing in a different format: “If you visit ‘momondo’: Momondo A/S, Farvergade 10, 1 DK-1463 Copenhagen K, Denmark”.

The terms of use name a different company for the same word. There, “‘we’, ‘us’, ‘our’ and ‘momondo’ refers to KAYAK Software Corporation” — a Connecticut company. Those terms are governed by Connecticut law without regard to its conflict-of-laws rules, any action has to be filed in the state and federal courts in New Haven, and disputes “will be resolved individually, without resort to any form of class action”. The terms have been effective since September 2018 and were last updated in March 2026.

Both documents are accurate and they govern different things: the Danish company operates the site and is the data controller a European user deals with, while the contract you accept by using the search is with the American one. The practical upshot for a British traveller is worth stating flatly. No UK-registered company is named anywhere on momondo's own pages as responsible for the momondo site, there is no UK telephone number, and the forum named for a dispute is a courthouse in Connecticut. The privacy notice, effective 25 July 2024, points the same way: the controller is “KAYAK Software Corporation and its subsidiaries, or subsidiaries of our parent company Booking Holdings Inc. that we manage”, and it covers eight brands at once — “KAYAK, momondo, HotelsCombined, SWOODOO, Cheapflights, checkfelix, Mundi and roomsearch”.

The $550 million that ended momondo's independence

momondo was a competitor before it was a stablemate. On 7 February 2017 The Priceline Group — now Booking Holdings — “signed a definitive agreement to acquire the Momondo Group in a cash transaction” and “will acquire all outstanding shares of the company for a price of $550 million”. The deal closed on 24 July 2017, when the buyer confirmed it had “acquired all outstanding shares of the Momondo Group for approximately $550 million”. The seller was a private equity house: Momondo Group was “currently a portfolio company of Great Hill Partners”.

What was bought was two brands, not one. The Momondo Group “operates momondo, a leading European travel meta engine that offers flights, hotels and car rentals, and Cheapflights, a leading global flight comparison and travel deals publishing platform”, and both “will roll under The Priceline Group's leading travel meta brand, KAYAK”. The press release is specific about the reporting line: “Headquartered in the UK and Copenhagen, with an office in Boston, the Momondo Group businesses will report into KAYAK CEO Steve Hafner.” Nothing in either announcement says the brands would be run independently.

The parent's own annual report for 2017 files momondo away in a single clause: “in 2017 we expanded our KAYAK meta-search business, in particular in Europe, through the acquisition of the Momondo Group”, and lists it among the sites through which “KAYAK offers its services in over 60 countries, with the United States being its largest market, through various websites, including Momondo and Cheapflights (websites we acquired in 2017)”. momondo is not one of the parent's primary brands and no revenue, booking or visitor figure is published for it separately — a reader who wants to know how big momondo is will not find out from the company that owns it.

What the UK register shows above the Cheapflights name

There is a UK company in this story, and the register tells its history better than any about-us page. MOMONDO GROUP LIMITED, company number 03914064, is active, a private limited company, registered at The Monument Building, 11 Monument Street, London EC3R 8AF under SIC 63120 (web portals), and it filed full accounts for the year to 31 December 2025 on 2 October 2026. It was incorporated on 26 January 2000 — and it has carried four earlier names. It began as SHARPICAL LIMITED, became CHEAPFLIGHTS.COM LIMITED on 2 July 2001, CHEAPFLIGHTS LIMITED on 17 January 2003, CHEAPFLIGHTS MEDIA LIMITED on 30 July 2010 and MOMONDO GROUP LIMITED on 8 November 2012. The corporate vehicle behind the Danish brand is the old Cheapflights company with a new name on it.

Above it sits MOMONDO GROUP HOLDINGS LIMITED, company 09258528, incorporated 10 October 2014, at the same London address, which the register records as the person with significant control over 03914064. The holding company's own controlling entry names Priceline.Com International Limited, with a change of details recorded on 14 October 2024. A third company shares that building: KAYAK SOFTWARE (UK) LIMITED, number 06076185, incorporated 31 January 2007, active, SIC 82990, accounts also made up to 31 December 2025.

Two caveats belong with that. A search of the UK register for “momondo” returns the two Group companies and nothing named simply Momondo, so the UK company is the Cheapflights entity rather than a British home for the momondo site — and momondo's own contacts page gives Momondo Group Ltd's address as 100 New Bridge Street, London EC4V 6JA, which is not the registered office the register shows today. What the filed accounts contain could not be established on this pass: Companies House refused both accounts documents, so no turnover, profit or headcount figure for the UK company is quoted here, and anyone who needs those will have to open the filings directly.

Hiding your fees gets a provider demoted, and momondo says so

This is the most creditable sentence on momondo's own how-it-works page, and it is the sort of thing a search engine is not obliged to publish. On flight results, momondo says it will “highlight things like a provider's booking or baggage fees” — and then: “if a provider isn't upfront with their fees, making their prices less clear, we'll tend not to recommend them.” It also says “We sometimes also judge the quality of the service you're getting from a provider site.”

A metasearch that is paid per click has an obvious incentive to show the lowest-looking number regardless of what it turns into at checkout. Writing down that the opposite rule applies is a commitment a reader can hold momondo to, and it is a better consumer protection than any badge. Note what it is not: momondo does not publish the list of providers it has demoted, or the test it applies, or how much weight the judgement carries against a provider paying more per click. The sentence is a stated policy rather than a measurable one.

The reviews on the site work on a similar principle of disclosure without detail. “We show reviews and review scores from other well-known travel websites”, and “Our review scores are calculated by averaging the scores of reviews that we collect and reviews collected by our partners”. Old scores are retired: momondo “may stop showing reviews once they are three years old - or if there has been a change of ownership”. Three years is a sensible cut-off, and it sits oddly beside booking advice built on fares from 2017.

momondo's own revenue is one of the things ordering your results

Flights have the cleanest default. momondo says that “by default flights are ranked to give you the best trade-off between price and convenience,” “based on factors such as duration, price, distance between origin and destination,” and you can override it: “Our price sort gives you quick access to the cheapest and/or the most expensive deals”. Once you have picked a flight and are choosing which seller to buy it from, the basis changes — “the ranking between these search results will be based mainly on click-through-rate and cost per click.”

On the other tabs, momondo's own income is an explicit input. Hotel results are ordered by a “Recommended” algorithm using price, guest ratings and “its popularity (measured in clicks)”, and momondo adds: “We also factor in the average revenue potential for momondo from each hotel result.” Car hire is “mainly prioritized by price, revenue potential for momondo,” and on packages “we order our results based on an internal algorithm that balances the prices and our revenue”. Across the site, “Providers can also become highlighted if they pay more for the clicks they get.” Trains and buses are the one tab with no money in the sort at all: “We have sorted our train and bus results by departure time”.

None of that is hidden, and publishing it is the right thing to do. It does mean the word “recommended” is doing two jobs on the same website — best for you on flights, part best-for-you and part best-for-momondo on hotels, cars and packages. If you are comparing anything other than a flight here, switch the sort to price before you read the page.

When a booking goes wrong, momondo is not the one you ring

momondo states this more plainly than many of its competitors, and a traveller should take it at face value. The terms say “Our Website is a travel search engine” and that “momondo does not provide, own or control any of the travel services and products that you can access through Our Website”. Where you book, “that booking is made with the Travel Provider named on the booking page”, and momondo “only acts as a user interface”. The provider sets the terms: “momondo has no involvement in creating the description of the Travel Product, in defining the price and any fees,” and “The Travel Providers are responsible for the Travel Products.”

The consequence is spelled out twice. If something goes wrong, “you agree to address and resolve these with the Travel Provider and not with us”, and “momondo does not bear any responsibility should anything go wrong with your booking or during your travel”. The help pages repeat it without the legal wrapping: “If you have questions concerning a booking, please contact the travel agency or airline company”, and the feedback page says “momondo is a travel search engine, not a provider” and tells you to “reach out to the company listed on your confirmation email”.

That is correct as a matter of law and it still leaves a gap worth knowing about. Your financial and cancellation protection comes entirely from whoever sold you the ticket, so the name on the booking page is the thing to check before you pay — an airline, a UK agency holding an ATOL where one is required, or an overseas agency with neither. momondo holds no travel licence, claims none, and is not in a position to help you recover money. Its help pages do carry one useful warning in the same spirit: contact that appears to come from momondo by email, WhatsApp or phone about a specific booking is not from momondo but from “imposters who are trying to defraud you”.

One complaints figure, published because the law requires it

The imprint carries a small table most readers will never scroll to, headed with a reference to Article 11 of Regulation (EU) 2019/1150. That regulation makes online intermediation platforms run an internal complaint-handling system for the businesses that sell through them, and publish how it is going. momondo's entry reads “Total number of complaints lodged: 0” and “Average time to process and resolve complaints: N/A”, with “Last Updated: February 2025”.

Read it for what it is. The complaints counted there are from travel companies, not from travellers, so a zero does not mean no passenger has ever been unhappy — it means no provider has used the formal channel. It is still a real disclosure, and the date on it is the interesting part: February 2025, which makes it 21 months old at the time of writing. A figure a platform has to keep current is a reasonable thing to expect to be current.

For a traveller the practical point is the route, not the number. Beyond the Danish phone line and feedback@momondo.com in the imprint, momondo's only published channel is a feedback form, it sets no response time, and its terms say in terms that while “We encourage you to share your comments and questions with us”, “we may not be able to respond to all of them.” For a free search tool that takes none of your money, that is a defensible position. If you want a company that will answer the phone about your trip, it is the airline or agency on your confirmation.

Where it wins

  • Free with no catch to find: no subscription, no account needed, no momondo booking fee, and the fare you see is the provider's own
  • Publishes how its results are ordered, including where click-through rate, cost per click and momondo's own revenue potential enter the sort
  • Says outright that a provider which is not upfront about its booking and baggage fees will tend not to be recommended
  • Gives region-by-region booking advice with a percentage attached to each recommendation, and footnotes the sample size and date range behind it
  • Admits its own coverage limits rather than claiming to search everything — “we're still not able to search them all”
  • Publishes a real operator, a Danish company number and VAT number, and a staffed telephone number, which several rival search engines do not
  • Retires review scores once they are three years old, or if the property changes hands
  • Trains and buses are sorted by departure time, with no revenue weighting in that tab at all

Where it falls short

  • Its best-known advice — book 60 days ahead, fly on a Tuesday — rests on fares collected on 08/08/17 from a search year that ended in August 2017, on a page whose own metadata says it was modified on 24 January 2026
  • momondo does not publish how those percentages were calculated: the sample size (more than 100 million fares, 1704 routes) is given, the method is not
  • The word “recommended” means different things on different tabs — momondo's own revenue potential is an input to the hotel, car hire and package sorts
  • No UK-registered company is named as responsible for the momondo site, there is no UK phone number, and the terms put any dispute under Connecticut law in the courts of New Haven, with class actions excluded
  • The address momondo publishes for Momondo Group Ltd, 100 New Bridge Street EC4V 6JA, is not the registered office the UK register shows for company 03914064
  • No complaints route with a stated response time: beyond a Danish phone number and an email address in the imprint there is only a feedback form, and the terms warn that “we may not be able to respond to all of them”
  • momondo holds no travel licence and carries no responsibility for a booking, so all financial and cancellation protection depends on which seller you land on
  • Booking Holdings publishes no revenue, booking or visitor figure for momondo on its own, and the UK accounts could not be opened to supply one
  • The business-user complaints disclosure required by Regulation (EU) 2019/1150 still reads zero and was last updated in February 2025

Common questions

Does momondo charge a booking fee?

No. momondo says it is “completely free to use – no hidden charges or fees” and that prices reach your screen “with no extra fee from momondo”. It is paid by the travel sites, which pay for the redirect or the booking, and by advertisers. The seller you click through to may still add its own charges: momondo's terms note that “payment fees, services charges, checked-in luggage fees, local taxes and fees” can apply, and some train and bus partners charge a service fee.

Is it really cheapest to book 60 days before a flight?

That is what momondo's own guide says, with 60 days quoted for five of the six regions it covers and 59 days for Oceania, and claimed savings of up to 30% within Europe, 29% to Africa, 26% to North America, 25% to South America, 24% to Asia and 21% to Oceania. The footnote matters as much as the figure: the study drew on more than 100 million fares across 1704 routes searched between 06/08/16 and 07/08/17, with data collection on 08/08/17. It is a large, well-documented sample from a long time ago, so treat the 60 days as a rule of thumb and set a price alert rather than relying on it.

Who am I actually buying the ticket from?

The airline or agency named on the booking page. momondo's help pages say “We are not a travel agency, and we don't sell tickets”, and the terms say the booking “is made with the Travel Provider named on the booking page” while momondo “only acts as a user interface”. Everything that follows — the payment, the ticket, any refund, any ATOL or chargeback protection — comes from that seller, so read the name before you pay.

Who owns momondo?

Booking Holdings Inc., through its KAYAK business. The Priceline Group agreed to buy the Momondo Group on 7 February 2017 for $550 million in cash and completed on 24 July 2017, with the brands set to “roll under” KAYAK and report to KAYAK's chief executive. The site itself is operated by Momondo A/S in Copenhagen, CVR 20181397, while momondo's terms of use define “momondo” as KAYAK Software Corporation of Connecticut.

How do I complain to momondo?

Through its feedback form, or the Danish contacts in the imprint — feedback@momondo.com and +45 33378080. There is no UK telephone number and no stated response time, and the terms say that while comments and questions are encouraged, “we may not be able to respond to all of them”. The complaints table momondo publishes under Article 11 of Regulation (EU) 2019/1150 covers complaints from the travel companies that sell through it, not from travellers, and it reads zero as at February 2025. For anything to do with a trip you have booked, complain to the airline or agency on your confirmation instead.

Does momondo show every flight available?

No, and it says so. momondo scans “hundreds of travel sites” and draws on the 700+ provider sites behind the group's search engines, but adds “While we do search as many sites as we can, we're still not able to search them all” and that “our results may not always reflect all available deals”. The terms are blunter: “we are not comprehensive and do not display all available providers and offers”. Checking one airline's own site alongside a search here still occasionally pays.

Our verdict

As a free way to see who is selling a flight and for how much, momondo earns its place: nothing to pay, hundreds of travel sites in one search, a fare set by the seller rather than the search engine, and an unusually candid account of how the results are ordered — including the parts where momondo's own income is an input. The rule that a provider hiding its fees will tend not to be recommended is a genuine consumer commitment and more than most competitors put in writing. Treat two things with care. The famous booking advice is precise, well-sampled and dated: 60 days ahead and a Tuesday departure came out of fares collected on 08/08/17, so use it as a rule of thumb and not as this week's price forecast. And remember who you are buying from — momondo does not sell tickets, the contract is with the airline or agency named on the booking page, and that name is where every refund, rebooking and complaint will have to go. Check it before you pay, and momondo is a good place to start a search and the wrong place to end one.
