Moss Review: What a Return Costs, What a Hire Costs, and the Deadlines Behind Both

Sending a suit back to Moss costs £3.99 by post, deducted from the refund, or nothing if you walk it into one of the shops, and a straight swap for a different size carries no courier fee either way. Delivery is £4.95 and free over £200, hire starts at £119.95 for a seven-day lounge suit, and the business has been tailoring since 1851. Figures checked 21 November 2026 against Moss’s own pages.

The thing to know before you click is that Moss is two businesses on two websites, and only one of them lets you change your mind about the fit. moss.co.uk sells suits to keep, with the 28-day window above. mossbroshire.co.uk hires them out, and that contract runs the other way: hire garments “cannot be altered in any way”, Moss “is not liable to provide alternative sizes or product for any errors made in self-sizing or online order creation”, and “We are not liable for any error discovered once you have left the store”. The hire catalogue, the prices and the fee list all live on the separate site, so a reader who lands on the shop and wants a wedding outfit has one more hop to make.

Two hire deadlines disqualify a lot of readers outright, and they are stated flatly here rather than buried. Moss will only take a hire order online if your wear date is “7 working days or more from the creation date”, and the hire terms want everybody’s measurements “no less than 60 days prior to your collection or delivery date” — 30 days for the junior range. Miss it badly enough and the order goes: “Failure to provide sizes 14 days before your collection or delivery date will result in your order being automatically cancelled and treated as a cancellation by you.” Inside 30 days, a cancellation by you costs the full order value.

Twenty-eight days on a suit you buy, and the fee that comes out of the refund

The retail side is the generous half of Moss and it is worth setting out properly. Buy online and you have 14 days from receiving the order to tell Moss through the returns portal, and then, in the policy’s own words, “you have a further 14 days to return the goods to us”. Twenty-eight days in total. The postal route costs £3.99, and Moss is unusually blunt about where that lands: “you will be charged a fee of £3.99 towards the cost of the courier”, and “This fee will be deducted from any refund amount due.” Carry the parcel into a shop instead and there is nothing to pay — “There are no fees to return unwanted items to Moss stores nationwide” — and a size swap is free whichever way you send it, because “We will not charge a courier fee in the case of an item being returned for an exchange to a different size.”

Do the arithmetic a shopper actually faces. One suit ordered on standard delivery costs £4.95 to arrive and £3.99 to go back, so trying a jacket at home and rejecting it is a £8.94 exercise. Spend over £200 and the delivery half disappears. Walk into a branch to hand it over and the whole £8.94 disappears, which on a chain of this size is a realistic option rather than a theoretical one.

The fee is lawful, and saying why is more use to a reader than complaining about it. Under the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, a purchase of goods is a “sales contract” — regulation 5 defines it as a contract under which “a trader transfers or agrees to transfer the ownership of goods to a consumer” — and regulation 30(3) starts the 14-day cancellation clock on the day the goods “come into the physical possession of” the buyer. The Regulations let the shopper bear the direct cost of sending them back, provided the trader said so up front, which Moss does on three separate pages. The 28 days is therefore the statutory shape of the thing, not a Moss promotion.

One wrinkle in the paperwork is worth knowing if you are close to the line. Clause 10.1 of the terms counts the 14 days “from the day on which the Goods were received”, while clause 10.3 and the returns page both count from receipt of the order. On a single-parcel order those are the same day. On a split delivery they are not, and nothing on the site says which governs.

Refunds are slow and Moss says so, which is better than the alternative. Allow “up to 7 days to receive them back to our processing centre”, “up to a further 7 days to inspect and process your return”, and “up to 7 days for the refund to appear back onto your payment card”, for a stated total of “up to 3 weeks”. Faulty goods run on a different clock — clause 8.4 asks you to “notify us within 30 days of receipt of the items” — and gift cards are outside all of it: “Gift Cards are non-returnable and non-refundable.”

The alteration that ends the right to send it back

This is the sharpest thing on the retail side and it catches exactly the customer Moss is built for. An off-the-peg suit almost never fits a real body in the sleeve and the trouser at the same time, so Moss sells the fix: “Moss stores partner with local professional tailors to provide an alterations service”, and “All alterations are also an extra cost on top of the price of the product.” No price for that work is published anywhere on the site.

Take it up and the return right is gone. The returns policy is unambiguous — “Unfortunately we cannot refund or exchange altered or personalised items” — and the help centre states the mechanism outright: “any alterations made will nullify our returns policy.” The sale-items page says the same thing from the other direction, requiring returns to be “unworn, unaltered and in their original packaging”. The policy’s own advice is the only defence available: “Please ensure that you are truly happy with any product before any alterations are made.”

There is a legal thread behind it. Regulation 28(1) of the 2013 Regulations takes goods made to a consumer’s own specification outside the cancellation right altogether, and clause 10.7 of the Moss terms leans on exactly that: “the right of cancellation does not apply where Goods are specifically made to your specification or personalised at your request.” A suit taken in at the waist by a tailor the shop introduced you to is a harder case than a monogrammed shirt, and the policy does not distinguish between them. The practical reading is that the shortening of a trouser leg and the loss of a £3.99 return route happen in the same appointment, and nobody in the shop is obliged to say so first.

Made-to-measure is governed separately and more tightly. Custom Made takes “a 50% deposit on the day you place your order and the remaining balance when you collect”, allows amendments for “24 hours from the time you pay your deposit, including weekends and bank holidays”, promises collection “28 days after you pay your deposit”, and gives you 30 days from that date to try it on, after which “We can only accept issues with the order within 30 days of the delivery date.” Cancel after the cooling-off period and “we will charge a cancellation fee equal to your balance” — the whole price. Alterations to put right Moss’s own work are free: “we will make alterations at our own cost”, under “a strict policy of repair, replace, and finally refund”. Leave the garment unclaimed for 30 days and “we reserve the right to dispose of it.”

Hiring is a different contract, and the clock starts somewhere else

Nothing in the 28 days above applies to a hire, and the reason is structural rather than mean-spirited. Hiring transfers no ownership, so it is not a “sales contract” under regulation 5 of the 2013 Regulations; it answers instead to the definition of a “service contract”, which is “a contract, other than a sales contract, under which a trader supplies or agrees to supply a service to a consumer”. Regulation 30(2) then moves the starting gun: for a service contract “the cancellation period ends at the end of 14 days after the day on which the contract is entered into.” Not after delivery. Fourteen days from the moment you book, whether the morning suit arrives in April or in August.

Moss’s hire terms track that precisely, and the limits they place on the cooling-off period are the Regulations’ limits rather than the company’s. Clause 12 gives a “14-day cooling-off period to cancel your order with a full refund” only “If your order is made online, and you have not visited one of our stores at any point”, and switches it off where “your order is made within 14 days of the collection or delivery date”. Regulation 29(1) only ever gave a cancellation right on a “distance or off-premises contract”, and regulation 5 calls anything else an “on-premises contract”. So a groom who walks into a branch, gets measured and books the party there has no statutory cooling-off period at all — every pound he can get back afterwards is a matter of Moss’s own cancellation table, not of consumer law.

There is a further carve-out the hire terms do not mention, and it is printed here because the reader is better off knowing the question exists. Regulation 28(1)(h) removes the cancellation right from contracts for “the supply of accommodation, transport of goods, vehicle rental services, catering or services related to leisure activities, if the contract provides for a specific date or period of performance.” A wedding outfit booked for one named Saturday is a date-specific hire, and whether the phrase reaches it is a question nobody has settled in print. Moss gives the 14 days on online orders anyway, so the point is academic for an online booker and worth asking about for anyone else.

What Moss charges to get out of a hire sits on a three-band table, and the bands are measured in days before collection. At “61 or more” days you get “a full refund of your deposit, minus £25 per individual order”. At “60 to 31”, “We will keep the deposit of the cancelled individual order” — half the price, since the deposit is 50%. At “30 days or less”, “We will keep the full individual order value.” Changes are harder than cancellations: “we will not be able to process any changes received 21 days or less before the agreed collection or delivery date”, and even inside 60 days Moss “cannot commit to any changes”. An amendment may carry a fee, and no amount for it is published.

What a hire really costs once the deposit and the cover are in

The advertised prices are real and readable, which puts Moss ahead of several menswear sites that print no price a text fetch can reach. The lounge suit rail runs from £119.95 for the Slim Fit Blue Slub and the Tailored Fit Navy Black Check, through £129.95 for the tweeds and the Black Performance, to £139.95 for the Blue Twill and the Navy, Neutral and Bottle Green Performance suits. Formal wear costs more and is priced as a package: the Tailored Fit White Tie Tails is £209.95 as a three-piece “Jacket, Trousers and Waistcoat”, or £234.95 for the full package with the Marcella waistcoat, shirt and bow tie. All of it buys seven days.

Then come the numbers that are not on the product page. A card is taken at collection as a £450 security deposit, with “£1.01 may be taken, then put straight back into your account as we authorise your card details” — that deposit is 3.5 times the price of the cheapest suit in the range. The Peace of Mind Waiver is £9.95 an outfit, which is 7.7% on top of a £129.95 hire. Getting the outfit to you by courier is £9.95 for DPD before 6.30pm, £12 by noon, £20 by 10.30am and £15 on a Saturday, with delivery into a store at under seven days’ notice costing £10; sending it back by courier is another £10, against free to any hire store. Courier both ways on a £119.95 suit adds £19.95, a sixth of the hire again.

Late is the expensive failure. The fee list reads “Late Return (Non Ascot Orders) | £10 per day, per order”, and the returns page puts it in the second person: “You will incur a £10 penalty for each day your return is late.” A suit a week late therefore costs £70 on top — more than half the hire price of the cheapest suit in the catalogue, and on an Ascot booking the charge is “The cost of the original hire per day”, so a week late on a £129.95 Ascot outfit is arithmetic nobody wants to do. Past seven days the matter closes: “Failure to return your order within 7 days of the agreed returns date will result in your security deposit, captured at the time of collection, to be redeemed for the value for all items at their current retail selling price.”

Damage is where the £9.95 earns itself or does not. With cover, “any damage or spillage deemed repairable by a tailor/cleaner will be covered”. Without it, “you will be charged a fee of £20 to restore the garment”, so the waiver pays for itself the first time a glass of red goes over a lapel and is money thrown away otherwise. What it never covers is the big loss: “Peace of Mind cover does not cover unreturned garments, loss of items, or intentional damage”, and “Non-return, loss and theft of hire garments are not covered by Peace of Mind Waiver.” Anything beyond repair is billed “at the current selling price”, as is any item you alter yourself, and the damaged-items page warns that “any intentional damage, bodily fluid or excessive stains upon an item will lead to the garment being considered as beyond repair”. Spillages carry their own £20 line on the fee list.

Sixty days, sixty-four days, and the deadline that cancels your order for you

The hire proposition stands or falls on measurements taken weeks in advance, and the site gives two different deadlines for them. The hire terms require everybody to be measured “no less than 60 days prior to your collection or delivery date”, and the FAQ agrees: “we ask for sizes to be provided no later than 60 days before your event date.” The sizing page asks for something else — “please ensure it is no later than 64 days before your event” — a four-day gap between two live pages on one website, with nothing to say which is the one that bites. The junior range runs on its own clock, “no later than 30 days prior”.

Miss it altogether and Moss cancels for you, in your name. “Failure to provide sizes 14 days before your collection or delivery date will result in your order being automatically cancelled and treated as a cancellation by you.” Read that against the cancellation table and the cost is plain: 14 days before collection sits inside the “30 days or less” band, where “We will keep the full individual order value”. A groomsman who never sends his chest measurement costs the booker £119.95 at the cheap end of the rail and £234.95 on white tie, with nothing hired and nothing worn.

You can be measured in a shop — “Arrange a sizing appointment with one of our Tailoring Experts in-store” — or submit your own, and the site is honest that the second route carries the risk. “Hire garments are naturally subject to slight differences in fit, so it’s best to try them on in person if you can”, and the terms remove the remedy: “Moss is not liable to provide alternative sizes or product for any errors made in self-sizing or online order creation.” Everything is in inches, “all of our sizes are measured in inches”, across four fits where “a skinny fit suit will be cut very close on the shoulders and thighs” and “you may need to size up for this fit”. Jackets cover chest 32in to 60in and trousers waist 26in to 56in, a genuinely wide rail, and the junior range stops at 10–11 years with no tuxedo in it at all.

The last chance to fix a fit is the counter. “You’ll need to try on your order when you collect it so our store team are on hand to assist”, the terms ask you to raise problems “at the time of collection so we have the opportunity to assist you”, and then close the door: “We are not liable for any error discovered once you have left the store.” On a home delivery the window is a single day — “You must notify us of any issues regarding your order on the day of delivery.” And the one remedy a man would reach for is forbidden: “Hire garments cannot be altered in any way”, with the fee list pricing a hirer’s own alteration at “The item’s current retail selling price”. Even iron-on hemming tape counts, per the FAQ, as damage.

Where the paperwork disagrees with itself

None of these is damaging on its own. Together they are the reason to read a Moss quote rather than assume it, and all six were read on 21 November 2026.

  • The middle cancellation band says two things. The hire terms keep “the deposit of the cancelled individual order” between 60 and 31 days out; the fee list says “50% of the cancelled order value”. Those happen to coincide while the deposit is 50%, and stop coinciding the moment anybody pays in full early — which Moss invites you to do.
  • The boundary moves by a day. The terms’ table reads “30 days or less”; the fee list reads “Cancellation within 31 days of your collection/delivery”.
  • Courier delivery is £8.95 on one page and £9.95 on two others. The FAQ quotes “a cost of £8.95 for UK mainland delivery”; the fee list and the delivery page both say £9.95 for DPD before 6.30pm.
  • The waiver excludes different things depending on where you read it. One page: “Hired top hats, suit covers, hat boxes and bags are not covered”. Another: “top hats, suit covers, hat boxes and kilts”. The terms themselves: “Top hats, top hat boxes and kilts”. One page describes cover for “accidental damage”, the others for “accidental repairable damage”, which is a materially narrower promise.
  • Two sentences are unfinished in public. The FAQ answer on late returns reads “If you return your hire after the return date, you’ll be charged .” with no figure in it, and the hire delivery page still carries its own editorial note to itself: “These costs can be found at (add link to charges page)”.
  • One help article is six years out of date in a way Companies House can prove. The Channel Islands returns article gives the address as “Moss Bros Group Plc, Unit B 13 Gascoigne Road, Barking, IG11 7HP, UK.” The company stopped being a plc on 26 June 2020, when MOSS BROS GROUP PLC became Moss Bros Group Limited, and every other page on both sites says “Ltd”. The Custom Made page carries an eight-digit VAT number, “VAT NO.23886422”, beside a 2022 copyright line, where every current footer gives the nine-digit “VAT No. 238864229”.

Who you are dealing with, and what happened to the company in 2020

The brand is older than the company that owns it. Moss dates itself to 1851 and describes itself as having “over 170 years around the (tailoring) block”, which on a 2026 reading is 175 years; the limited company you actually contract with, Moss Bros Group Ltd, was incorporated on 1 April 1914, sixty-three years into the story. Companies House holds it as number 00134995, active, at 128 Albert Street, London NW1 7NE — an address it moved to on 21 October 2024 from 8 St Johns Hill, Clapham Junction — under SIC 47710, retail sale of clothing in specialised stores. Its previous names are the corporate history in two lines: MOSS BROS PLC until 8 July 1988, then MOSS BROS GROUP PLC until 26 June 2020.

That June 2020 date is the one that matters. The listed company was taken private by a bid vehicle called Brigadier Acquisition Company Limited, incorporated on 14 October 2019 and renamed Moss Bros Group Holdings Limited on 12 September 2020; it is company 12260782 and files under SIC 64209 as a holding company. The shop end then went through a formal restructuring, and the filing history records the end of it rather than the beginning: a CVA4, “Notice of completion of voluntary arrangement”, filed on 13 April 2022. A company voluntary arrangement is a creditor deal, and the completion notice is the document that says the deal was seen through. Trading since then has been filed on time — full accounts to 31 January 2026 lodged on 7 August 2026 at 44 pages, the previous set to 25 January 2025 lodged 17 October 2025, a confirmation statement made on 8 July 2026, and a charge registered on 3 April 2023. No strike-off and no insolvency document appears after the 2022 completion notice.

On scale the two sites do not agree with the corporate one. The 2025 modern slavery statement, which is the company speaking about itself, gives “114 stores across the United Kingdom and Ireland” and “A head office based in London”. The hire site claims “over 120 Moss Hire stores across the country” and, two pages later, “one of our 120 stores”. Those cannot all be true, and the most defensible figure for a reader is the 114 in the statutory statement.

That statement is also where Moss is weakest on paper, and it is a one-fetch check anybody can repeat. It is headed “Modern Slavery Statement 2025” and says it covers “the financial year ending 2025”, which is the right year — but it carries no date and no signature, only “This statement has been approved by the CEO Brian Brick of Moss”, and section 54 of the Modern Slavery Act 2015 is a signing regime as much as a publishing one. It names the eight countries the clothes come from — China, Bangladesh, Vietnam, Sri Lanka, India, Italy, Portugal and the United Kingdom — and then gives no supplier count, no factory count, no audit count and no percentage anywhere. It measures its own programme against the Ethical Trading Initiative base code while stating plainly that “we are not a formal member of the Ethical Trading Initiative (ETI)”. Its commitment on site visits is “Annual visits to key supplier sites where possible”.

And if something goes wrong there is nowhere independent to take it. The retail terms name no ombudsman, no alternative dispute resolution provider and no trade body; clause 8.2 offers only that “Further information on your legal rights can be obtained from Trading Standards or Citizens Advice Bureaux”. The hire terms cap what Moss can owe you — “Moss liability in any event is limited to the Grand Total paid” — while preserving the liabilities the law will not let a trader exclude, and both sets of terms submit to “the laws of England and Wales”. There is no independent customer rating to fall back on either: Which? runs rated surveys of baby shops, technology, appliances, furniture and DIY, and publishes none for clothing shops, so no scored verdict on a menswear retailer exists from anybody.

One last asymmetry worth a line. Clause 3.1 of the retail terms asks only that “you warrant that you are at least 16 years old” to buy a suit online; the hire terms open with “You confirm you are 18 or over.” The business will sell a sixteen-year-old a dinner jacket and will not lend him one.

  • Returns to a shop are free and there are enough branches for that to be a real option — “There are no fees to return unwanted items to Moss stores nationwide”, against 114 stores in the UK and Ireland

  • Hire prices are printed and readable — £119.95 to £139.95 for a lounge suit, £209.95 or £234.95 for white tie — where several menswear sites publish no price at all

  • Free in-store measuring by a tailoring expert, free one-to-one styling advice, and free click and collect on both the shop and the hire site
  • £9.95 Peace of Mind cover is cheap set against the £20 repair fee it replaces, and the exclusions are stated rather than hidden
  • Delivery is free over £200 on the shop and £35 on click and collect, with next working day at £9.95 and BFPO addresses served at £4.95
  • Custom Made puts Moss’s own mistakes on Moss: “we will make alterations at our own cost”, under “a strict policy of repair, replace, and finally refund”

Where it falls short

  • Having a suit altered ends your right to return it, and the shop sells the alterations: “any alterations made will nullify our returns policy”, with no price for that work published anywhere
  • A hired outfit that does not fit has no remedy at all — hire garments “cannot be altered in any way”, Moss “is not liable to provide alternative sizes or product for any errors made in self-sizing”, and “We are not liable for any error discovered once you have left the store”
  • The cancellation charge is stated two different ways in the 60-to-31-day band: the terms keep the deposit, the fee list says “50% of the cancelled order value”, and the boundary itself is “30 days or less” on one page and “within 31 days” on the other

  • Late hire returns run at £10 a day per order, so a week late is £70 on a suit that cost £119.95, and on an Ascot hire the charge is a full day’s hire per day
  • The 2025 modern slavery statement carries no date and no signature, only approval by the CEO, gives no supplier, factory, audit or training figure, and states that Moss “are not a formal member of the Ethical Trading Initiative (ETI)”

Common questions

How much does it cost to return something to Moss?

£3.99 a parcel by post, taken out of the refund: “you will be charged a fee of £3.99 towards the cost of the courier” and “This fee will be deducted from any refund amount due.” Returning to any Moss store is free, and a swap for a different size carries no courier fee either — Moss “will not charge a courier fee in the case of an item being returned for an exchange to a different size.” Returns from the USA are free through the Moss portal, and returns from the rest of the world are at your own cost.

How long do I have, and when will the money arrive?

Fourteen days from receiving the order to notify Moss through the returns portal, then “a further 14 days to return the goods to us” — 28 days in total. A store purchase gets 28 days from the date of purchase and must go back to a store, since “purchases made in store can only be returned in store”. Then allow “up to 3 weeks after returning your items to receive any applicable refund”, which Moss splits into up to seven days to reach the processing centre, up to seven to inspect and up to seven to clear.

Can I return a suit after it has been taken up or taken in?

No. “Unfortunately we cannot refund or exchange altered or personalised items”, and the help centre puts it more bluntly still: “any alterations made will nullify our returns policy.” Alterations are bought separately, from “local professional tailors” the store partners with, at “an extra cost on top of the price of the product” that is not published. The order of operations matters: decide you are keeping the suit, then book the tailor.

What does hiring an outfit cost, including the extras?

Lounge suits run from £119.95 to £139.95 and the Tailored Fit White Tie Tails is £209.95 as a three-piece or £234.95 as a full package, each for a seven-day hire collected four days before the event and returned two days after. On top of that, Peace of Mind damage cover is £9.95 per outfit, a £450 security deposit is held against your card at collection with a £1.01 authorisation, courier delivery is £9.95 before 6.30pm (£12 by noon, £20 by 10.30am, £15 on a Saturday), and a courier return is £10 against free to any hire store.

What happens if the hired suit does not fit on the day?

Very little, which is why the collection appointment matters. “You’ll need to try on your order when you collect it so our store team are on hand to assist”, and problems must be raised then: the terms say “We are not liable for any error discovered once you have left the store”, and on a home delivery “You must notify us of any issues regarding your order on the day of delivery.” Altering the garment yourself is not an option — “Hire garments cannot be altered in any way”, and the fee list charges a hirer’s alteration at the item’s current retail selling price. If the sizes were self-submitted, “Moss is not liable to provide alternative sizes or product for any errors made in self-sizing or online order creation.”

Can I cancel a hire booking and get my money back?

It depends when, and where you booked. Online, and only if “you have not visited one of our stores at any point”, there is a 14-day cooling-off period with a full refund — which matches the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013, where a service contract’s 14 days run “after the day on which the contract is entered into” rather than from delivery. Book in a branch and no statutory cooling-off period arises at all. After that, Moss’s own table applies: at 61 days or more you get the deposit back “minus £25 per individual order”, between 60 and 31 days Moss keeps the deposit, and at 30 days or less “We will keep the full individual order value.” Changes cannot be made at all “21 days or less before the agreed collection or delivery date”.

Our verdict

On the shop side Moss is better than its own small print makes it sound: 28 days, £3.99 by post, nothing at all to hand a parcel back across a counter in one of 114 stores, and no fee whatever on the size swap that most men are really making. The one trap is the alteration, because the service that makes an off-the-peg jacket fit is the same service that cancels the right to send it back, and the shop does not have to warn you in the fitting room. On the hire side the money is clear enough — £119.95 to £139.95 for a seven-day lounge suit, £234.95 for white tie, £9.95 for damage cover that pays for itself the first time somebody spills a drink — but the risk sits almost entirely with the hirer: a £450 deposit, £10 a day for lateness, retail price for anything beyond repair, and a contract that says in three different places that a fit discovered to be wrong after you leave the shop is your problem. Book the fitting in a branch, sixty days out, and read the fee list before the product page.
