Motorway Sell Your Car

Rates and terms checked 20 November 2026 · Sell Your Car · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Motorway charges the person selling the car a service fee of between £29.99 and £119.99, and takes nothing unless the car actually sells. Its own fee page is blunt about it: “There’s no upfront cost when you sell with Motorway”, “you only pay the service fee once the sale is complete”, and “If your car doesn’t sell, you never pay a fee”. There is no listing fee and no fee at all on a car that sells for under £1,000. Those figures were read on 20 November 2026 from Motorway’s fee pages, its terms last updated in April 2026, its help centre, its trade site and Companies House.

Charging the seller rather than only the buyer is unusual in this corner of the market, and Motorway does the decent thing with it — the fee is shown as an estimate when you get the free valuation and again when you list, so the number is on screen before you commit. What you are buying is a daily auction in front of what the company calls “8,000+ verified dealers”, which it says is “over two thirds of the UK market”. Its own about page claims “up to 9,000+ cars listed in our auction every week, on average”, that “A car sells every two minutes”, and that more than a million people have now used it. The business runs on 400 staff across London and Brighton.

One thing to be clear on before you start, because it decides who you ring if the sale goes wrong: Motorway does not buy your car. Its terms state that “your vehicle will be purchased by a Car Buying Partner and not Motorway”, that “the contract for the sale of your vehicle will be between you and that Car Buying Partner”, and that “At no point does Motorway purchase any vehicles itself, nor does it acquire any risk or title to any vehicle”. That also shapes how the fee reaches Motorway. On the Motorway Pay route it is deducted before the balance lands in your account; if the buying dealer pays you by bank transfer instead, Motorway invoices you for the fee within 14 days of the sale and payment is due within 14 days of that invoice.

What the service fee comes to on a real car

Motorway publishes two different descriptions of the same charge and they only agree in the middle of the range. Three of its pages give the band — “The service fee ranges from £29.99 to £119.99 depending on the final price of your car” — while the page arguing the case for selling through it adds a percentage: “you pay a small service fee, typically 0.3-0.5% of your car’s value”.

Those two statements are consistent over a surprisingly narrow stretch. A £29.99 floor is 0.5% of £5,998 and 0.3% of £9,997; a £119.99 ceiling is 0.5% of £23,998 and 0.3% of £39,997. So for a car worth roughly £6,000 to £40,000 the percentage is a fair description. Below about £6,000 the flat minimum bites and the real percentage climbs; above about £40,000 the ceiling holds and it falls away.

Motorway’s own evidence shows how often that happens. Its sell-my-car page scrolls a list of 30 recent sales with the price each one made, from a £1,367 Ford Fiesta to a £64,751 Range Rover. Twelve of those 30 sold for less than £5,998, so on each of them the £29.99 minimum is already more than 0.5% of the price — 2.19% on the Fiesta, 1.50% on a £2,001 SEAT Leon, 0.51% on a £5,888 Range Rover Evoque. One sold above £39,997, where the £119.99 cap works out at 0.19%. Thirteen of the provider’s own 30 worked examples sit outside the band its marketing quotes.

What nobody can do from the published material is work out what any individual seller paid. Clause 2 of the terms says the fee is “based on the final purchase price of your car in accordance with the Fee Schedule”, and that “The Fee Schedule forms part of these Terms”. The schedule it points at is the service-fees page, and that page gives the two ends of the range and no bands between them. A £7,001 Vauxhall Corsa from Motorway’s own list could be anywhere from £29.99 (0.43%) to £119.99 (1.71%) and the documents will not narrow it.

Twenty-three hours, one offer, and a guide price the dealers can read

The mechanism is tighter than the marketing suggests, and the help centre sets it out precisely. “The auction begins at 16:30, and ends at 15:30 the following day” — a single window of about 23 hours. “During the auction, dealers will see your guide price, giving them an idea of the amount you’re happy to accept for your car.” When bidding closes, “we’ll share your highest offer on the same day”, and you can “instantly accept this offer to proceed”.

You get one number, not a spread of competing bids to pick from, and the dealers have seen the floor you put under it before they bid. Motorway’s answer to the obvious worry is a figure rather than a reassurance: “over 50% of cars that go for sale at the guide price on Motorway achieve more than expected”. Read plainly, that also means nearly half do not.

On the trade side the bidding is a proxy auction. Motorway tells dealers that “our proxy system does the legwork, so you only ever pay £1 more than the next highest bid once the car hits reserve”, which is the arrangement that stops a dealer paying its maximum simply for having typed it in. Dealers must act fast once a sale is confirmed — “Within 1 (one) Working Day of receipt of that confirmation of sale” — and the trade terms require that “you shall make payment of the Purchase Price during the collection of the relevant Vehicle from the Seller”, with clause 3.17 stating flatly: “At no point shall any of the Purchase Price for a Vehicle be made in cash.”

Two of Motorway’s own scale figures can be set against each other. If “A car sells every two minutes” is read as a round-the-clock average, that is 720 a day and 5,040 a week, against “up to 9,000+ cars listed in our auction every week” — a sell-through of about 56%. Motorway does not say whether the two-minute figure covers the whole day or only the trading window, so treat the ratio as an indication rather than a published rate; it does, though, sit comfortably alongside the company’s own help article on “What if my vehicle does not sell in an online sale?”.

What a dealer may and may not do once it is standing on your drive

This is the part of a car sale that goes wrong, and Motorway’s terms are better on it than the sales pages let on. The buyer inspects on collection to “make sure that it matches the description and condition you gave to us”, and if it does, “then the purchase price should be the same as the winning bid”. If it does not, the dealer “may choose to either (a) offer a revised purchase price, or (b) not proceed with the purchase of your vehicle” — and only on three stated grounds: damage or a difference in condition “which wasn’t in your vehicle profile”; “a difference in the amount of outstanding finance on your vehicle to the figure you disclosed to us”; or reason to believe “you have engaged in any fraudulent, criminal, or misleading behaviour”.

Then comes the sentence worth knowing before anyone turns up: “You are under no obligation to accept a revised purchase price from the Car Buying Partner.” The trade terms say the same thing from the other side — clause 3.9 fixes the price at the winning bid, and clause 3.10 opens with “You shall only be entitled to cancel a Bid or seek an adjustment to the Purchase Price in the following circumstances”. If the sale collapses at that point, “the Service Fee will not be payable”, and you can re-list without paying a second fee, though a fee is still due if the car sells next time round.

The flip side is that the accuracy of your own listing is what the whole thing rests on. The terms ask you to “take every effort to make sure that the information you provide is accurate and up-to-date”, and warn that “Providing inaccurate information could lead to changes in the eventual price you are paid for your vehicle” or “a Car Buying Partner refusing to purchase your vehicle”. You supply the photographs — the help centre says “You also need to provide photos that our network of dealers will see that will inform their highest offer” — and no photographic standard, damage threshold or minimum number of images is published anywhere on the site. Clause 13 also indemnifies Motorway against “any unpaid vehicle finance for which you are responsible”, and clause 15 contemplates a vehicle turning out “to be a ‘clocked’ vehicle, to be a cloned vehicle, to be a stolen vehicle”.

The cars Motorway’s dealers will not touch, and the paperwork that stops a sale

None of this is in the terms, which say only that some cars are “ineligible for our online daily sale (e.g., because of their make, model, age, or mileage)” and that a car must meet “our minimum eligibility criteria” without ever defining them. The criteria live in the help centre instead, article by article, and they are specific.

Write-offs are out: “Dealers working with Motorway do not buy category A, B, C, D or S or N cars”, which covers every current and legacy insurance category. So are imports, scrap and non-runners — “No, Motorway doesn’t sell scrap or non-runner vehicles.” The platform handles “cars, vans, campervans and some trucks”, and “Currently, none of our dealers are able to purchase motorbikes or scooters”.

Two paperwork rules catch people out. The MOT is one: “Your vehicle needs to have an MOT certificate with at least two weeks’ validity left before it enters the online daily sale.” The other is how long the car has been yours. If you bought from a dealership there is no waiting period, but under three months you must produce proof of purchase; if you bought privately or at auction, “it needs to have been registered in your name for a minimum of 3 months before you can sell it with us”. A leased car can only go through if you have already bought it from the leasing company “and the V5C is now in your name”.

No age limit and no mileage ceiling appear anywhere in the twelve help articles that deal with eligibility, which is a genuine point in Motorway’s favour against sellers of older cars — but the terms reserve the right to refuse on exactly those grounds, so the absence of a published figure is not the same as the absence of a rule.

Nobody is regulating the sale itself, and the small print says so

Selling your own car to a trader is not a regulated activity, there is no ombudsman scheme sitting behind it, and the Consumer Rights Act protects buyers rather than sellers. What a private seller has is contract law and whatever the platform has written down, so the written-down part matters more here than on almost any other kind of page.

Motorway’s trade site footer states that “Motorway Online Limited is an appointed representative of ITC Compliance Limited” under FCA reference 313486, which is a permission held for insurance and credit arrangements rather than anything to do with buying and selling a car. The scope and start date of that appointment could not be confirmed: the Financial Services Register at register.fca.org.uk returned only a “Loading / Sorry to interrupt / CSS Error” shell on both the search page and its root on 20 November 2026, so the entry itself was unreadable and nothing about it has been assumed here.

The liability clause is the one to read twice. Clause 12 says Motorway “do not accept any liability to you for any failure of the provision of services by a Car Buying Partner”, and caps what it will pay at “the lower of £100 or the Service Fee paid by you in respect of the sale of your vehicle”. On a car where the fee was £29.99, Motorway’s maximum exposure is £29.99. Clause 6 adds that “we accept no responsibility for any deal, car purchase, payment or disputes that may arise”. Complaints go to Motorway direct, which undertakes to “acknowledge your complaint within 5 business days and provide a substantive response within 28 days”, and English law governs.

There is a statutory cancellation right and it expires early. “You have a legal right to cancel the auction service within 14 days of entering your car into the auction”, but “you will lose your right to cancel once we have fully performed the auction service”, after which “you will not be entitled to a refund of the Service Fee (unless there is a material defect in the service)”. If you owe the fee on an invoice and do not pay, Motorway may charge interest “at a rate of 2% per annum above the Bank of England base rate” — with Bank Rate at 4.00% since 5 November 2026, that is 6.00% — suspend your access and “pursue recovery of the outstanding amount as a debt”. Dealers get harsher treatment: their late-payment rate is 5% over Barclays Bank plc’s base rate, and Motorway may cut them off once a Purchase Fee is 30 days overdue.

Where the £1,600 comes from

Motorway puts a single number at the top of almost every page — “Motorway sellers get £1,600 more than part exchange on average” — and, to its credit, publishes the working at motorway.co.uk/claims rather than leaving it as a footnote. The exact figure is “£1,607.19”, and it comes from a customer survey in April 2026 of sellers who had sold within the previous 96 days.

The funnel is published too: “in total 30,367 sellers were sent the survey, 2,220 of which completed the survey”, and “Of those who completed the survey, 589 also received a viable part exchange valuation”. So the headline average rests on 589 responses, which is 1.9% of the people asked, and the comparison figures were self-reported — “Part-exchange offers were self-reported by sellers”, with anyone who had not been given a part-exchange quote excluded. No median, no spread and no breakdown by car value is published, and the claims page is explicit that the £1,600 is measured “before fees”.

The other claims are dated the same way. “84% of Motorway sellers sold their vehicle for more than the average market price valuation” is measured “between Jan 2020 and November 2025” against “two leading, independent market price guides” that are never named, with cars excluded where guide data was missing or where the two guides disagreed too sharply. A 97% satisfaction claim comes from an August 2024 survey in which “14,616 sellers were sent the survey and 454 sellers responded” — a 3.1% response rate — and of those answering the question “96.83% answered yes”, rounded up on the page to 97%.

One claim resists checking altogether. “8,000+ verified dealers - that’s over two thirds of the UK market” implies a market of fewer than 12,000 dealers, and Motorway never says what it is counting. The nearest official figure is the ONS business count published on 24 September 2026, which recorded about 85,000 VAT or PAYE-registered businesses in the broad “Motor trades” group at 13 March 2026 — a wider category than used-car retailers, but wide enough to show that the denominator behind “two thirds” is a definition of Motorway’s own choosing.

The company behind the auction, and the market your car is bid into

Companies House settles a small puzzle on Motorway’s own site. The about page dates the business to 2017, when “Tom Leathes, Harry Jones and Alex Buttle founded Motorway”, and the consumer footer reads “© Motorway Online Ltd. 2017-2026”; the trade site footer says 2016. Both are right. Motorway Online Ltd, company 10285711, was incorporated on 19 July 2016 under the name For Tomorrow Ltd and took the Motorway name on 28 February 2017. Its registered office is 12-13 Wells Mews, London W1T 3HE, and its two SIC codes are 62090 and 70229 — information technology services and management consultancy. There is no motor-trade code on the register at all, which is exactly what you would expect of a company that never owns a car.

It is a venture-backed business and the register shows it. The about page records a “$1bn+ valuation” in 2021, and the press page carries the round behind it: “Motorway raises $190m Series C funding round from Index Ventures and ICONIQ Growth”, dated November 2021. Index Ventures, ICONIQ Capital, Marchmont Ventures, Local Globe and BMW i Ventures appear as investors, Danny Rimer has sat on the board since 10 June 2021, and share allotments have been filed at Companies House most months through 2025 and 2026. A charge, 102857110001, was created on 23 December 2025. Of the three founders, Tom Leathes and Harry Jones remain directors from the 2016 incorporation; Alex Buttle resigned on 31 August 2024 and no longer appears on the executive team.

The register also records a stumble worth knowing about. A First Gazette notice for compulsory strike-off was issued on 15 April 2025 and discontinued the next day, 16 April 2025. It followed an administrative tangle rather than a trading problem: the registered office had been moved to the Companies House default PO Box in Cardiff on 25 February 2025 and was corrected to Wells Mews on 3 April 2025. Nothing came of it, and the company has filed normally since, with a confirmation statement made on 18 July 2026.

As for the market, the second-hand car index is moving in a direction that argues for selling sooner rather than later. The ONS series for second-hand cars, released on 18 November 2026, shows the annual rate at -4.1% in October 2026, after -3.2% in September, -2.5% in August, -1.9% in July, -1.2% in June and -0.6% in May. Six consecutive months of deepening falls means a car held back for a quarter is likely to be auctioned into a weaker book than today’s.

Where it wins

  • Nothing is payable unless the car sells: no listing fee, no upfront cost, and no service fee at all on a car that sells for under £1,000
  • The fee estimate is shown at the free valuation and again at listing, so the number is on screen before you commit
  • The terms state plainly that “You are under no obligation to accept a revised purchase price from the Car Buying Partner”, and the trade terms restrict a dealer to three stated grounds for adjusting or cancelling
  • A dealer may only adjust the price for undisclosed damage, understated outstanding finance, or suspected fraud — not at its own discretion
  • The methodology behind the £1,600 claim is published in full, with the survey size, the response count and the self-reporting caveat, rather than left as a footnote
  • If the sale falls through the fee is not payable, and a car can be re-listed without a second fee
  • A clear fee ladder beneath the auction: no upfront cost, deduction at source on Motorway Pay, and 14 days to pay an invoice otherwise
  • No published age or mileage ceiling, so older and higher-mileage cars are not excluded on paper
  • Collection is from your driveway with payment made by the dealer at handover, and 83% of payments are stated to land on the day
  • Eligibility is documented article by article in the help centre — write-off categories, MOT validity, ownership period and vehicle types are all written down somewhere a seller can read them

Where it falls short

  • The Fee Schedule is incorporated into the contract by clause 2 of the terms, but the page it points at publishes only the two ends of the range — there is no band table, so no seller can calculate the fee in advance or check the one they were charged
  • The “typically 0.3-0.5%” claim holds only between about £6,000 and £40,000. Thirteen of the 30 sales Motorway displays on its own sell-my-car page fall outside it, and on the £1,367 Ford Fiesta in that list the £29.99 minimum is at least 2.19% of the price
  • Motorway’s liability to the seller is capped at “the lower of £100 or the Service Fee” — on a £29.99 fee, £29.99 — and it disclaims responsibility for the dealer’s failure to pay or perform, with no ombudsman scheme behind the sale
  • What the buying dealer pays is not published anywhere readable. The trade terms define a Purchase Fee by reference to “the pricing table found at https://pro.motorway.co.uk/pricing”, and that address serves the trade homepage instead of a fee table
  • The “over two thirds of the UK market” claim cannot be tested, because Motorway never states the denominator; 8,000 dealers would imply a market under 12,000 against roughly 85,000 motor-trade businesses in the ONS count at March 2026
  • The £1,600 headline rests on 589 usable responses out of 30,367 sellers contacted — 1.9% — with part-exchange offers self-reported, no median published, and the figure measured before the service fee is deducted
  • The 84% claim is averaged over nearly six years, from January 2020 to November 2025, against two price guides that are never named, with cars excluded where the guides disagreed
  • No results, revenue or sales figure has been published since “Motorway doubles sales to £1.7bn in 2022”, and the press page carries nothing dated later than September 2024
  • No photographic standard, damage threshold or minimum image count is published, even though the terms make the accuracy of the seller’s own profile the trigger for a price reduction or a refused purchase
  • The statutory 14-day cancellation right lapses “once we have fully performed the auction service”, after which the fee is not refundable except for a material defect in the service
  • You get one offer at the end of a 23-hour window, and the bidding dealers can see the guide price you set before they bid

Common questions

How much does Motorway charge to sell a car?

Between £29.99 and £119.99, set by the final sale price and taken only once the sale completes. There is no listing fee and no upfront cost, and Motorway’s help centre states there is “No fee on cars sold under £1,000”. One of its pages describes the charge as “typically 0.3-0.5% of your car’s value”, which matches the £29.99–£119.99 band for a car worth roughly £6,000 to £40,000 and overstates how little you pay below that.

When do I actually pay the fee?

It depends how the dealer pays you. On the Motorway Pay route the dealer pays Motorway, which says it will “deduct the Service Fee and transfer the remaining balance” to you. If the dealer transfers the money to your account directly, Motorway will “issue you with an invoice for the Service Fee via email within 14 days of the date of your sale”, and payment is “due within 14 days of the date of our invoice”. Late payment attracts interest at 2% over the Bank of England base rate, which is 6.00% with Bank Rate at 4.00%.

Can the dealer knock the price down when it collects the car?

Only on three grounds set out in the terms: damage or a difference in condition that was not in your vehicle profile, a difference between the outstanding finance you declared and the real figure, or reason to believe you have acted fraudulently or misleadingly. Otherwise “the purchase price should be the same as the winning bid”. And if a revised price is offered, the terms say “You are under no obligation to accept a revised purchase price from the Car Buying Partner.”

How long does the whole thing take?

Motorway’s help centre says getting from a valuation to approving an offer “generally takes around two to seven days, though it can be completed in as little as 24 hours”, and that “Collection generally happens within a week to ten days of the sale being agreed”. The sale itself is a single daily auction that “begins at 16:30, and ends at 15:30 the following day”, with the highest offer shared on the day it closes.

Which cars will Motorway not sell?

Insurance write-offs in any category — “Dealers working with Motorway do not buy category A, B, C, D or S or N cars” — plus imported vehicles, scrap and non-runners, and motorbikes and scooters. The platform covers “cars, vans, campervans and some trucks”. Your car also needs an MOT with “at least two weeks’ validity left”, and if you bought it privately or at auction it must have been registered in your name for at least three months.

If the dealer never pays me, what can Motorway do about it?

Very little, by its own terms. Motorway is not a party to the sale, takes no title to the car and states that it “do not accept any liability to you for any failure of the provision of services by a Car Buying Partner”. Where it does accept liability, clause 12 caps it at “the lower of £100 or the Service Fee paid by you in respect of the sale of your vehicle”. Complaints go to Motorway, which aims to acknowledge within 5 business days and respond substantively within 28 days. Selling your own car is not a regulated activity, so there is no ombudsman behind it.

What happens if my car does not sell?

You pay nothing — “If your car doesn’t sell, you never pay a fee” — and you can put it back into a later sale without a second fee, although a fee becomes payable if it sells that time. Motorway also says that where a car cannot be sold through the dealer network at all, it will tell you “immediately after you enter your reg and mileage” and then recommend an instant online buyer instead.

Our verdict

Motorway is the one large car-selling marketplace that asks the seller for money, and the fairest thing to say about it is that it charges only when it delivers: £29.99 to £119.99 on a completed sale, nothing if the car does not go, nothing under £1,000, and an estimate on screen before you list. Against a part-exchange offer it is very likely to be worth it, and the terms are better than the category average in the place that matters most — a dealer standing on your drive cannot simply chip the price, and you are explicitly not obliged to accept a revised one. The weakness is arithmetic rather than conduct. A fee schedule that forms part of the contract ought to publish its bands, and because it does not, the percentage Motorway quotes in its marketing is right only over the middle of the range and visibly wrong on nearly half the sales the company itself puts on display. If your car is worth £6,000 to £40,000, the fee is a rounding error on what the auction is likely to add. If it is worth £1,500, you are paying about 2% for the privilege, and that is the number to weigh before you list.
