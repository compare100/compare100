Mr Clutch Autocentres: Servicing, Clutches and MOTs Reviewed

Rates and terms checked 20 November 2026 · Servicing · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Mr Clutch Autocentres prices a full service at £261.95 for an engine up to 1300cc and an MOT at £41.99, and it fits a replacement clutch under a two-year or 20,000-mile warranty — twice the twelve months most fast-fit chains offer. Figures checked on 20 November 2026 against the company’s own price pages and warranty document.

The business has been fitting clutches since 1978, when the brothers Joe and Will Yussuf started it in a railway arch, and it says it has passed one million clutch replacements and repairs since. All three brothers — Joe, Will and Sef — are still directors, appointed on 1 June 1999 and listed as such on the register today. There are over 40 branches, open 8:30am to 5:30pm Monday to Friday and 8:30am to 5:00pm on Saturdays at selected sites.

What sets it apart from a general garage chain is that it publishes real fixed prices rather than estimates: five service levels across five engine bands, a brake matrix, a cambelt matrix and a list of exchange gearboxes by vehicle and gearbox type, all with parts, labour and VAT included. A clutch check is free, needs no appointment and is done while you wait.

One job, done a million times, and two prices for it

Almost every chain on a high street sells servicing, tyres, brakes and an MOT. Mr Clutch sells all of those and one thing more: the clutch, which is where the name and the trade came from. Its own figure is over one million clutch replacements and repairs since 1978, and the pitch is savings of “up to 60% off main dealer prices”.

The free clutch check is the genuinely useful part. The company says it will “complete a free clutch check while-you-wait”, that “there is no need to book” and that the check “will only take a few minutes”. If you then get a cheaper like-for-like quotation elsewhere, it undertakes to match it “even up to 7 days after your invoice date” — a week after the work is paid for, which is longer than most price promises run.

The price is where it gets harder to pin down. The clutch page carries a figure in its text — “In June 2026, the cost of a new clutch replacement started from £385” — and a figure in its own sidebar, “Clutches From £150.00”. Both appear on the same page and nothing on it reconciles them. The £385 is dated and the £150 is not, so the dated one is the safer number to plan around, but a reader cannot tell from the page which job either figure buys.

Nor does the page say what a clutch price includes. It recommends inspecting the dual mass flywheel, warning that a worn one “will affect the performance and efficiency of the new clutch and almost certainly result in premature failure”, and it recommends “fitting a new concentric slave cylinder whenever performing a clutch replacement” on vehicles that use them. Neither carries a published price. A flywheel and a slave cylinder are the two items most likely to turn a quoted clutch job into a larger bill, so the free check is where you find out, not the website.

Beyond the clutch itself the published starting prices run: dual clutch and DSG units from £950, gearboxes from £745, wet belts from £1,450, cambelts from £344.95, turbos from £280, fixed-price brakes from £149.95, an air conditioning recharge at £69.99, front wheel alignment at £59.99, an engine diagnostic scan at £45.95, exhausts and batteries from £74.95, tyres from £47.34 and a winter safety check at £19.99. Interest-free finance is offered at most branches.

The two-year warranty has four appointments attached to it

The warranty is the strongest commercial argument the company has, and it is worth reading in full before relying on it. The site promises “a minimum of a 1 year or 12,000 mile warranty for all repairs that we undertake on non-commercial vehicles” and extends that “to a 2 year or 20,000 mile warranty for clutch replacements”. The published warranty document puts it as two years or 20,000 miles, whichever is soonest from the original invoice date or mileage, on a complete clutch kit fitted to a non-commercial vehicle. Taxis, driving schools and light commercials get one year or 12,000 miles.

Two years is double the industry habit and it is a real selling point. The conditions behind it are the part nobody mentions. The warranty requires clutch checks at 500, 3,000, 6,000 and 12,000 mile intervals, each one “recorded on the invoice”, and it is void if those checks are not done or if the invoice is not produced when a claim is made. The checks themselves are free, so the cost is four trips back to the branch rather than money — but a driver who skips the 500-mile call-in during the first fortnight has given up the second year of cover without knowing it, and a second-hand buyer inheriting the car inherits a warranty they almost certainly cannot evidence.

What the warranty covers is narrower than the badge suggests, too. It covers “manufacturing defects”, and it excludes misuse, missed clutch checks, the wrong oil grade, failures caused by other faulty parts and undue wear and tear. The site badges the work as a “parts & labour guarantee”; the warranty document itself does not mention labour anywhere. On a clutch, labour is most of the bill, so that silence matters more here than it would on a battery.

The gearbox terms are shorter and clearly stated: a reconditioned manual gearbox carries one year or 12,000 miles on a non-commercial vehicle and six months or 6,000 miles on a commercial one, all units are exchange units, and a gearbox that has been repaired rather than replaced carries a “restricted warranty applying only to those parts which have been replaced”. Everything else the company fits “carr[ies] only the manufacturer’s warranty”. Mr Clutch says it uses only “NEW top quality parts of an original manufacturer specification”.

The servicing is sold as five levels — oil and filter change, interim, full, major, and major with an MOT — priced across five engine bands, with fully synthetic oil included at every level. The company says plainly: “We won’t charge you more for fully synthetic oil.” The grid, read on 20 November 2026, runs like this.

Subtract one column from the next and the MOT inside the bundle prices itself: £316.95 − £296.95 = £20.00, and the same £20.00 gap holds in all five bands. Bought on its own the test is £41.99, so taking it with a major service saves £21.99. Both figures sit under the statutory ceiling: DVSA caps a class 4 car test at £54.85, with no VAT charged on the fee, so £41.99 is £12.86 below the maximum and the in-bundle £20.00 is well under half of it.

There is a 5% discount for booking online, and it explains an advertised figure that otherwise looks wrong. The sidebar on every page says “Servicing From £158.60”, which is less than the cheapest cell in the grid. It is not an error: £166.95 less 5% is £158.6025, which rounds to the £158.60 advertised. The “from” price is derived honestly from the cheapest real price rather than padded, which is worth saying in a trade where “from” prices often match nothing on the list. A free annual Privilege Card comes with a major service and gives 5% off “the majority of work”.

The service checklist stretches to around 70 lines and includes the row “Stamp service book”. Nothing on the servicing page mentions a digital or online service record, which is how most cars sold in the last decade keep their history — and nothing on it says whether hybrids or electric cars can be booked at all. Spark plugs carry an extra charge for platinum, and the pollen filter is listed at “additional cost”, so the fixed price is fixed around a defined job rather than around your car.

“No Surprises” is printed on a price list two years out of date

Mr Clutch publishes a downloadable brochure headed Fixed Prices Guide — No Surprises, and it is the most complete document of its kind any garage chain on this site publishes: the full servicing grid, a four-column brake matrix, a three-column cambelt matrix and twenty-one light commercial and nineteen car exchange gearboxes priced by model, engine and gearbox. The only date on it is an issue reference, D0224, on the last page.

Its prices are not the website’s prices. The guide puts a full service on an engine up to 1300cc at £201.95 against the live grid’s £261.95 — a gap of £60.00 on the same job. Oil and filter is £106.95 in the guide and £166.95 on the site; interim is £146.95 against £206.95; major with an MOT is £251.95 against £316.95. The MOT inside the bundle was £15.00 in the guide (£251.95 less £236.95) and is £20.00 now.

None of that is concealment — the live grid is on the servicing page and is easy to find — and prices rising over two years is what prices do. The problem is that a customer who downloads a brochure titled No Surprises and takes it to a branch is holding the wrong numbers, and the brochure carries no date on its face to warn them. The same gap runs through the matrices nobody has reproduced on the live site: front pads and discs at £249.95 up to 1300cc rising to £299.95 above 2200cc, cambelts from £319.95 for a single cam up to 1300cc to £499.95 for a four cam above 2200cc, and exchange gearboxes from £795 on a Fiat 500 1.2 five-speed to £2,450 on a Citroën Relay or Peugeot Boxer six-speed. Those are the only published prices for that work, and they are two years old.

Both the brake and cambelt matrices carry exclusions worth reading before relying on them. The brake prices exclude “electric, sports, high performance, modified, utility, 4x4, commercial vehicles” among others; the cambelt prices exclude the water pump, which “can be fitted at an additional cost”, and replacing a pump with the belt is standard practice on many engines. Both matrices, and the MOT price, apply “at participating branches only”, and the company publishes no list of which branches participate. The Gravesend branch, for one, advertises its MOT at £45.99 rather than £41.99.

The company that takes the money, and nine years of a register nobody corrected

The brand says 1978 and means it — but the company you contract with is younger. Mr Clutch Autocentres Ltd. is company 03678433, incorporated on 3 December 1998 and registered at 2 Priory Road, Rochester, Kent ME2 2EG under SIC code 45200, maintenance and repair of motor vehicles. It has worn three other names: it was incorporated as Global 4 Safety Limited, became Mister Clutch Limited on 15 June 1999, Mr Clutch Limited on 24 September 1999 and took its present name on 26 October 2000. A business trading since 1978 inside a company formed in 1998 is ordinary — companies get restructured — but both halves are worth knowing, because the twenty years before 1998 are not on any file a customer can read.

What is on file is reassuring in the ways that matter. The company files full accounts, not the small-company or micro-entity versions a chain this size could get away with: the last were made up to 31 March 2025, ran to 33 pages and were filed on 19 December 2025, with the next due by 31 December 2026. The confirmation statement is current, dated 3 December 2025, with the next due by 17 December 2026. There is no strike-off, administration or liquidation filing anywhere on the record. One charge is registered and outstanding: a debenture created on 1 February 2011 and delivered on 4 February 2011 in favour of HSBC Bank plc. Nine officers have served and six have resigned; the three serving directors are Joseph, Sefer and William Yussuf, all appointed on 1 June 1999.

“Family-owned since 1978” is how the site puts it, and the register adds a layer to that. The person with significant control is not a Yussuf but C I (Holdings) Limited, company 03628474, holding 75% or more of both shares and voting rights from the same Rochester address. The three brothers’ own entries as controlling persons ceased on the day the holding company’s began, 22 December 2016. The striking part is the filing dates: the form notifying C I (Holdings) Limited and the three forms recording the brothers’ cessations were all filed on 16 February 2026 — more than nine years after the event they record — and a replacement confirmation statement for 3 December 2016 was filed on 5 March 2026. For nine years the public register named the wrong controlling persons. It has been put right, and the correction is itself on the file, but anyone who looked up who owned Mr Clutch between 2016 and February 2026 was given an answer that was out of date.

A second company shares the address: Mr Clutch Franchising Ltd, company 03791013, incorporated on 17 June 1999 and briefly named Eurocom Holiday UK Limited until 14 July 1999. Its registered activity is SIC 68209, other letting and operating of own or leased real estate — property rather than repair. Nothing on the consumer site explains which branches, if any, are franchised rather than company-owned, and that is the one thing a customer with a warranty claim would want settled: the company named on the warranty document is Mr Clutch Autocentres Ltd. at Head Office, 2 Priory Road, Strood, Kent ME2 2EG.

Two things a reader may look for are missing from the terms and conditions themselves: the company number and the VAT number appear nowhere on them. The company is registered with the Information Commissioner as a data controller, reference ZA617313, registered on 10 January 2020 and due to expire on 9 January 2027.

Trading Standards approval is not something only one chain has

Across its manufacturer pages Mr Clutch makes a superlative claim: “Mr Clutch is the only Trading Standards Approved Independant Garage Network in the UK today”, spelling as published. The approval behind it is real — the site carries a CTSI approved-code badge, and its disputes go to the National Conciliation Service, which describes itself as “an alternative dispute resolution (ADR) scheme which has been accredited and regulated by the Chartered Trading Standards Institute (CTSI)”. The word doing the work is “only”.

CTSI publishes its own audits, and they do not support it. Trust My Garage, sponsored by the Independent Garages Association and administered by Retail Motor Industry Standards & Certification, holds a CTSI-approved consumer code for independent garages and was audited on site on 23 April 2026 by two Trading Standards Practitioners from CTSI. That audit records 3,077 members at the end of December 2025, up 77 in twelve months, with 480 audits completed in the twelve months to 29 December 2025 — 225 on-site audits of new members and 255 re-audits, 114 of them remote. 469 scored between 85% and 100% and 11 failed; 197 members left the scheme over the period; and the National Conciliation Service handled 53 complaints for Trust My Garage during 2025.

There is a second one. The Motor Ombudsman operates four CTSI-approved codes of practice, and its Service and Repair Code was approved in 2008 and last audited in July 2026. Its own accreditation guidance says the code is open to “Independent garages (including fast-fit specialists and MOT stations)” as well as franchised dealerships, and it claims “the largest number of accredited businesses of any automotive ADR provider” without publishing the count; the sector row on CTSI’s page gives 4,839 members across the codes. Neither body calls itself the only, the first or the largest CTSI-approved garage network; Mr Clutch does.

The dispute route itself is set out reasonably. The terms say Mr Clutch “actively subscribe[s] to the Retail Motor Industry (RMI) Federation” and name the National Conciliation Service at PO Box 6562, Rugby CV21 9QP, with an email address and the phone number 01788 538317, to be used once the internal complaints process is exhausted. The scheme’s own rules give a consumer 12 months from the trader’s final response to refer a dispute, aim to finish within 90 calendar days of a complete file, and allow claims under £50 to be declined. One detail is missing from the terms, and the law names it specifically: regulation 19(1) of the Alternative Dispute Resolution for Consumer Disputes (Competent Authorities and Information) Regulations 2015 requires “the name and website address” of the ADR entity on the trader’s website and in its general terms. Mr Clutch gives the name, the post, the email and the phone, and no web address, though the scheme publishes one. No internal time limit for a first response is stated either.

On the way there, the cancellation terms are worth knowing. A consumer may cancel within 14 days without giving a reason and be refunded “without any deductions”, though work started inside that window at your request is chargeable. A business customer gets 7 working days, lost the moment the service starts. Payment falls due “immediately at the point of purchase”. The clause most likely to catch someone out is the no-show: on a prepaid online order the company “shall retain fifty percent (50%) of the relevant advance payment”, so missing a booked slot costs half the money. A reasonable daily storage charge applies to a car not collected on time. There is no general limitation-of-liability clause, which is unusually customer-friendly — but the governing-law clauses contradict each other, clause 3.1 choosing English law and the “non-exclusive jurisdiction” of the English courts while clause 14.3 chooses the laws of England and Wales and the “exclusive jurisdiction” of the English courts.

Every MOT at every branch is one named person’s legal problem

A garage cannot test cars because it owns ramps. It has to be an authorised examiner, and DVSA’s MOT testing guide — updated on 16 November 2026 — defines one as “the organisation that operates and manages one or more vehicle testing stations (VTSs) and is responsible for controlling the quality of testing carried out”. The obligation is not delegable. Authorised examiners “are in law fully responsible for ensuring that statutory MOT testing at their VTSs … is carried out to the required standard” and “are held fully responsible for the actions of their staff and others acting on their behalf or with their agreement”, with all such staff “adequately supervised and fully conversant with their testing duties”. Each authorisation needs a designated manager who has attended a DVSA-approved MOT Manager’s Course, and each site a site manager, who may be the same person.

The rest of the regime is unusually concrete for something consumers never see. Examiners “must, at all times, properly maintain the premises, facilities and testing equipment at all VTSs”, and “testing must stop on any class or type of vehicle as soon as any mandatory item of equipment malfunctions”. DVSA works to a risk-based model and an examiner “can view their site’s risk rating on the MOT testing service”, with the stated aim of “a low risk of non-compliance”. The agency “has the discretionary power to take disciplinary action against an AE, including cessation of the authorisation”. Business changes must be notified within 28 calendar days, there is a 14-day window to appeal an intention to refuse, an authorisation ceases automatically after 12 consecutive months with no tests, and a designated manager must contact DVSA after 3 consecutive months without one. For the driver, the two numbers to know are the free retest, available up to the 10th working day, and the one hour allowed to rectify a minor fault at the station before it becomes a failure.

Which brings the page back to the clutch. The MOT inspection manual, updated 14 October 2026, is built from eleven numbered sections — identification of the vehicle, brakes, steering, visibility, lamps and electrical equipment, axles and wheels and tyres and suspension, body and structure, other equipment, nuisance, supplementary tests for buses and coaches, and seat belt installation checks — and not one of them is the transmission. A clutch is never tested, never fails anything and never appears on a certificate. No national dataset exists on the condition of the country’s clutches in the way DVSA publishes one for tyres and brakes, which is a reasonable argument for a free check that takes a few minutes and costs nothing.

The fleet it is checking is getting older. SMMT’s vehicles-in-use figures, published 24 April 2026 for 2025, put 42,549,649 vehicles on UK roads, of which 36,676,185 are cars and 5,175,598 are vans, both records. The average car is now 9.7 years old, up from 9.5 in 2024, and a record 45.7% of cars have been in service more than a decade, against 43.4% a year earlier. A clutch is a wear item on a mileage clock, not an age one, but a fleet where nearly half the cars are over ten years old is the market a clutch specialist sells into.

Common questions

How much is a full service at Mr Clutch?

£261.95 for an engine up to 1300cc, £271.95 to 1600cc, £281.95 to 1900cc, £301.95 to 2200cc and £333.95 above 2200cc, checked on 20 November 2026. Fully synthetic oil is included at every level and there is a 5% discount for booking online. The downloadable Fixed Prices Guide shows lower figures — £201.95 for the same up-to-1300cc service — but it carries only the issue reference D0224 and the live grid is the current price list.

What does an MOT cost, and is it cheaper with a service?

£41.99 on its own “at participating branches”, though the Gravesend branch advertises £45.99. Taking it inside a major service costs £20.00: the Major Service and MOT column is exactly £20.00 above the Major Service column in all five engine bands. DVSA caps a class 4 car test at £54.85 with no VAT on the fee, so both prices sit under the legal maximum.

How long is the clutch warranty and what do I have to do to keep it?

Two years or 20,000 miles, whichever comes first from the original invoice date or mileage, on a complete clutch kit fitted to a non-commercial vehicle; one year or 12,000 miles on taxis, driving schools and light commercials. The condition is free clutch checks at 500, 3,000, 6,000 and 12,000 miles, each recorded on the invoice. Skip them, or lose the invoice, and the warranty is void. It covers manufacturing defects and excludes misuse, the wrong oil grade, failures caused by other faulty parts and wear and tear.

Will an MOT tell me my clutch is on the way out?

No. The MOT inspection manual is organised into eleven numbered sections — vehicle identification, brakes, steering, visibility, lamps and electrical equipment, axles and wheels and tyres and suspension, body and structure, other equipment, nuisance, bus and coach supplementary tests and seat belt installation — and none of them covers the transmission. A clutch is never inspected and never appears on a certificate, which is why a free check is worth having.

Who owns Mr Clutch?

The trading company is Mr Clutch Autocentres Ltd., company 03678433, incorporated on 3 December 1998 at 2 Priory Road, Rochester ME2 2EG and formerly named Global 4 Safety Limited, Mister Clutch Limited and Mr Clutch Limited. Its controlling shareholder is C I (Holdings) Limited, company 03628474, with 75% or more of the shares and votes from the same address, effective 22 December 2016 although only filed on 16 February 2026. Joseph, Sefer and William Yussuf have been directors since 1 June 1999.

What happens if I have a complaint that the branch will not settle?

Raise it with the customer services team first, by email or on 0800 67 67 67. Once that is exhausted the terms point you to the National Conciliation Service, the RMI’s CTSI-certified dispute body, at PO Box 6562, Rugby CV21 9QP or 01788 538317. The scheme gives you 12 months from the company’s final response to refer the dispute, aims to decide within 90 calendar days of a complete file, and may decline claims under £50. The terms do not print the scheme’s website address, and no internal response deadline is published.

Does a Mr Clutch service keep a manufacturer’s warranty intact?

The service checklist includes stamping the service book, and the company says it uses only new parts of original manufacturer specification. What it does not say anywhere on its servicing pages is whether it can update a digital service record, which is how most cars sold in the last ten years hold their history rather than in a paper book. If your car has no service book, ask the branch before booking how the work will be recorded.

Our verdict

Mr Clutch is the rare garage chain that will tell you the price before you arrive, and on the one job it was built around it backs the work for two years or 20,000 miles rather than the usual twelve months. A full service is £261.95 up to 1300cc with fully synthetic oil included, the MOT costs £20.00 if you take it with a major service and £41.99 if you do not, and the clutch check that decides whether you need any of this is free and takes minutes. For a car over ten years old — and 45.7% of British cars now are — that is a sensible first phone call. Go in knowing three things. The brochure called “No Surprises” is two years behind the website and £60 cheaper on a full service, so price the job off the live grid. The clutch page quotes both £150 and £385 and settles neither, so get the free check and a written quotation before committing. And if you want the second year of the clutch warranty, diarise the 500-mile check the moment you drive away, because that is the condition most people will miss.
