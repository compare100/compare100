My Car Inspections Review: Any Age, Any Write-Off Category

Rates and terms checked 21 November 2026 · Vehicle Inspections · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

My Car Inspections will send an engineer to a used car of any age — a Category N, S, C or D write-off, an import, a left-hand drive car, a van, a 4x4 or an electric one — and its 300+ point inspection happens where the car is, at the dealer or the private seller’s address, with the written report and photographs online within 24 hours. The two best-known names in the trade will not do that at their advertised price: the RAC’s Basic inspection, from £132, excludes write-offs, hybrids, imports, convertibles, vans, 4-wheel drives and anything over 10 years old or past 120,000 miles, and the AA caps all three of its packages at 20 years and sells its fixed £110 Basic for petrol and diesel cars only. All three providers’ figures were read from their own pages on 21 November 2026.

The price is the one thing this company does not print. No page carries a price list or a from-price; a figure comes from its own booking tool, which asks for the vehicle’s age bracket and make and takes no payment at that stage. The only money figure published anywhere on the site is a £30 charge for cancelling an inspection because of what the free history check turned up.

The business behind the brand is Inspect My Car Ltd, company 12650225, incorporated on 8 June 2020, with its registered office at 128 City Road, London EC1V 2NX and its inspection planning team at Cheatles Bridge, Dog Lane, Birmingham B76 9JD on 0121 339 5037. The phones are open 8.30 to 18.00 on weekdays and 9.00 to midday on Saturday; booking runs around the clock.

The cars the AA and the RAC send away

This is the part of the product worth reading first, because it is the only place in the category where the three providers genuinely disagree. The RAC prints its exclusions as a footnote to the Basic package: imports, convertibles, write-offs, hybrids, vans, vehicles over 10 years old, vehicles with more than 5 seats, prestige vehicles, 4-wheel drives, LPGs, motorhomes and vehicles with mileage in excess of 120,000 miles. The AA caps all three of its packages at vehicles up to 20 years old, says in its own words that the Basic inspection is only available for petrol/diesel engine cars, and will not touch motorcycles, London taxis or anything over 3.5 tonnes.

My Car Inspections publishes the opposite list. Its pre-purchase page covers used vehicles of any age and names classic cars, imports and exports, write-offs in Categories N, S, C and D, hybrid, plug-in hybrid and electric, high performance, vans, 4x4 and left-hand drive. There is no mileage ceiling anywhere on the site and no age ceiling either. A reader shopping for a fifteen-year-old diesel estate, a Category S repair or a Japanese import is the customer both big brands price out of their entry tier, and is this firm’s ordinary job.

One qualification on that, read from the firm’s own pages rather than inferred. The classic car page lists a shorter set — left-hand drive, imports, 4x4, high performance and vans — and leaves out write-offs and electric cars, which the pre-purchase and salvage pages both carry. The three lists are not identical, so a reader with an electric classic should say so when booking rather than assume the widest list applies.

What 300 points buys, and the four words that bound it

The inspection is described as a 300+ point examination carried out where the car is, at a dealer’s premises or a private seller’s address, with a written report and images delivered online within 24 hours. The checklist runs to ten headings: documentation, body, interior, engine, clutch and transmission, exhaust and fuel, front steering and suspension, rear suspension, wheels and brakes, and a road test. A free vehicle history check is run before the engineer is sent, which is the step that produces the £30 figure further down this page.

The bound on all of it is set out in the FAQ page, and it is the kind of thing worth knowing before the engineer arrives rather than after. The standard inspection is a visual and external check that does not include a fault diagnosis, or any dismantling or removal of parts. No stress or weight test is applied to seat fittings, belts, grab rails, mountings or harnesses. On a campervan, motorhome or horsebox the roof hatch internals and fittings are not inspected, and the vehicle should be unladen.

The road test is the one figure the firm does not quantify. Its FAQ says the engineer drives within the local speed limits and stops there. The RAC publishes 3 miles on its Basic, 10 on the Comprehensive and 20 on the Advanced; the AA publishes up to 10 miles on both of its upper tiers; ClickMechanic’s checklist gives a single driving test (up to 5 miles) across its 86-, 136- and 185-point tiers. A buyer who cares whether a gearbox is tested warm has no published distance to hold this firm to, and the honest advice is to agree one on the phone.

Two conditions sit alongside that and both are the seller’s to meet. The engineer needs keys, enough fuel for the road test, and a hard level surface off the highway; without those the inspection may be restricted, and the FAQ states plainly that the full inspection fee will remain payable. Daylight and reasonable weather are also needed, and poor weather may restrict the examination. The car should be clean, roadworthy and driveable, with a valid MOT certificate where applicable and the DVLA registration documents to hand.

The battery report nobody else in this category has

On an electric or hybrid car the firm says its full EV inspections also include a Aviloo battery report, giving an overall state of health score and evaluating four named inputs: battery usage history, cell voltage balance, temperature readings and battery management system data. That is a real, named, third-party test, and it is something no other provider in this category offers at any price. The AA’s Advanced package includes a Digital Battery Test without ever saying which battery it reads, and the RAC publishes nothing equivalent on any of its three tiers.

AVILOO’s own figures are worth having. It states that in at least 95% of reference measurements, the maximum possible deviation of the SoH value does not exceed ±3%, and says on the same page that its testing is based on more than 500,000 independent tests. It sells two products: a FLASH test taken off the car’s diagnostic interface in 3 minutes, and a PREMIUM test that records a complete charging cycle of the traction battery. Its certificate confirms the state of health (SoH) of the traction battery and carries a QR code so a later buyer can verify it.

There are two questions to put to the booking team, and they are questions rather than accusations. The first is which AVILOO test is included, because the two are not the same exercise: a 3-minute read of the car’s own control units is not a recorded charging cycle. The second follows from what the certifying body publishes about itself. CARA, named on the inspection page as recognising the test, is the Car Remarketing Association Europe, a non-profit organization set up by key players in the use car market industry, and its Battery Health Check scheme places the AVILOO Flash Test in its basic category — of which CARA itself writes that it should be noted that the Basic test is not an independent assessment of the battery health, because it reads state of health from the battery management system. AVILOO also notes on the same subject that deviations in the SoH value can be caused by faulty or manipulated data in the control units. Both statements are the providers’ own, and together they are the reason to ask which test you are getting.

One further gap, and it belongs to the whole trade rather than to this firm. Nothing on the hybrid page describes a high-voltage isolation or insulation test, and nothing names a qualification level for the technicians who work on the pack — the page says only that they are fully independent qualified engineers with vast mechanical, electrical and panel work experience, and the vehicle inspections page calls them EV ready technicians. There is still no British standard an inspector can be tested against on a traction battery, which is why the AVILOO report is a sensible answer to a problem the industry has not solved. The battery test is also stated to be an add-on for hybrids for an additional fee, and that fee is not published either.

An engineer who owes a duty to the court above the person paying

This firm does three jobs the other four providers on this site do not. It writes expert witness reports for motor disputes, saying that the format of our report will be governed by the courts Civil Procedure Rules (Part 35) and any directions provided by your legal council, delivered within 72 hours of the examination. It inspects salvage categories where an owner disputes the grade an insurer applied. And it carries out post-repair roadworthiness inspections on Category S and N cars to put in front of an insurer, with the report emailed within 24 hours of the inspection.

Part 35 is free to read and it is the most useful thing on this page, because it is the standard the firm has volunteered to be held to. Rule 35.3 says in two sentences that it is the duty of experts to help the court on matters within their expertise and that this duty overrides any obligation to the person from whom experts have received instructions or by whom they are paid. Rule 35.10 requires the report to end with a statement that the expert understands and has complied with their duty to the court and to state the substance of all material instructions, whether written or oral — so a buyer cannot quietly steer the conclusion, and nor can the seller. Rule 35.1 restricts expert evidence to that which is reasonably required to resolve the proceedings. Under rule 35.6 the other side may put written questions once, within 28 days of service, and if the expert does not answer the court may bar reliance on them or bar recovery of their fees. The rule as published was last updated on 31 May 2021.

For an ordinary pre-purchase buyer that framework is a quiet reassurance rather than a feature: the same engineers who sign reports under a duty that outranks the fee are the ones walking round a car on a driveway. It also has a limit the sales page does not mention, and it is in rule 35.4(1): no party may call an expert or put in evidence an expert’s report without the court’s permission. A report bought privately and written to Part 35 is admissible if a court allows it, not because it was written that way, and the application has to identify the field and the issues and give a costs estimate. Anyone buying this product for a live dispute should take that point to their solicitor before instructing anybody.

Checking the accreditation yourself, in about two minutes

The expert witness and valuation pages both say the assessors are accredited by the IAEA and leave it at that — our independent car assessors are accredited by the IAEA — without expanding the initials. They stand for the Institute of Automotive Engineer Assessors, which turns out to be a serious thing to claim: a registered charity, number 273452, a Professional Affiliate of the Engineering Council, holding what it calls Learned Society status. It is also one of the few bodies in this trade a reader can audit without asking anyone’s permission.

The grades are not interchangeable. A Member, post-nominals M.Inst.A.E.A., has to complete at least four of the Institute’s written examinations — sections 2, 3 and 4 compulsory, plus a choice of section 1 or 5, at 100 pounds a section — and hold a practical examination less than four years old, at which candidates are required to achieve 70% in all learning outcomes across the three vehicle assessments. An Associate, A.Inst.A.E.A., sits an oral examination of 40 minutes and 10 questions at a 60% pass mark, with a salvage categorisation question that must itself score 60%, or passes two written papers and holds a current ATA VDA certificate. Every grade must record a minimum of 30 hours in a 12-month period, of which 20 hours must be formal learning. Subscriptions run at £149 for corporate members and £145 for Associates, each plus £3 to the Institute’s benevolent fund.

Then there is the grade that does none of that. An Affiliate pays £75 a year and is admitted on educational and employment grounds rather than by examination — none appears in the Institute’s description of the grade — and it states flatly that the grade of Affiliate does not convey or confirm any form of qualification or level of competency and does not provide the holder with any post nominals that can be referenced in reports. The Institute keeps a public register, searchable by forename and surname, which prints each member’s grade — and notes that Affiliates are not listed in the Member Register.

That is the whole value of the claim, and it is in the reader’s hands rather than the firm’s. Ask for the engineer’s name when the booking is confirmed, search the register, and read the grade column. A Member or a Fellow is a tested assessor. An Associate has passed an oral examination that includes a compulsory salvage question. A name that does not appear at all is, on the Institute’s own rule, either an Affiliate or not a member — and in neither case has anybody examined them. The firm is not being asked to prove anything it has not already claimed; it simply never says which grade, on any of its pages.

The salvage work, where the qualification is specified

The write-off inspection is the one product where the firm names a qualification instead of an adjective, and it is also the one it says is done in-house: our in-depth total loss/write off inspections are only carried out by our own fully qualified staff engineers. Those engineers are described as registered professionals at The Institute of the Motor Industry holding valid ATA/VDA vehicle damage assessor qualifications which require certification every 3 years at Thatcham Research, and the page adds that a Category A, B, N or S vehicle on the MIAFTR register must be assessed by an APQ (Appropriately Qualified Person).

Thatcham publishes what that costs in effort. Its IMI Vehicle Damage Assessor accreditation is a four-day programme for assessors who need accreditation for compliance to BS10125, gated behind three years industry experience, with three preparation days, six 15-minute online tests at a required minimum pass rate of 70%, and an assessment day. Reaccreditation is a further two days, open to people who should have completed the accreditation within the past 3 years. The IAEA’s own practical examination is also four days — three of preparation and one assessment day — is open only to candidates who are a minimum of an affiliate member of the IAEA, and asks for at least three years’ experience. The three-year recertification the firm quotes is real and it is an outside body’s rule, not its own.

Two small things on the same page. It spells the qualification APQ in one sentence and AQP in the next, which is a typographical slip rather than a substantive one, but it is on the page a reader uses to judge technical care. And the same page says the Cat inspection includes a road test and diagnostic scan of the vehicle safety systems, where the FAQ says the standard inspection carries no fault diagnosis. Those two can be reconciled — a scan of driver assistance systems after a repair is not the same exercise as diagnosing a fault on a car for sale — but the site never reconciles them, so ask which applies to the booking you are making.

Who actually turns up, and what the firm promises about them

On a pre-purchase booking the answer is not always an employee, and the About page says so without being asked: these assessors include our own staff engineers in addition to specialist freelance inspectors. The firm says it will only allocate engineers that have suitable qualifications & expertise and describes the network as audited, insured & accredited independent engineers throughout the UK. The FAQ uses three different phrases for the same person across one document — network engineer, appointed agent and our staff and contractors — which is at least consistent with a mixed model honestly described.

Volunteering the freelance half is a point in the firm’s favour, because it is the thing a competitor’s marketing usually leaves out, and the salvage carve-out above shows the distinction is managed rather than ignored. It also puts the register check in the last section where it belongs: on a national freelance network, the name of the engineer allocated to your car is the only thing that tells you what you are buying.

The insurance position is stated and is the right one for a service like this. The firm says its people carry suitable Road Traffic, Public Liability and Professional Negligence insurance, and that our staff and contractors will use reasonable skill and care when providing the vehicle Examination service — which is the wording the Consumer Rights Act 2015 imposes on any service supplied to a consumer in any event. Professional negligence cover is the one that matters on a report, and a majority of firms in this trade never mention it.

Cancelling, and the three ways a fee stays payable

Cancel for any reason up to close of business on the day before and there is no charge. After that the booking will be subject to the full fee, which cannot be checked in advance because the full fee is never published. Cancel because of what the free history check turned up and there is a charge of £30 — the only money figure on the public site, and a reasonable one, since the check has already been paid for by then. The firm also reserves a right most of its rivals do not spell out: where the inspection cannot proceed for reasons beyond its control, the inspection fee is non refundable, and where an engineer meets abusive behaviour or a vehicle parked unsafely, you will not be entitled to any refund.

Set that against what the two national brands publish. The AA charges £15 for a same-day cancellation and refunds in full before the day, on a Basic price of £110 that is fixed rather than indicative. ClickMechanic allows free cancellation at 24 hours, up to £20 inside it and up to £40 once the mechanic has set off. Each of those is a capped, knowable amount. The full fee is not, and on this provider it is the reader who carries that uncertainty.

What the report is not is set out just as clearly, and it is standard for the trade rather than mean. The inspection does not constitute a warranty or guarantee of durability, nor is it a guarantee against defects arising in the future, and liability cannot be accepted for defects not reasonably apparent or for vehicles that have been subject to abnormal use. The engineer is also instructed to give the buyer the chance to allow you the option to abort the inspeciton should you wish to, typographical slip and all, which is the useful half of the same clause.

If it goes wrong, there is a conduct route and no redress route

Nothing on any of the firm’s fourteen public pages names an ombudsman or an alternative dispute resolution body, and a pre-purchase inspection is not a regulated financial product, so there is no Financial Ombudsman Service behind it either. The complaints wording is short: complaints can be notified by phone but must be made in writing, the firm asks that you please bring your complaint to our attention as soon as you can, and it reserves the right to investigate and re-examine the vehicle before any repair is carried out. It confirms that this does not affect your statutory rights to take legal action or exercise any other legal remedy, under the laws of England and Wales.

The IAEA runs the only external route, and it is worth understanding exactly what it is and is not. A complaint is free, goes to the Institute’s secretary, must be submitted within 6 months of the event, and draws a response normally within 30 days after an initial investigation by a member of the Institute’s National Council. The standard applied is the Institute’s 22 Rules of Conduct, which include undertaking only professional tasks for which they are competent and disclosing limitations of competence, avoiding real or perceived conflict of interest, and acting in a strictly fiduciary and professional manner towards clients and employers. An appeal goes to an elected committee of at least three National Council members whose findings are final, and an expelled member’s name and recorded address are published.

Here is the limit, and it is the single most important thing on this page for anyone weighing what a bad report would cost them. The Institute considers complaints against an individual member only, not against a company, and its panel’s powers run to recommendations for disciplinary action only — corrective action, suspension or in extreme cases expulsion. No money changes hands. A complainant who has bought a car on a report they believe was wrong has a professional conduct route against a person and a contract claim against a firm, and nothing in between. That is true of every inspection provider in this category, and this one is simply the first to publish the body that would hear the first half.

Four claims on the site that could not be stood up

An award with no awarder. The home page says the firm was voted best report in a audit by an independent consumer magazine, ahead of the AA and RAC, and the About page repeats it as a blind audit of four companies. No magazine is named, no date is given and no issue is cited, so there is nothing to look up. An award is worth more with a name attached, and this one has a good chance of being real — but as printed it cannot be checked by a reader or by anyone else.

A rival package that does not exist. The pre-purchase page positions the inspection as the equivalent to the AA Comprehensive Inspection & RAC Essentials Plus car inspection. The AA does sell a Comprehensive, at £199 and up to 214 points. The RAC’s own inspections page, read on 21 November 2026, sells Basic from £132, Comprehensive from £225 and Advanced from £290, and nothing called Essentials Plus appears on it. Half of a two-brand comparison therefore has no counterpart to compare with.

A decade that the register does not show. The About page opens with for over a decade My Car Inspections have been providing independent vehicle inspections, and the home page with our Engineer’s have carried out 1000’s of independent inspections over the last decade. The company named on the contact page, Inspect My Car Ltd, was incorporated on 8 June 2020, which is five years and five months. A trading name can of course be older than the company that now runs it, and the claim may be perfectly true of the people rather than the entity. What cannot be done is check it: a company registered as My Car Inspections Limited, number 07512498, was a different business incorporated on 1 February 2011 that filed dormant accounts and was dissolved on 17 June 2014 after a compulsory strike-off. A reader searching Companies House for the brand name finds that company.

A site stamped three years ago. Every page carries a footer reading © 2023, including the pages selling an AVILOO battery report and ADAS scanning. The company’s filings are current — total exemption full accounts to 30 June 2025 were filed on 2 February 2026, the next are due by 31 March 2027, and the confirmation statement of 1 June 2026 is on the register with nothing overdue and no charge, gazette notice or insolvency filing in 18 filings since incorporation. The business is plainly being run. The website is the part that has not been dated since 2023, which matters only because it is the document a buyer reads.

The free checks to run before you book anything

Two of them cost nothing and neither needs this firm or any other. GOV.UK’s vehicle recall checker will tell you whether a registration has an outstanding safety recall, and the MOT history service gives every test result, every advisory and the recorded mileage at each test — which is the cheapest mileage-discrepancy check in existence and the first thing any inspector looks at. Running both before you pay for an inspection occasionally ends the matter for free.

The firm’s own free history check is the third, and it is the reason the £30 cancellation charge exists. It runs before the engineer is dispatched, and the booking terms are written on the assumption that it may be what stops the purchase. A reader who already holds a provenance report should say so, because the paid-for part of this product is an engineer standing next to a car, not a database lookup.

One last practical point on the quote. The booking tool asks only for the vehicle’s age bracket — up to 10 years or older than 10 years — and the manufacturer, and it states that no payment is required during this process. Both national brands footnote their prices as varying by vehicle: the RAC with prices depend on the age, value and model of vehicle, the AA with a disclosure that at least 10% of customers paid this price or less for the specific inspection between 1-Jul-26 and 31-Aug-26. A from-price on an inspection is an estimate everywhere in this category. The difference here is that there is no from-price at all to start from.

Common questions

How much does a My Car Inspections inspection cost?

The company publishes no price list. A figure comes from its own booking tool, which asks for the vehicle's age bracket — up to 10 years or older than 10 years — and the manufacturer, and takes no payment at the quote stage. For context from the two national brands' own pages on 21 November 2026, the AA's Basic is a fixed £110 and its Comprehensive from £199, and the RAC's Basic is from £132, Comprehensive from £225 and Advanced from £290. The only money figure published anywhere on the My Car Inspections site is a £30 charge for cancelling after the free history check.

Will it inspect a Category S or Category N write-off?

Yes, and that is one of the reasons to use it. Categories N, S, C and D are all covered, and the firm says total-loss inspections are carried out only by its own staff engineers rather than its freelance network. The RAC's Basic inspection excludes write-offs outright. The firm also offers a post-repair roadworthiness inspection for a repaired Cat S or Cat N car, with the report emailed within 24 hours, and will inspect a vehicle where the owner disputes the salvage category an insurer applied.

Does the inspection check an electric car's battery?

The hybrid and electric page says full EV inspections include an AVILOO battery report giving an overall state of health score from battery usage history, cell voltage balance, temperature readings and battery management system data. On a hybrid it is an add-on for an unpublished additional fee. Ask which AVILOO test is used: the FLASH test takes three minutes off the car's diagnostic interface, while the PREMIUM test records a complete charging cycle of the traction battery, and CARA's own scheme page says the basic category the Flash Test sits in is not an independent assessment of battery health.

How far does the engineer drive the car?

No distance is published. The FAQ page says the road test is carried out within the local speed limits and gives no mileage or duration. If the gearbox, clutch or cooling system matters to your decision, agree a distance with the planning team before booking. For comparison, the RAC publishes a road test of up to 3 miles on its Basic, 10 on its Comprehensive and 20 on its Advanced, the AA publishes up to 10 miles on both upper tiers, and ClickMechanic's checklist gives one figure, a driving test of up to 5 miles, across its 86-, 136- and 185-point tiers.

What does “IAEA accredited” actually mean?

The Institute of Automotive Engineer Assessors is a registered charity, number 273452, and a Professional Affiliate of the Engineering Council. A Member, M.Inst.A.E.A., passes at least four written examinations and a practical at which 70% is required in all learning outcomes across three vehicle assessments. An Associate, A.Inst.A.E.A., passes a 40-minute oral examination at 60%, including a compulsory salvage categorisation question, or two written papers plus a current ATA VDA certificate. An Affiliate is admitted on educational and employment grounds with no examination named, and the Institute states the grade confirms no qualification or level of competency and gives no post-nominals usable in reports. The Institute's public register is searchable by name and shows each member's grade, and Affiliates are not listed in it — so ask for the engineer's name and look it up.

Who do I complain to if the report is wrong?

The company first: complaints may be notified by phone but must be made in writing, and the firm reserves the right to re-examine the vehicle before any repair. There is no ombudsman and no ADR scheme covering vehicle inspections, and the service is not a regulated financial product. Separately, the IAEA takes free complaints against an individual member within 6 months of the event and normally replies within 30 days, measured against its 22 Rules of Conduct. Its panel can recommend corrective action, suspension or expulsion but awards no compensation, and it does not consider complaints against companies.

Its sole director and person with significant control is James Parkes, holding 75% or more of the shares. Its SIC codes are 71129, other engineering activities, and 71200, technical testing and analysis. A separate company called My Car Inspections Limited, number 07512498, was dissolved on 17 June 2014 after a compulsory strike-off and is unconnected to the current business.
