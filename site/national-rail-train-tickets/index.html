National Rail Train Tickets: What the Site Does and What the Rules Say

Rates and terms checked 21 November 2026 · Train Tickets · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

National Rail Enquiries handles an average of 2.5 million journey planning enquiries every weekday, and it charges nothing for any of them. Its journey planner, live departure boards, fares information and station pages are free, there is no account to open, and no booking fee exists anywhere on the site. Rail fares in England are frozen until March 2027, which the site says on its own home page.

What it does not do is sell you a ticket. Pick an option in its Season Ticket Calculator and the page tells you plainly what happens next: "Choose your option from the list and you will be directed to a ticket retailer website, where you can buy your tickets." The money is taken somewhere else, by a train company or by a licensed retailer, and whatever booking fee that seller charges is the one you pay. Read National Rail for the plan and the rules; buy wherever the fee is lowest.

The thing it does own is the rulebook. Every train ticket sold in Great Britain is sold under the National Rail Conditions of Travel, published on this site as a PDF — currently Version 8, issued 30 July 2026, the fourth edition since April 2024. That document, not the website, is where a passenger's rights actually live, and most of what follows comes straight out of it.

The journey planner behind 2.5 million enquiries a day

National Rail Enquiries describes its own job narrowly: it "provides journey planning, real-time information, ticket and fares information, and a range of rail-related information". The 2.5 million daily enquiries are counted across the website, the apps, the contact centre and the third-party services that take its data, which is a reasonable measure of how much of Britain's rail planning passes through one set of pages.

The live information comes from Darwin, the industry prediction engine. Darwin supplies "real-time arrival and departure predictions, platform numbers, delay estimates, as well as real-time schedule changes and cancellations", and it "powers all NRE and train operator customer facing real-time information tools, including websites, mobile apps and train station departure board screens". Since 1 June 2014 it has been licensed to all users on open terms, which is why a third-party app and the official one usually agree to the minute — they are reading the same feed. The journey planner itself is free and anonymous; nothing on it asks for a card.

Which ticket you end up buying, and when the clock starts

The fares pages are where the site earns its keep, because the rules behind each ticket type are national and the retailer you eventually buy from will not explain them. Off-Peak hours, in National Rail's own words, "begin at 09:30 from Monday to Friday in cities and large towns, and at 09:00 everywhere else", with weekends and bank holidays off-peak throughout. An Off-Peak Day ticket is valid only on the date printed on it; an Off-Peak Return runs for one month from that date.

Advance singles can "usually" be bought up to 12 weeks ahead, and the site adds the part most people get wrong — they remain available on many routes until the day of travel, "sometimes up to 10 minutes before departure", and around half a million Advance tickets priced under a tenner are released every week. The trade-off is rigidity: an Advance ticket is valid on one train on one date, and changing it costs £10.

Season tickets cover seven consecutive days, a month, any period from one month and one day up to a year, or a Flexi Season giving eight days of travel inside 28. The Season Ticket Calculator prices those against each other and divides by the days you enter to give a cost per day. Two of its rules of thumb are worth carrying: a seven-day season beats singles if you travel three or more days in the week, and an annual beats monthlies if you need ten months or more. A year of Flexi arrives as twelve separate 28-day tickets rather than one.

Version 8, issued 30 July 2026, and whose contract it is

The National Rail Conditions of Travel are the terms on which every ticket in Great Britain is sold, and this is the site that publishes them. The current edition is dated "From 30th July 2026" on its cover and carries the line "Copyright Rail Settlement Plan Ltd 2026 Version 8 Issued 30th July 2026". Three superseded editions sit beside it for download: one valid from 1 April 2026 to 29 July 2026, one from 23 November 2025 to 31 March 2026, and one from 2 April 2024 to 22 November 2025.

National Rail is straightforward about what the document is for. It sets out "the minimum level of service you are entitled to expect when you travel with a valid ticket", and "[i]ndividual train companies may offer above this minimum, but not below it". That is the sentence to hold on to when an operator advertises its own generosity: the floor is national, the extra is the operator's.

The contract, though, is not with the website and not with the retailer. Part A of the Conditions describes "the binding contract that comes into effect between you and the Train Companies" when a ticket is bought, and Part B puts it more precisely still: "you enter into a binding contract with each of the Train Companies whose trains your Ticket allows you to use". Appendix A lists the 23 of them. Condition 4.3 then sets the only limit on where you may buy: "You may only buy your Ticket from a Train Company or Licensed Retailer, otherwise it may not be valid." Applying the Conditions is a requirement of the passenger licence the Office of Rail and Road grants each train company, which is what gives a document published by an industry body its teeth.

Sixty minutes and half the fare, as a floor rather than a promise

Condition 32.1 is blunt about the threshold: "You are entitled to compensation if the delay was 60 minutes or longer, regardless of fault." The table at 33.3 sets the amount at 50% of the price paid on a single, or on a return delayed in both directions, and 50% of the relevant portion where only one leg went wrong. A claim has to be made within 28 days of completing the journey unless the operator extends that, and payment follows within 14 days of the claim being agreed. Condition 33.4 carves out delays under an hour caused entirely outside the rail industry's control.

For a season ticket holder the Conditions pass the question straight back: compensation follows "[t]he discount or compensation arrangements in the relevant Train Company's Passenger's Charter". That is the honest shape of rail compensation — a national floor of 50% at an hour, and every operator's own Delay Repay scheme sitting above it on its own terms. National Rail's own compensation page declines to put any number on it, saying only that "each train company has its own compensation threshold, which will be detailed in their Passenger's Charter" and that "[y]ou will need to request compensation directly from your train company". For a page whose whole purpose is national information, that is a gap rather than an answer.

Disruption itself is better covered. Where a train is cancelled, Condition 28.3 lets a passenger travel "up to either two trains prior or two trains after the affected service, using the same operator". Where a delay is likely to run beyond 60 minutes, the ticket may be used later instead; past the same 60 minutes, meals or refreshments may be provided. Miss a booked train because a connection failed and Condition 9.4 lets you take the next service without penalty, and if a delay means the ticket expires mid-journey, Condition 11.2 lets you finish it anyway.

The refund window closed on 1 April 2026

The rule that catches the most people is new and it is national. Condition 29 now requires a refund claim on an unused ticket to be made "prior to your Ticket(s) first becoming valid for travel", with the information panel beside it putting a time on that: no later than 23:59 the day before the ticket becomes valid. A later claim is considered "only in exceptional circumstances" and must reach the retailer within 28 days of the ticket expiring. The Department for Transport confirmed the change from its own side when the fares freeze began, noting that from 1 April passengers "will only be able to claim refunds for unused tickets ahead of travel".

The fee for that refund is capped rather than fixed. Condition 29.2 sets an administration charge that "will not exceed £5 per Ticket (£10 per Ticket in the case of Season Tickets)", and the refund itself must be paid within one month of the application. National Rail's own ticket pages quote the same ceilings from the other direction — "an admin fee of no more than £5" on an Off-Peak cancellation, "an administration charge of no more than £10" on a season.

None of that applies when the railway is at fault, and the distinction is worth knowing before an argument at a ticket window. Where a train is delayed, cancelled or rescheduled, Condition 30 gives a full refund "with no administration fee charged", on a claim made within 28 days of the date you meant to travel and paid within 14 days of agreement. National Rail states the same thing in a sentence: "your unused ticket can be refunded without any fees by the original retailer". A voluntary change of plan costs up to £5; the operator's failure costs nothing.

Season ticket holders have their own ladder of charges, all capped. A refund on a forgotten or mislaid season carries an administration charge "not exceeding £10" and only two such claims are considered in any 12-month period; a duplicate costs up to £20; handing a season back early costs up to £10; and changing one costs nothing at all. The hard stop is Condition 40.4: "Annual Season Tickets do not qualify for a monetary refund after ten months and twelve days." The parallel rule for a forgotten railcard is tighter still, at a maximum of one claim in any 12 months.

Three items, two dogs and a £1,500 ceiling

The back half of the Conditions is the part nobody reads until it matters, and it is full of numbers no retailer will show you. Luggage is limited to three items taken into the passenger accommodation. A wheelchair is carried "subject to a maximum width of 70cm and length of 120cm" and a pushchair has to be capable of folding. A musical instrument over 30 x 70 x 90 cm travels at the train company's discretion; furniture over the same dimensions is not allowed on a passenger train at all.

Two dogs or other small domestic animals travel free. Anything that is not a dog needs a carrier "with dimensions not exceeding 85 x 60 x 60 cm", and livestock and non-domestic animals "cannot be carried in any circumstances". Children under five travel free without a ticket provided they are with a ticket holder; five to fifteen need a ticket and get a child discount, the size of which the Conditions leave to the fare rather than fixing.

Then the ceiling. Condition 26 caps "the maximum liability of a Train Company or Rail Service Provider" at £1,500 per passenger, with mobility and other disability equipment outside the cap and unlimited. Set £1,500 against a laptop, a bicycle and a suitcase and the case for travel insurance makes itself. Lost property has its own tariff: storage up to £2 per day per item, retrieval up to £30 per item, and anything not collected within three months is sold or disposed of. Penalty fares, where notices are displayed, are charged under the Railways (Penalty Fares) Regulations 2018; for journeys wholly within Scotland the minimum fare charged is the higher of the full undiscounted single or a sum not exceeding £20. A damaged ticket can be replaced for an administration charge of no more than £10.

What the fare freeze saves on the three routes the department costed

The biggest change to a rail fare in thirty years is not a discount but a pause, and National Rail carries it on its home page: "Rail fares have been frozen until March 2027, benefitting more than a billion passenger journeys across England." The freeze began in the week of 2 March 2026 and covers regulated fares — season tickets, commuter peak returns and off-peak returns between major cities — across England. The Department for Transport put the counterfactual at a 5.8% rise, built from July 2025 RPI plus 1%, and expects the freeze to save existing passengers £600 million in 2026/27, a figure it rounds to the nearest £100 million.

Its worked examples are the useful part, all for a commuter travelling three days a week on a flexi-season: £315 a year from Milton Keynes to London, £173 from Woking, and £57 from Bradford to Leeds. Commuters on the most expensive routes save more than £300 a year. For scale on what came before, the department's own figure is that fares rose 60% between 2010 and 2024.

Two limits matter and neither is advertised. The freeze applies in England, and the analysis behind it is based on the 14 train operating companies the department manages — it does not say that unregulated fares are frozen, only that the calculation "assumes that unregulated fares move in line with regulated fares", and it acknowledges that those operators "may earn some revenue from fares set by a TOC outside the scope of the fare freeze". Advance fares, which is what most leisure passengers actually buy, are not mentioned in the announcement at all.

£35 for a railcard, and the saving the industry measured itself

Railcards are the one product this side of the industry does sell, through the official railcard site rather than through National Rail itself, and the prices are flat: £35 for one year and £80 for three on the 16-25, Family & Friends, Senior and Veterans cards, with a Disabled Persons Railcard at £20 and £54. The 16-17 Saver, 26-30, Two Together and Network cards are also £35 for a year. Most give a third off; the 16-17 Saver gives 50% off most travel including season tickets, and Family & Friends gives a third off adult fares with 60% off for children aged five to fifteen.

The eligibility rules carry their own arithmetic. A Senior Railcard starts at 60. A 16-25 bought before a 24th birthday runs for three years. Family & Friends covers up to four adults and four children on one card; a Veterans Railcard covers the holder, one adult companion and up to four children; a Disabled Persons Railcard covers the holder and one adult companion. Two Together is the restricted one — travel together "after 09:30am on weekdays, or any time at weekends and on public holidays".

What makes the savings table unusual is that it is a measurement rather than a promise. The published annual figures — £417 on a 16-17 Saver, £303 on a Veterans, £283 on a 26-30, £212 on a 16-25, £154 on a Network, £132 on a Disabled Persons, £125 on Family & Friends, £114 on a Senior, £106 on Two Together, and £173 averaged across all of them — are "based on the average savings made by combined Railcard holders between April 2025 and March 2026", net of the card's own price. A real period, a real population and the cost deducted is better disclosure than most of the rail industry manages. One cost to note: a replacement card is free within 30 days of purchase and £10 after that, and changing the name on one carries a £10 administration fee.

Four company names on one website

Work out who runs National Rail Enquiries and you get four answers. The About This Site page says "We are part of the Rail Delivery Group (RDG)", that "[m]anagement of the site is shared by Train Operating Companies and RDG", and that "[t]he National Rail website and app is managed by the Rail Delivery Group". The Conditions of Travel are copyright Rail Settlement Plan Ltd. The official railcard site's footer reads "© 2026 ATOC Limited". RDG's own corporate site is "designed and maintained by Webx UK". The passenger-facing pages carry a bare "© 2026" and name nobody.

Companies House untangles it. ATOC Limited, company 03069033, was incorporated on 12 June 1995 and is still active, a private limited company at First Floor North, 1 Puddle Dock, London EC4V 3DS, under SIC codes 49100 and 52212, with accounts last made up to 31 March 2025 and the next due by 31 December 2026. Rail Delivery Group Limited, company 08176197, was incorporated on 10 August 2012 at the same address, as a company limited by guarantee without share capital under SIC 52290. ATOC was recorded as a person with significant control over Rail Delivery Group from 6 April 2016 until 14 August 2017, since when RDG has declared that it knows of no registrable person at all. RDG's own history explains the shape: the Association of Train Operating Companies "was set up after privatisation in 1993", and "[i]n October 2016, the single name of Rail Delivery Group was adopted to describe ATOC and RDG". It is "mainly funded through our members, who are the train operating companies".

That matters for one reason. The body that publishes the contract, sets retailing standards and runs the railcard scheme is funded by the companies on the other side of that contract, and it is the same body that tells a passenger what their rights are. Government plans put National Rail's information role inside Great British Railways, whose promised app is described as a "one-stop shop for passengers to check train times and buy tickets at the best prices across the rail network, without any booking fees" — which would make the site that sells nothing today a seller tomorrow. Until then, the useful distinction is simple: this site is where the rules are published, not where the ticket is sold.

Where it wins

  • Free to use with no account, no booking fee and no commission taken on anything you plan
  • Publishes the full National Rail Conditions of Travel as a downloadable PDF, along with the three previous editions
  • Impartial between 23 train companies and every licensed retailer, because it is not selling against any of them
  • An average of 2.5 million journey planning enquiries every weekday, answered from the Darwin feed that also drives station departure screens
  • Darwin has been licensed on open terms since 1 June 2014, so the same live data sits behind third-party apps as behind the official one
  • The Season Ticket Calculator prices weekly, monthly, Flexi and annual options side by side and gives a cost per day
  • Railcard savings are published as measured figures with their period attached, not as marketing estimates
  • Names the Rail Ombudsman and its telephone number rather than leaving a complaint to die with the operator

Where it falls short

  • It sells nothing, so every price you find ends with a handover to someone else's checkout, and the site does not publish what any of those retailers charge to sell a ticket — the booking fee is decided after you leave.
  • Version 8 of the Conditions contradicts itself about its own effective date: the contents page says Appendix A's list of train companies applies as of 30 July 2026 while the heading above the list says 1 April 2026.
  • Four editions of the Conditions have been issued since April 2024, two of them in 2026 alone, and the site publishes the superseded PDFs without any statement of what changed between them.
  • The compensation the Conditions guarantee is thin — nothing at all below 60 minutes, and 50% of the fare above it — and National Rail's own compensation page quotes no percentage, no minute threshold and no claim deadline, sending you to each operator's Passenger's Charter instead.
  • Since 1 April 2026 a refund on an unused Anytime, Off-Peak or Season ticket has to be claimed before the ticket becomes valid, by 23:59 the day before travel; afterwards it is considered only in exceptional circumstances, and an annual Season Ticket stops qualifying for any monetary refund after ten months and twelve days.
  • The site does not say who runs it on any page a passenger is likely to open: the copyright line reads only "© 2026", the Conditions are copyright Rail Settlement Plan Ltd, the official railcard site is copyright ATOC Limited, and only the About This Site page names the Rail Delivery Group.
  • The train company's liability is capped at £1,500 per passenger under Condition 26, which is low against the value of what people routinely carry, and nothing on the consumer pages mentions it.
  • No independent body rates it. Defaqto, Which? and Fairer Finance score nothing in rail information, and the Rail Ombudsman produces no score, so there is no outside measure of the service at all.

Common questions

Does National Rail sell train tickets?

No. Its Season Ticket Calculator says so directly: "Choose your option from the list and you will be directed to a ticket retailer website, where you can buy your tickets." National Rail Enquiries plans journeys and publishes fares and rules; the sale is made by a train company or a licensed retailer, and the booking fee is theirs to set. Condition 4.3 of the Conditions of Travel is the only restriction on where you buy: "You may only buy your Ticket from a Train Company or Licensed Retailer, otherwise it may not be valid."

What does National Rail charge to use the site?

Nothing. There is no booking fee, no card fee and no account. The Darwin feed behind its live departure information has been licensed on open terms since 1 June 2014, which is why third-party apps show the same predictions. The fee you pay comes from whichever retailer you are handed to.

How much compensation am I entitled to if my train is late?

The national floor is 50% of the price paid for a delay of 60 minutes or longer, under Conditions 32 and 33, claimed within 28 days of finishing the journey and paid within 14 days of the claim being agreed. Nothing is guaranteed below an hour. Most train companies pay more than that through their own Delay Repay scheme, and the Conditions say operators "may offer above this minimum, but not below it" — so check your operator's Passenger's Charter, which is where National Rail's own compensation page sends you.

Can I still get a refund on a ticket I did not use?

Only if you claim before the ticket becomes valid, which in practice means by 23:59 the day before travel. That rule took effect on 1 April 2026. A later claim is considered only in exceptional circumstances and must be made within 28 days of the ticket expiring. The administration charge is capped at £5 per ticket, or £10 for a season. If the train was delayed, cancelled or rescheduled, Condition 30 gives a full refund with no administration fee at all.

Are rail fares still going up in 2026?

Regulated fares in England are frozen until March 2027 — season tickets, commuter peak returns and off-peak returns between major cities. The Department for Transport says they would otherwise have risen 5.8%, and puts the saving at £600 million in 2026/27, worth £315 a year to a three-day-a-week commuter from Milton Keynes to London and £57 from Bradford to Leeds. The freeze covers the 14 operators the department manages, and the analysis only assumes that unregulated fares follow.

How much luggage can I take on a British train?

Three items into the passenger accommodation, under Condition 23. A wheelchair is carried up to 70cm wide and 120cm long; a musical instrument over 30 x 70 x 90 cm travels at the train company's discretion and furniture over that size is not allowed at all. Two dogs or small domestic animals travel free, with non-dogs needing a carrier no larger than 85 x 60 x 60 cm. Note the liability ceiling of £1,500 per passenger in Condition 26, with disability equipment excluded from it.

Which railcard saves the most, and what do they cost?

Most cost £35 for a year or £80 for three; a Disabled Persons Railcard is £20 and £54. On the industry's own measurement of holders between April 2025 and March 2026, net of the card price, the 16-17 Saver saved £417 a year, a Veterans card £303 and a 26-30 card £283, against an average of £173 across all cards. A replacement costs £10 once 30 days have passed.

Who do I complain to about a train company or a ticket retailer?

The operator or retailer first, then the Rail Ombudsman at railombudsman.org or 0330 094 0362, whose address is 1st Floor, Premier House, Argyle Way, Stevenage SG1 2AD. The Conditions are governed by the law of England and Wales, except where a ticket is bought in Scotland for travel wholly within Scotland.

Our verdict

National Rail Enquiries is the best free planning tool on the British railway and the worst place to judge what a ticket costs to buy, for the same reason: it is not a shop. Use it for the timetable, the live platform numbers, the Season Ticket Calculator and above all for the Conditions of Travel, which set the floor under every ticket sold in Great Britain — 50% back at 60 minutes, a £5 cap on a refund admin fee, three items of luggage, two dogs, £1,500 of liability. Then take the journey you have planned to whichever retailer charges least to sell it, because that fee is the only part of the price that varies and this site will not tell you what it is. With regulated fares frozen until March 2027, the fare itself is one thing you no longer have to shop around for.
