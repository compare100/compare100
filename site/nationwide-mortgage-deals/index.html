Nationwide Mortgage Deals: 4.99% Fixed, a 4.09% Tracker and Six Times Income

Nationwide's cheapest fixed rate is 4.99%, on a two-year fix to 60% loan-to-value carrying a £1,499 product fee and a minimum loan of £300,000, and its cheapest two-year tracker is 4.09% — Bank Rate plus 0.34 points. Both figures are taken from Nationwide's own intermediary product guide, effective 30 September 2026 and read on 10 October 2026.

It is the biggest building society in the country and, on its own audited numbers for the year to 31 March 2026, the second-biggest mortgage lender: £286.3 billion of mortgage balances at a 16.3% share of the market, and £45.8 billion of new lending in twelve months at a 15.6% share. Arrears are low — 0.39% of residential accounts were more than three months behind — and it keeps 605 branches open under a Branch Promise now extended to 2030. It also publishes a dated product guide and an archive of every rate sheet it has issued since December 2023, which is more than most lenders will let a borrower see.

The thing that separates Nationwide from most of its rivals is not the rate. It is how much it will lend against a given income: six times for buyers, movers and remortgage customers, and six and a half on a like-for-like remortgage. The rate itself has been moving — Nationwide repriced on 18 and 24 August and on 10, 15 and 30 September 2026, and the direction since mid-August has been upward, so a 4.48% figure quoted anywhere else has been superseded.

Six times income, and the rule change that let Nationwide do it

Nationwide's own standard criteria stop at 4.49 times income. Its Helping Hand mortgage goes to six, which on a £30,000 salary is the difference between £134,700 and £180,000 — roughly £45,000 of extra borrowing from the same payslip. Nationwide's own worked examples put a single buyer on £30,000 at £180,000 against £225,000 for a couple on £50,000 without the product and £300,000 with it. It says Helping Hand helped over 17,000 customers onto the ladder during 2025.

The conditions are specific. You must be a first-time buyer, which Nationwide defines as not having had a mortgage in the last three years, and on a joint application both of you must qualify; you need at least a 5% deposit; and you have to take a five or ten-year fix. The five-year version runs to 95% LTV and the ten-year stops at 90%. Minimum income is £30,000 sole and £50,000 joint, cut from £35,000 and £55,000 on 15 July 2025. Self-employed income does not count towards it, and anyone buying through shared ownership or Right to Buy is excluded.

On 21 January 2026 Nationwide extended six times income beyond first-time buyers, to home movers and to remortgage customers borrowing more, at up to 95% LTV. That release set the entry price at £75,000 of income for a sole applicant and £100,000 for a joint one. The joint figure has since come down. Nationwide's current affordability criteria say the requirement is to "have an eligible income of £75,000 or more for both sole and joint applicants", a change its intermediary site dates to 16 July 2026, so a couple now needs £25,000 less between them than the January announcement demanded. Existing Nationwide members moving home face no minimum income at all — the criteria give the example of a £50,000 earner borrowing £300,000. And unlike Helping Hand, this route states plainly that "clients who are self employed are also eligible to borrow up to six times income".

The full ladder, as Nationwide publishes it, is worth reading as a whole: 6x on Helping Hand, 6x on a purchase or remortgage with extra borrowing where the income test is met, 6x for existing customers, 6.5x on a like-for-like remortgage (5.5x where the loan is interest-only), 4.75x on a further advance, 4.49x on standard lending and 4.49x on Equity Share. Six and a half times a £50,000 joint income is £325,000. Nationwide is careful to say these are ceilings rather than offers: they "represent the maximum possible amounts" and remain "subject to a Decision in Principle".

None of it was possible until the Financial Policy Committee moved in July 2025. The governing rule is the loan-to-income flow limit, which caps lending at 4.5 times income or above to 15% of a lender's new mortgages. The FPC recommended letting individual lenders exceed their own 15% while the market total held at 15%, on the reasoning that uneven use of the cap was keeping creditworthy households out. The Prudential Regulation Authority then consulted on removing the firm-level limit altogether in CP6/26, published on 1 April 2026 and closed on 1 July 2026, keeping the 15% aggregate and exempting firms with under £150 million of lending or fewer than 300 regulated mortgages.

Henry Jordan, Nationwide's Group Director of Mortgages, called the changes "a game changer for first-time buyers" and said the society had seen "a five-fold increase in the number of first-time buyers borrowing between 5.5 and six times income". A separate change on 15 May 2025 cut affordability stress rates by 0.75 to 1.25 percentage points, which Nationwide said let applicants borrow an average of £28,000 more.

Five repricings in six weeks, and where that leaves the rate

Nationwide changes its mortgage rates often, and it leaves a paper trail that makes the movement measurable rather than anecdotal. Its intermediary product guide carries an effective date on the front page and a document reference in the footer, and every superseded guide stays online with the dates it was valid between.

Follow one product through that archive and the picture is plain. The guide marked F6 (18.08.2026) priced the two-year fix for home movers at up to 60% LTV at 4.48% with a £1,499 fee on loans of £300,000 to £5 million, product code 205931. The current guide, F6 (30.09.2026), prices the same slot at 4.99% on the same fee and the same loan band, product code 207216. That is 0.51 percentage points in six weeks, or about £88 a month on a £300,000 loan over 25 years, and Bank Rate did not move in between: it has been 3.75% throughout, held again on 17 September 2026 with the next decision due on 5 November.

The archive dates the steps as well as the total. Nationwide's rate sheets ran from 4 August to 17 August, then 18 to 23 August, 24 August to 9 September, 10 to 14 September, 15 to 29 September, and from 30 September to now — five repricings in the period, announced to brokers on 17 August, 23 August, 9 September, 14 September and 29 September.

What the current guide gives a first-time buyer with a 5% deposit, at 90.01% to 95% LTV: a five-year fix at 5.64% with a £1,499 fee, 5.69% with a £999 fee or 5.74% with no fee, and a two-year fix at 5.79% with a £999 fee or 5.89% fee-free. At 60% LTV the same buyer pays 5.05% on a five-year fix with the larger fee and 5.54% on a two-year with none. A home mover at 85.01% to 90% pays 5.26% over five years with a £1,499 fee. The spread between the 60% and the 95% rate is roughly 0.6 to 0.7 percentage points, which is the price of a small deposit stated plainly.

Carlo Pileggi, Head of Mortgage Products, said in August that the cuts then brought "our lowest mortgage rate back below 4.5 per cent for new and existing borrowers moving home". That was accurate on the day and is no longer true of the range. The wider market has cooled with it: Nationwide's own house price index for September 2026, published on 2 October, put annual growth at 0.8%, halved from 1.6% in August, with prices down 0.2% on the month and the average house at £274,251. Robert Gardner, Nationwide's Chief Economist, called it "the weakest rate of growth since December 2025".

First-time buyers get £500 cashback paid within a month of completion — £500 per mortgage, not per applicant — with £250 to £500 more available through the Green Reward on an energy-efficient property. The stamp duty position in England and Northern Ireland is unchanged: nothing to £300,000, 5% from £300,001 to £500,000, and no first-time buyer relief at all above £500,000.

The fee tiers do not overlap, and one pound decides which one you get

Nationwide prices most fixed products three ways: a £1,499 fee, a £999 fee and no fee at all. The rate falls as the fee rises, which is ordinary. What is not ordinary is that the two fee-paying tiers are mutually exclusive by loan size. The £999 product at up to 60% LTV runs from £25,000 to £299,999. The £1,499 product at the same LTV runs from £300,000 to £5 million. There is no overlap, so the borrower never chooses between them — the size of the loan chooses.

Run the two-year numbers over a 25-year term and the boundary does something odd. At £299,999 a home mover takes 5.04% with a £999 fee: £1,760.76 a month, and £30,640 of true cost across the two years once the capital repaid is netted off. At £300,000 the same borrower is on 4.99% with a £1,499 fee: £1,752.02 a month, which looks better, and £30,842 of true cost, which is £202 worse. The extra pound of borrowing costs £201.78, because the 0.05-point rate cut does not pay for the extra £500 of fee.

The decision that does exist is fee against no fee, and it turns on size. The fee-free two-year at 60% LTV is 5.29% and is available from £25,000 to £5 million. On a £150,000 loan the £999 product costs £253 more over the two years than paying nothing and 5.29%. On £250,000 it saves £245. The crossover sits at roughly £200,800: below that the fee-free rate wins, above it the fee pays for itself. Spread across 24 payments, £1,499 is £62.46 a month and £999 is £41.63 — which is why the headline rate on its own tells a borrower under £200,000 almost nothing useful.

Maximum loan size also tightens as the deposit shrinks: £5 million to 75% LTV, £2 million from 75.01% to 85%, £1 million from 85.01% to 90% and £750,000 from 90.01% to 95%.

A tracker that undercuts every fix Nationwide sells

The cheapest way into a Nationwide mortgage in October 2026 is not a fixed rate. A home mover at up to 60% LTV can take a two-year tracker at Bank Rate plus 0.34%, which with Bank Rate at 3.75% is 4.09%, on a £999 fee and loans of £25,000 to £5 million. The fee-free version is Bank Rate plus 0.99%, or 4.74%. The cheapest fix in the same band is 4.99%.

On a £300,000 loan over 25 years that gap is £153.57 a month, or £1,843 a year, in the tracker's favour at today's Bank Rate. Two further features make it more attractive than the rate alone suggests. Nationwide states in the guide that "ERCs don't currently apply to tracker rate products", so there is no charge for leaving — a fixed-rate borrower in year one of a five-year deal pays 5%. And all tracker mortgages reserved on or after 2 May 2014 can be overpaid without limit, against the 10% a year allowed on a fix.

The catch is the obvious one and it deserves saying flatly: the rate moves with Bank Rate, there is no ceiling on it, and a borrower who takes 4.09% is betting that Bank Rate does not climb 0.90 points inside two years. The Monetary Policy Committee held at 3.75% on 17 September 2026 and reports again on 5 November. Nobody should read a tracker as a cheaper fix; it is a different product with the risk moved onto the borrower.

One more rate in the guide is worth knowing about because it is not a mortgage deal so much as a grant with repayments. Green Additional Borrowing is priced at 0% over two or five years, with no product fee, on amounts from £5,000 to £20,000 and up to 90% LTV. The condition is absolute: "you must use 100% of what you borrow for 'green purchases'", which Nationwide gives as solar panels, boiler upgrades or insulation. An existing borrower funding insulation out of a further advance at 4.75 times income should check this first.

Two reversion rates, and the 2009 line that decides which one you get

When a Nationwide fix ends the borrower moves to one of two variable rates, and the difference between them is real money. The Standard Mortgage Rate is 6.49% and the Base Mortgage Rate is 5.75%, both effective from 1 January 2026 and both unchanged since.

The BMR carries a promise: it "is guaranteed to be no more than 2% above the Bank of England base rate". With Bank Rate at 3.75% the BMR is sitting exactly on its ceiling. The SMR carries no such promise — Nationwide's own words are that it "has no upper limit or cap" — and at 6.49% it stands 2.74 points above Bank Rate.

Which rate you land on was settled years ago. Nationwide borrowers who reserved on or before 29 April 2009 are on the BMR, as are all former Portman customers, Cheshire members from before 15 June 2009 and Derbyshire members from before 31 May 2009. Anyone who reserved on or after 30 April 2009, every former Dunfermline customer, and anyone who switches to a new mortgage product goes to the SMR. That last clause is the one to read twice: taking a new deal from Nationwide moves a capped BMR borrower permanently onto the uncapped rate.

The size of the gap is the argument against drifting. A Helping Hand borrower on £180,000 over 30 years pays about £1,044 a month on the current 5.69% five-year fix at 95% LTV and £1,137 on the SMR — £93 a month, or £1,116 a year, for doing nothing at the end of a deal. On the older rates that gap was wider; rising fixes have narrowed it, which makes the end-of-deal letter easier to ignore and no less expensive.

Switching deal without advice, and the four-month window

Existing borrowers can take a new Nationwide deal through Mortgage Manager once they have fewer than four months left on a fixed rate; do it earlier and an early repayment charge applies. Borrowers already on a tracker, the SMR or the BMR can switch at any time. Retirement mortgage holders are restricted to the last three months of the deal and cannot do it online at all.

Two features of that route deserve stating together. Nationwide says there is "no affordability check when you switch deal", which is a genuine advantage for anyone whose income has fallen, whose circumstances have changed, or who has gone self-employed since they borrowed. It also says the online journey is execution-only: you must be "comfortable choosing a new rate without any advice". No affordability test and no advice is a fast, cheap route for someone who knows what they want, and nobody at all checking the choice for someone who does not. Free phone and video appointments with an adviser are available for that reason.

The pricing promise is that switcher rates are "as good as, or better than, those available to new customers remortgaging to us", and the 30 September figures bear it out by a wider margin than they did in the summer. A rate switch at up to 60% LTV with a £999 fee is 4.89% over two or three years and 4.97% over five. A new remortgage customer at the same LTV and fee pays 5.09% over two years and 5.11% over five. The existing member is 0.20 and 0.14 points ahead. At 90.01% to 95% LTV the switcher pays 5.69% over two years and 5.64% over five, against 5.74% and 5.69% for a new remortgage. That is the reverse of the loyalty penalty this market was criticised for, and Nationwide deserves the credit for publishing both sets in the same document.

One practical detail has gone missing. Nationwide's switching page used to publish the deadline for cancelling a switch; it now says only that the cancellation deadline sits inside Mortgage Manager, so a borrower cannot find out when it falls without logging in. The other warning in its own guide still stands: "If you want to switch and borrow more at the same time, you could end up paying more if you apply online. Please call us for advice."

Getting out early, and the 10% you are allowed first

Overpayments are generous by the standards of the market. Products reserved from 29 May 2013 allow 10% a year of the original loan amount with no charge; all tracker mortgages reserved from 2 May 2014 are unlimited, as are the SMR and BMR; older products from 1 February 2001 to 28 May 2013 run on a £500 a month allowance instead. Note the base: 10% of the original advance, not of the current balance, which on a loan you have been paying down for years is the more useful of the two definitions. On a £180,000 Helping Hand loan that is £18,000 a year.

Go beyond it and the early repayment charge lands. Nationwide's published ladder is 2% in year one and 1% in year two on a two-year fix; 3%, 2% then 1% across a three-year fix; 5% falling a point a year to 1% across a five-year fix; and 6% for each of the first five years of a ten-year fix, tapering a point a year to 1% by year ten. On a £180,000 loan that is £9,000 to leave a five-year fix in its first year and £10,800 to leave a ten-year one — which is the price of the six-times-income deal if life changes, because Helping Hand is only sold on those two terms. Borrowers who reserved before 13 September 2024 are a separate case: for them Nationwide says only that "ERC details can be found in their mortgage offer", so the published ladder cannot be relied on and the offer document has to be read.

The other charges are modest and published: £20 for a telegraphic transfer at completion, up to £100 plus VAT for a home visit if the account falls into arrears, and £65 for unpaid ground rent. Nationwide charges nothing for an unpaid cheque or an unpaid direct debit, which many lenders still do, and remortgage products include free standard legal work. The minimum it will lend at all is £10,000 except for home improvements.

The size of the lender behind the rate

For the year to 31 March 2026, Nationwide reported £45.8 billion of gross mortgage lending, a 15.6% share of all new lending, and £286.3 billion of balances at a 16.3% share. Total assets were £382.3 billion, underlying profit before tax £2,026 million, statutory profit before tax £1,490 million, and the CET1 capital ratio 19.1%. Arrears of more than three months stood at 0.39% of residential accounts, against a market figure of 1.1% of balances in the Bank of England's Q2 2026 statistics.

The mutual structure shows up in what it gave back: £1.8 billion of member value including a third Fairer Share payment of £0.4 billion, and £1.4 billion of member financial benefit through pricing, down from £1.8 billion the year before. It completed the Part VII transfer of most of Virgin Money's business on 2 April 2026, described in its own results as the largest non-ring-fencing banking business transfer ever made in the UK, and runs 605 Nationwide branches alongside 91 Virgin Money ones.

One number in those results runs against the direction of everything above. Nationwide helped 88,000 first-time buyers into a home in the year to March 2026, against 120,000 the year before — a fall of 26.7% in the same period it was loosening stress rates and extending six-times lending. The wider market moved the other way: Bank of England figures published on 8 September 2026 put gross advances at £77.4 billion in Q2 2026, up 31.7% on the year, with lending above 90% LTV at 8.4%, the highest share since 2008 Q2, and lending at 4.5 times income or more at 46.0% of advances. Nationwide has not explained the gap, and it is a fair question to put to it.

What the complaints return admits, and what it does not

Nationwide publishes its own complaints data every six months, broken down by product, and the latest return still covers 1 October 2025 to 31 March 2026. Home finance — the mortgage line — recorded 6,915 complaints opened at 3.57 per 1,000 accounts. 29.18% were closed within three days and a further 69.96% after three days but within eight weeks, which puts 99.14% inside the eight-week deadline. 56.36% were upheld, and the leading cause was general administration and customer service.

Two readings of that. More than half of mortgage complaints being upheld means most of the people who complained had a point, which is an argument for complaining rather than a reason not to borrow. And fewer than three in ten closing inside three days means a mortgage complaint here is usually not a quick phone call, even though almost all of them are resolved before the regulatory clock runs out.

The same return covers The Mortgage Works separately, and the comparison is useful: 1,477 complaints at 5.19 per 1,000 accounts, 26.64% closed in three days, 72.62% after three days but within eight weeks and 52.08% upheld. The buy-to-let arm generates complaints at almost half as much again per account as the residential book.

A complaint not resolved within eight weeks can go to the Financial Ombudsman Service. For completeness on the regulatory record, the FCA fined Nationwide £44,078,500 on 11 December 2025 over anti-money-laundering control failures between October 2016 and July 2021. That penalty concerns financial crime systems rather than mortgage lending, and reading it as a comment on the mortgage book would be wrong.

Buy-to-let is written by a different company

A landlord searching for a Nationwide buy-to-let mortgage will not find one under that name. Buy-to-let is written by The Mortgage Works, part of the Nationwide group, with its own criteria, its own products and, as the complaints table above shows, its own service record.

Those criteria moved on 3 September 2026. The maximum age at application for a first-time landlord rose from 70 to 75 for lending up to 70% LTV, and experienced landlords applying up to the same 70% now face no maximum age at all. Maximum total borrowing across the whole Nationwide group went from £7.5 million to £10 million, and TMW began accepting purchases of existing buy-to-let properties from family members. Dan Clinton, its Head of Buy-to-Let Mortgages, framed it as lending criteria evolving with landlords' circumstances.

The market context cuts the other way. Buy-to-let was 8.0% of gross advances in Q2 2026 on Bank of England figures, the lowest share since 2024 Q3 and down 1.2 points on the year. Loosening age and borrowing limits into a shrinking share of the market is a bid for the landlords who are still buying, not a sign that many are.

Residential lending into later life is tighter than the buy-to-let arm's. Nationwide's own borrower criteria put the maximum retirement age it will accept at 70, and require the mortgage term to end before the oldest applicant's 75th birthday.

Common questions

What is Nationwide's cheapest mortgage rate?

On the product guide effective 30 September 2026, the cheapest fixed rate is 4.99% — a two-year fix for home movers at up to 60% LTV, with a £1,499 product fee and a minimum loan of £300,000. Below £300,000 the equivalent two-year is 5.04% with a £999 fee, or 5.29% with no fee. The cheapest rate in the range overall is the two-year tracker at 4.09%, which is Bank Rate plus 0.34% with a £999 fee.

How much will Nationwide lend me?

Up to six times income for first-time buyers through Helping Hand, and since 21 January 2026 for home movers and remortgage customers borrowing more. A like-for-like remortgage can go to 6.5 times, or 5.5 times on interest-only. First-time buyers need £30,000 sole or £50,000 joint income; new borrowers using the mover and remortgage route need £75,000 whether applying alone or jointly, while existing members have no minimum. Standard lending is 4.49 times, so on a £30,000 salary the difference is £134,700 against £180,000.

Have Nationwide's mortgage rates gone up or down recently?

Up, since the middle of August 2026. The two-year fix for home movers at up to 60% LTV with a £1,499 fee was 4.48% on the guide effective 18 August and is 4.99% on the guide effective 30 September — 0.51 percentage points across five repricings, with Bank Rate unchanged at 3.75% throughout. Nationwide keeps every superseded rate sheet on its intermediary site, so the movement can be checked product by product.

What rate will I move to when my fixed deal ends?

Almost certainly the Standard Mortgage Rate, which is 6.49% and has been since 1 January 2026. It has no cap. The older Base Mortgage Rate of 5.75% is guaranteed never to exceed Bank Rate plus 2%, but it is closed to anyone who reserved on or after 30 April 2009 and is lost permanently if a BMR borrower takes a new Nationwide product.

Is a Nationwide tracker cheaper than a fix?

At today's Bank Rate, yes, and by a wide margin. The two-year tracker at up to 60% LTV is Bank Rate plus 0.34%, or 4.09%, against 4.99% on the cheapest two-year fix — about £154 a month on a £300,000 loan over 25 years. Trackers also carry no early repayment charge and, where reserved on or after 2 May 2014, can be overpaid without limit. The rate moves with Bank Rate and has no ceiling, so the saving is not guaranteed for the two years.

Can I overpay without being charged?

Yes, up to 10% a year of the original loan amount on any product reserved from 29 May 2013 — £18,000 a year on a £180,000 loan. Tracker mortgages reserved from 2 May 2014, and anything sitting on the SMR or BMR, can be overpaid without limit. Products from 1 February 2001 to 28 May 2013 are capped at £500 a month instead.

When can I switch to a new Nationwide deal?

Once there are fewer than four months left on a fixed rate, or at any time from a tracker, the SMR or the BMR. There is no affordability check, and the online route through Mortgage Manager is execution-only with no advice given. Switcher pricing is better than new-customer remortgage pricing — 4.89% against 5.09% on the two-year 60% LTV product with a £999 fee — but the cancellation deadline is only shown inside Mortgage Manager, and Nationwide advises phoning rather than applying online if you want to borrow more at the same time.

Does Nationwide do buy-to-let mortgages?

Not under the Nationwide name. Buy-to-let is written by The Mortgage Works, a Nationwide group company. From 3 September 2026 its maximum age at application for a first-time landlord rose from 70 to 75 at up to 70% LTV, experienced landlords lending to that level face no maximum age, and maximum total group borrowing rose from £7.5 million to £10 million.
