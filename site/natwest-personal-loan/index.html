NatWest Personal Loan: 7.3% APR, and Why the Reason Matters

Rates and terms checked 21 November 2026 · Loans · Compare100 editorial team

NatWest lends from £1,000 to £50,000 on an unsecured personal loan at 7.3% APR representative, and the money can reach the account the same day: its own pages say you “could get your money today — if you apply and are approved before 5:45pm, Monday to Friday, excluding bank holidays.” The 7.3% applies to loans between £7,500 and £19,950, and the only other rate the bank publishes is a ceiling: “we won’t offer you a rate over 29.9% p.a. (fixed).” Figures read from NatWest’s own loan pages on 21 November 2026.

The lender is National Westminster Bank plc, firm reference number 121878, part of a group holding £435.9bn of customer loans and £448.6bn of deposits at 30 June 2026 and serving, in its own words, “more than 20 million customers throughout the UK”, with Retail Banking alone describing “our customer base of over 19 million”. The rate is fixed for the life of the agreement, no arrangement fee appears anywhere on the loan pages, and the eligibility check comes with a plain promise — “Check your eligibility before you apply - it won’t hurt your credit score.”

Two things are worth knowing before you click, because neither is a criticism and both change the answer. A new customer can borrow up to £35,000; £50,000 is available only to existing customers applying through the app, telephone or Online Banking. And the maximum term is not set by the size of the loan alone but by what you say the money is for: five years on a holiday, eight on a car, ten on home improvements.

Four maximum terms, and the reason you give decides which one you get

Most lenders set the term by the size of the loan. NatWest sets it by the size of the loan and the purpose you declare, and it publishes all four combinations in advance. “Borrowing from £1,000 to £7,450?” you pay it back “in 1 to 5 years.” “Borrowing from £7,500 and £50,000? If it’s not for home improvements:” the answer is “in 1 to 8 years.” Make it home improvements and the same money runs “in 1 to 10 years.” Say it is for a holiday and the rules change again: “Borrowing for a holiday? You could borrow from £1,000 to £25,000:” repayable “in 1 to 5 years.”

On the arithmetic those are different products. Take £10,000 at 7.3% APR. Over five years it costs £198.33 a month and £11,899.57 in total. Over eight years the monthly payment falls to £136.67 but the total rises to £13,120.19. Over the ten years reserved for home improvements it is £116.45 a month and £13,974.22 repaid — £2,074.65 more interest than the five-year version of the identical loan. Those are this page’s own calculations from the published representative rate, not NatWest quotations.

The holiday band is the one that bites hardest, because it caps the term rather than the amount. A £25,000 holiday loan has to be cleared inside five years, which is £495.82 a month at 7.3%; the same £25,000 for a kitchen could run eight years at £341.67 — £154.15 a month of difference on identical borrowing from the same bank. The loan calculator offers eight purposes to choose from: Car (New), Car (Used), Home Improvements, Debt Consolidation, Wedding, Holiday, Household Goods and Other. Which one you pick is a pricing and term decision, and nothing on the pages says so.

£35,000 as a new customer, £50,000 if you already bank there

NatWest will lend to someone who banks elsewhere, which not every high street name does, and it says so plainly: “existing customers could borrow up to £50k if applying via our app, Telephone or Online Banking.” Then, in the next breath, “New customers up to £35k.” The gap is £15,000, and the only thing that closes it is an existing relationship and a particular application channel.

Three other gates sit alongside it, all published. You must “Be 18+, a UK resident”, must “Not have been declared bankrupt in the last six years” and must “Not have applied for a loan with us in the last 28 days.” That last one is unusual and worth reading twice: a declined or abandoned application locks you out of a second attempt for four weeks, so an application made on a whim costs you the option of a considered one. The self-employed face a further requirement — a loan is open to them “providing you are 18+ and a UK resident with a NatWest current account (held for 3+ months)” — so for that group the three-month account history is not a discount, it is the entry ticket.

There is also a cap on how much of your borrowing NatWest will carry at once. “You can now only have 3 loans with us,” the pages say, and “Already got 3 loans with us? You’ll need to pay off a loan before you apply.” The word “now” implies the limit used to be higher. Nothing published says what it was, and nothing published says whether a loan inherited through an acquisition counts towards the three.

Three representative APRs, and all of them are NatWest’s own

A representative APR is not marketing copy. It is the figure consumer credit rules oblige a lender to publish, and it is the number a borrower uses to compare one bank against another. NatWest publishes it three different ways.

The main loans page carries “Representative 7.3% APR”, and so do the car loan page, the debt consolidation page and the explainer page, each with the same qualifier: “This rate is available on loans between £7,500 and £19,950.” The loan FAQ page carries “Representative 7.9% APR” against the identical band and the identical sentence. Both were read twice on 21 November 2026. The page metadata dates the loans page to 13 November 2026 and the FAQ to 9 September 2026, so the likeliest explanation is an older page nobody repointed when the rate moved — which makes 7.3% the live figure and 7.9% a two-month-old one still being served to anyone who arrives at the questions page first.

The third figure is the strangest, and it sits on the same page as the first. The loan calculator and the debt consolidation page both render a worked example of £7,500 borrowed, “Monthly repayments £137.55” and “Total to repay £8,253.00”, with both the representative APR and the fixed interest rate shown as 3.9. Those numbers are internally consistent: £137.55 multiplied by 60 is exactly £8,253.00, and £7,500 repaid that way prices out at 3.898% APR, so the example really is a five-year loan at 3.9%. At the 7.3% printed at the top of the same page, £7,500 over five years costs £148.74 a month and £8,924.68 in total — £11.19 a month and £671.68 more than the example underneath it. The term itself renders as “0 months 0 months”, so the one label that would reconcile the two is missing.

The honest reading is that 7.3% is the regulated number and the 3.9% example is stale template content rather than an offer. Nobody applying will be quoted 3.9%. But a reader cannot know that from the page, and the calculator’s own small print does not help: “This calculator shows you an example. Your actual interest rate might be different — it depends on your circumstances, how much you borrow, and how long for.”

Ten things the money cannot be used for, and the rent is one

NatWest prints its prohibited-use list identically on five separate pages, which is more disclosure than most lenders manage. The money cannot go on “Buying property, a static caravan, or land”, a “Deposit for property, a static caravan, or land”, “Household bills, rent or mortgage payments”, “Car tax”, “Business purposes”, “Late payments”, “Tax avoidance schemes”, “Court or solicitors’ fees”, “Gambling” or “Buying shares or other investment funds.”

Set that list against the eight purposes the calculator lets you select and a line appears in an odd place. Debt Consolidation is an approved purpose and has a product page of its own, so clearing a credit card with borrowed money is fine. “Household bills, rent or mortgage payments” and “Late payments” are barred, so catching up on the mortgage or paying off an arrears balance is not. The distinction is defensible — a lender has no business funding somebody’s rent on credit, and the regulator would say the same — but it is the one borrower who most often goes looking for a loan, and the pages never explain the reasoning. Car tax is the other surprise, given that two of the eight purposes are cars.

A debt consolidation loan, and the free statement it competes with

Credit where it is due: NatWest’s debt consolidation page carries its own warnings rather than burying them. “Taking on new debt is a big decision”, it says, before adding that “your debt consolidation loan may not be enough to cover all your debts” and that “You may have to pay an early repayment charge.” That is three cautions on a sales page, and the page still leads with the thing a reader wants: “You could pay less each month with just one simple payment.”

What no lender’s page mentions is the document the free alternative runs on. The Standard Financial Statement is described by the Money and Pensions Service, which “has a statutory responsibility to improve the quality, consistency, and availability of debt advice services across the UK”, as “a universal income and expenditure statement, together with a single set of spending guidelines”. It was designed with the lenders themselves: the pre-launch governance group included Royal Bank of Scotland — NatWest Group’s own sister brand — alongside Citizens Advice, StepChange Debt Charity, the Finance and Leasing Association and the Insolvency Service. A borrower who goes to free debt advice instead of a consolidation loan is assessed on that form, and the creditor is expected to accept it.

The borrower cannot read it. The site states that the statement is “not intended for the public to use” and that organisations need “a membership number to gain access to the related materials and guidance”. The current figures, listed in the navigation as Spending Guidelines 2026/27, sit behind a sign-in gate that returned only “This page contains gated resources that require login through our secure authentication system” on 21 November 2026. So the monthly allowances against which an offer of forbearance would be judged are not public, which is worth knowing before treating a consolidation loan as the only route.

One number makes the comparison concrete. The Bank of England’s Money and Credit release of 29 October 2026 puts the effective rate on interest-charging credit cards at 21.71% in September. NatWest’s 29.9% ceiling is 8.19 percentage points above that, so a consolidation loan priced at the top of its range is dearer than the average card balance it was taken out to clear. At the 7.3% representative rate it is barely a third of the card rate. The spread between those two outcomes is the whole decision, and the only one of the two figures NatWest advertises is the good one.

Fifty-eight days, twenty-eight days, and the taper nobody advertises

Paying a NatWest loan off early costs money, and the bank devotes a page to saying so. “Yes, but you’ll have to pay an early repayment charge (ERC),” it opens. “Did you borrow for over 12 months? You’ll pay 58 days’ interest as an ERC.” “Did you borrow for 12 months? You’ll pay 28 days’ interest as an ERC.” The charge is “in addition to your outstanding loan amount and any outstanding interest.”

The 58 days is not NatWest’s invention. The right to settle early comes from the Consumer Credit Act 1974, and the Consumer Credit (Early Settlement) Regulations 2004 let a lender assume settlement 28 days after notice and defer it by a further 30 where the agreement runs longer than a year. Every mainstream lender takes the whole allowance, so what matters is not that NatWest charges it but what it amounts to. On £5,000 outstanding at 7.3%, 58 days’ interest is £58.00 and the 12-month version £28.00. On £10,000 at 7.3% it is £116.00. At the 29.9% ceiling on £10,000 it is £475.12. Tens of pounds on a mid-sized loan at the advertised rate; a few hundred at the top of the range.

The part worth praising is the end of the term, where NatWest publishes something most lenders leave to a phone call. “Have you got 58 days, or fewer, left on your loan?” it asks, and answers: “We’ll calculate your interest based on the exact number of days left.” “So, if you’ve got 20 days left on your loan, we’ll charge you 20 days’ interest.” On £10,000 at 7.3% that is £40.00 rather than £116.00. Nothing is hidden there, and the taper is real.

Overpaying without closing the account is treated separately: “you can make an extra payment in addition to your normal monthly loan repayment”, after which NatWest says it will be in touch within a few days with how the payment “could change your monthly payment or the length of your loan.” Which of the two it changes is not stated on the page, and on most agreements that choice is worth more than the ERC.

Two ways to borrow more, and one of them charges you to leave

NatWest sets out both routes rather than steering you to one. Option one, “Replace your existing loan(s)”, means “Get one bigger loan that pays off your existing NatWest loan(s) - and gives you the extra money you need.” The page states both consequences in its own words: “You’ll have a new interest rate, which might be more than the interest on your existing loan” and “You’ll need to pay an early repayment fee. This will be included in the amount needed to pay off your old loan.”

So a top-up by replacement reprices the whole balance, not just the new money, and the charge for closing the old agreement is folded into the figure you borrow — which means you pay interest on it for the life of the new loan. On a £10,000 balance at 7.3%, the £116.00 of early repayment interest carried into a fresh five-year loan costs about £138 by the time it is repaid, on this page’s arithmetic.

Option two, “Apply for a separate loan”, avoids all of that: “You’ll keep your existing NatWest loan – which will stay the same, with the same interest rate”, and “Each loan will have its own interest rate and a different number of years to pay it back over.” The cost is a slot: a second loan uses one of the three the bank allows, and the second loan is priced on today’s rates and today’s view of you. Where the original rate is below the current representative APR, option two is plainly the cheaper answer, and NatWest publishes enough to let a reader work that out.

The loan taken out with Sainsbury’s Bank is a NatWest loan now

A good many people hold a NatWest loan without ever having applied to NatWest. The bank announced on 20 June 2024 that it had agreed “to acquire certain retail banking assets and liabilities of Sainsbury’s Bank which comprises its outstanding credit card, unsecured personal loans and savings accounts.” The mechanism was not a sale of a company but a statutory transfer of the agreements themselves — “a banking business transfer scheme under Part VII of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000” — which requires a judge rather than a customer’s consent.

The dates are on the record. Objections were invited by 8 April 2025, the hearing was listed at the Rolls Building in London for 15 April 2025, and Sainsbury’s own transfer page now states that “legal ownership of certain credit cards, loans and savings accounts transferred to NatWest on 1 May 2025” after “The High Court approved the transfer in April 2025.” The accounts themselves moved later: terms changed on 3 October 2025 for credit cards, 31 October 2025 for loans and 6 November 2025 for savings, and the page confirms that “Certain credit card, loan and savings accounts have now moved on to NatWest systems.”

For an existing borrower the reassurance is specific and limited. “The overall amount of interest charged for personal loans will be the same as long as you pay your loan in line with your loan agreement,” the transfer page says, and customers were told “there’s nothing you need to do right now to continue using and accessing your account(s) as you do today.” Read closely, that is a promise about contractual interest and not about everything else: the same page warned of changes “to reflect the way NatWest operates and looks after your account(s)”, delivered by a notice of variation “sent at least 60 days before any changes are made”, and told some customers they “may need to make changes so your money continues to be protected by the FSCS.”

One figure is missing from both sides. Neither NatWest’s own transfer page nor Sainsbury’s states how many accounts moved or what balances came with them, and NatWest Group’s interim results of 31 July 2026 do not mention Sainsbury’s at all in the sections that could be read — they record the completion of a different acquisition, Evelyn Partners, on 30 June. The scale of the loan book NatWest bought is not something a reader can establish from the primary documents, and it is not stated here.

The letters the law makes a lender send, and what silence costs it

Nothing on NatWest’s loan pages describes what happens if a payment is missed, which is a gap rather than a fault: the answer is in statute and it applies to every regulated fixed-sum loan in the country. The Consumer Credit Act 1974 was last updated for changes in force on or before 11 November 2026, and section 86B is the part a borrower in difficulty should know about.

The duty bites once the shortfall reaches “the sum of the last two payments” on a monthly agreement (four on a weekly one). The lender then has to send a notice of sums in arrears “within the period of 14 days beginning with the day on which the conditions mentioned in subsection (1) are satisfied”, and keep sending them “at intervals of not more than six months”. Each one must carry “a copy of the current arrears information sheet under section 86A”, which is the regulator’s own plain-English guidance rather than the bank’s.

What makes those deadlines more than paperwork is section 86D. Miss them and “The creditor or owner shall not be entitled to enforce the agreement during the period of non-compliance”, and the borrower “shall have no liability to pay” either “any sum of interest to the extent calculated by reference to the period of non-compliance” or any default sum that would have fallen due in it. Interest stops running for as long as the letters do not arrive.

Charges have their own clock. Under section 86E a notice of default sums must go out within a prescribed period, the lender “shall not be entitled to enforce the agreement until the notice is given”, and there is no liability for interest on that charge calculated “by reference to a period occurring before the 29th day after the day on which the debtor or hirer is given the notice”. Section 86F(2) then limits what can be charged on it at all: “The debtor or hirer shall only be liable to pay interest in connection with the default sum if the interest is simple interest.” None of this appears on a sales page, at NatWest or anywhere else, and all of it is free to read.

7,428 lending complaints, and a reporting period that cannot be right

National Westminster Bank plc publishes its own complaints return, covering the bank and National Westminster Home Loans Limited. The line a personal loan grievance lands in is “Credit related”, and it ran at 7,428 complaints opened and 7,979 closed, at a rate of 6.88 per 1,000 loan accounts, with 52% upheld.

Two things stand out when that is set beside the rest of the return. The rate is high: banking and credit cards ran at 2.74 complaints per 1,000 accounts, home finance at 4.60 per 1,000 balances outstanding, investments at 1.71 per 1,000 client accounts and insurance at 0.84 per 1,000 policies. Lending draws complaints two and a half times as often per account as the bank’s current accounts and cards do. But the uphold rate is 52% against 55% on banking and credit cards, which is the opposite of the pattern most lenders’ returns show, where credit complaints are disputed decisions and are mostly rejected. At NatWest, more than half of the people who complain about a loan are found to have a point.

The gaps are real, though. Both speed columns — headed “% closed within 3 days” and “% closed after 3 days but within 8 weeks” — read N/A on the Credit related line, as does the main cause column, so the return says nothing at all about how quickly a loan complaint is handled or what it was about. Every other product line in the same table carries both figures.

And the dates do not work. The return gives its “Current reporting period” as “01 January 2025 to 30 June 2026” — eighteen months — while describing the same data in the narrative as “In H1 2026”, and it gives the “Previous Reporting Period” as “01 July 2025 to 31 December 2025”, which sits inside the current one. One of those two labels is wrong, and a reader cannot tell which. The same page has a smaller inconsistency beside it: the narrative says “For every 1,000 home finance loans that we had outstanding, we received five complaints” where the table for the same line says 4.60, with nothing reconciling them. One sum the published figures invite is worth doing but not trusting — 7,428 complaints at 6.88 per 1,000 implies roughly 1.08 million loan accounts, which is this page’s own division rather than a NatWest figure, and the return never publishes the denominator or defines what counts as a loan account.

Where 7.3% sits, and the book it comes out of

An APR means little without a market to measure it against, and the Bank of England publishes one free every month. Its Money and Credit release of 29 October 2026 reports that “The effective rate on new personal loans to individuals decreased to 9.85% in September from 9.96% in August.” NatWest’s 7.3% is 2.55 percentage points below what lenders as a whole were actually charging; its 29.9% ceiling is 20.05 points above it. The same release puts interest-charging overdrafts at 20.79%, “increased by 52 basis points”, and notes that “Net borrowing of consumer credit by individuals decreased to £1.9 billion in September, from £2.7 billion in August” while the annual growth rate for all consumer credit “increased slightly to 9.8% in September, from 9.7% in August.”

The lender behind the rate is large and the loan book inside it is not. NatWest Group reported £435.9bn of customer loans and £448.6bn of deposits at 30 June 2026, operating profit before tax of £4,318m for the half year against £3,585m a year earlier, a net interest margin of 2.48%, a CET1 ratio of 13.2% and a return on tangible equity of 19.7%. Unsecured personal lending is not broken out, but Retail Banking reported “£0.2 billion, or 2.1%, higher personal advances” in the half, which implies a personal advances book of roughly £9.5bn — about 2% of the group’s lending, on this page’s arithmetic rather than a published figure. A small book inside a big bank is the straightforward reason a headline rate can sit comfortably under the market average.

Independent scoring is thinner. Smart Money People rates the NatWest personal loan 4.49 out of 5 from 155 reviews and labels it Good, where the brand as a whole is rated 4.73 from 24,013 product reviews and labelled Excellent, with the note that “Reviews for NatWest have trended negatively over the past 90 days.” The loan scores below the brand and below most of the bank’s accounts; it scores above its business banking, home insurance and junior savings. Defaqto does not rate personal loan ranges. One last thing a borrower should know before typing a web address: the FCA has a standing clone-firm warning naming natwestrefund.com, first published 27 February 2024 and last updated 2 July 2024, against the genuine “NATIONAL WESTMINSTER BANK PUBLIC LIMITED COMPANY”, firm reference number 121878.

Where it wins

  • A published ceiling of 29.9% a year fixed, so the worst case is knowable before you apply rather than after
  • Money the same day where the application is approved and signed before 5:45pm, Monday to Friday
  • Up to ten years to repay where the money is for home improvements, against eight years on any other purpose and five below £7,500
  • The early repayment charge tapers inside the last 58 days and is charged on the actual days remaining — 20 days left, 20 days’ interest
  • A second loan can run alongside the first, leaving the original rate and term untouched
  • No arrangement fee or monthly fee named anywhere on the loan pages
  • The loan amount bands, the term bands and the full prohibited-use list are all published before you apply
  • More than half of lending complaints are upheld — 52% — which is a higher strike rate than most lenders’ credit lines show
  • The debt consolidation page carries three of its own warnings, including that the loan “may not be enough to cover all your debts”
  • An eligibility check NatWest says “won’t hurt your credit score” returns a personal rate before any formal application

  • Two NatWest pages give different representative APRs for the same £7,500 to £19,950 band — 7.3% on the loans page and 7.9% on the loan FAQ page — both read twice on 21 November 2026
  • The worked example served on the loan calculator and the debt consolidation page prices £7,500 at 3.9%, £137.55 a month and £8,253.00 in total, which is £671.68 cheaper over five years than the 7.3% printed at the top of the same page
  • The term in that example renders as “0 months 0 months”, so the one figure that would reconcile it with the headline rate is not shown
  • The 7.3% covers £7,500 to £19,950 of a product sold from £1,000 to £50,000, and the only other rate published is the 29.9% ceiling — a 22.6-point spread with no rate table behind it
  • A new customer is capped at £35,000 where an existing customer can borrow £50,000, and only through the app, telephone or Online Banking

Common questions

What APR will I actually pay on a NatWest personal loan?

The representative 7.3% APR applies to loans between £7,500 and £19,950, and NatWest says only that “Other loan amounts are available at alternative rates.” No rate table is published for anything outside that band. The one firm limit is the ceiling: “we won’t offer you a rate over 29.9% p.a. (fixed).” On £10,000 over five years the difference between those two rates is £198.33 a month against £302.05, and £11,899.57 repaid against £18,123.29 — £6,223.72 of extra interest on the same loan. The only way to find out which end you sit at is the eligibility check, which NatWest says “won’t hurt your credit score”. Note that its loan FAQ page was still showing a representative 7.9% APR for the same band on 21 November 2026.

How long can I borrow a NatWest loan for?

It depends on both the amount and the purpose. From £1,000 to £7,450 the term is one to five years. From £7,500 to £50,000 it is one to eight years, rising to one to ten years where the money is for home improvements. A holiday loan of any size from £1,000 to £25,000 is one to five years. Longer is not automatically better: £10,000 at 7.3% costs £198.33 a month over five years and £11,899.57 in total, against £116.45 a month over ten years and £13,974.22 in total — £2,074.65 more interest for the lower payment.

Does NatWest charge you to pay a loan off early?

Yes. “Did you borrow for over 12 months? You’ll pay 58 days’ interest as an ERC,” the bank says, falling to 28 days’ interest where the loan was taken over 12 months. On £5,000 outstanding at 7.3% that is £58.00; on £10,000 at 7.3% it is £116.00; at the 29.9% ceiling on £10,000 it is £475.12. The allowance comes from the Consumer Credit (Early Settlement) Regulations 2004 rather than from NatWest. Inside the final stretch the charge tapers: “if you’ve got 20 days left on your loan, we’ll charge you 20 days’ interest.” Making an extra payment without closing the account is treated differently, and NatWest will tell you afterwards whether it has cut the payment or the term.

Can I get a NatWest loan if I bank somewhere else?

Yes, but for less. “New customers up to £35k,” the pages say, where “existing customers could borrow up to £50k if applying via our app, Telephone or Online Banking.” You must be 18 or over and a UK resident, must not have been declared bankrupt in the last six years, and must not have applied for a NatWest loan in the previous 28 days. The exception is self-employment: a self-employed applicant needs “a NatWest current account (held for 3+ months)”. No more than three NatWest loans can be held at one time.

What can a NatWest loan not be used for?

Ten uses are barred and the list is printed identically on five of its pages: buying property, a static caravan or land; a deposit for any of those; “Household bills, rent or mortgage payments”; car tax; business purposes; “Late payments”; tax avoidance schemes; “Court or solicitors’ fees”; gambling; and buying shares or other investment funds. Debt consolidation is allowed and has a product page of its own, so clearing a credit card is fine while catching up on arrears or rent is not.

My loan was with Sainsbury’s Bank. Is it a NatWest loan now?

Yes. NatWest announced the purchase on 20 June 2024 and the agreements were moved by “a banking business transfer scheme under Part VII of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000”, approved at a High Court hearing listed for 15 April 2025. Sainsbury’s own page states that “legal ownership of certain credit cards, loans and savings accounts transferred to NatWest on 1 May 2025”. Accounts moved onto NatWest systems later in the year, with loan terms changing on 31 October 2025. On the rate, the promise is narrow but specific: “The overall amount of interest charged for personal loans will be the same as long as you pay your loan in line with your loan agreement.” Other terms could change by a notice of variation “sent at least 60 days before any changes are made”.

What happens if I miss a NatWest loan payment?

The loan pages do not say, but the Consumer Credit Act 1974 does and it applies to every regulated fixed-sum loan. Once the arrears reach “the sum of the last two payments”, section 86B requires a notice of sums in arrears “within the period of 14 days” and repeat notices “at intervals of not more than six months”, each carrying the statutory arrears information sheet. If the lender misses those deadlines, section 86D says it “shall not be entitled to enforce the agreement during the period of non-compliance” and the borrower has no liability for interest calculated by reference to that period. Interest on any default charge cannot run “before the 29th day after” notice of it is given, and under section 86F(2) it can only ever be simple interest.

Our verdict

NatWest is a straightforward unsecured lender for the middle of its own range: £7,500 to £19,950 at 7.3% APR representative is 2.55 points below the 9.85% the Bank of England says lenders were actually charging in September 2026, the money can arrive the same day, and there is no arrangement fee to find. It is at its best on a home improvement loan, where the term runs to ten years rather than the eight allowed on any other purpose. Outside that band the picture is thinner: the only other rate published is a 29.9% ceiling, a new customer is held to £35,000, and a holiday is capped at five years however much you borrow. Check which of the four term bands your purpose falls into before applying, and read the representative APR off the main loans page rather than the questions page, because on 21 November 2026 the two did not agree.
