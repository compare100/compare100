Next Children's Fashion

Rates and terms checked 21 November 2026 · Children's Fashion · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Next will carry a children's order to any of its 458 UK stores for nothing, the next day, with no minimum spend, and take the unwanted half of it back at the same counter for nothing as well. Delivery to a home address is £4.95 an order, to an Evri ParcelShop £3.50, and you get 28 days to change your mind rather than the 14 the law requires. In the year to January 2026 10.4 million UK customers ordered something, and group sales reached £7,004m.

The childrenswear itself runs from preterm and first size up to 17 to 18 years, with schoolwear filtered out as a range of its own, and Next scored 83% in Which?'s baby shops survey — fieldwork July and August 2025, 2,317 respondents, category average 80% — placing it above every other national chain in that table. Orders placed before 11pm reach most stores the following day, and a store parcel sits waiting for 10 days.

One structural fact is worth knowing before you start, because it is unusual for a clothing retailer and it is entirely in the shopper's favour on the day the parcel is wrong: the business that takes your money is the business that sells the clothes. Next Retail Limited operates the website, supplies the goods and runs the credit account behind them, so there is one company, one contract and one phone number rather than a marketplace seller standing between you and the shop. That same company is an authorised lender, and its pay in 3 option charges 0% interest if each of the three payments lands in full and on time.

What the order costs before you count the clothes

A children's basket is small and it comes back often, which makes the charges round the outside of it matter more than they would on a sofa. Next's are published in its terms and they are flat. Home delivery is “£4.95 per order for one person delivery items”, and the terms say plainly that “You will only pay a £4.95 delivery charge for each order regardless of how many items you order”. There is no basket value that switches it off: the help centre's answer to the question is “No. There is no minimum spend.” Delivery to a store is free, with one exception the terms disclose up front — “At times of peak trade, we may charge 25p for the Next Day To Store service”.

Returns run the same way. Taking a parcel into a store costs nothing “except our airport stores”; handing it to a courier, dropping it at an Evri ParcelShop or posting it is “charged at £2.50 per return/collection”, once per collection however many jumpers are in the bag. Return the whole order and “the £4.95 delivery charge will be refunded”. So the worst case on an order you send back in full is the £2.50, and the realistic case on an order you keep half of is £4.95 out and £2.50 back — £7.45 of carriage on a basket that might be two school polo shirts.

That is the arithmetic the nextunlimited subscription is built on, and the claim Next makes for it stands up. At £24.50 for twelve months it is, in the company's own words, “less than the cost of 5 standard deliveries” — five home deliveries come to £24.75, so the subscription wins by 25p on the fifth order of the year, or on the seventh if you have your parcels sent to a ParcelShop at £3.50. The £2.04 a month it quotes is the right division of £24.50 by twelve. Renewal is automatic on the anniversary with 28 days' notice by email, and the returns charge is untouched: “The £2.50 returns charge will apply for each collection/return, regardless of the number of items you return.”

Two deadlines are worth writing down. A store parcel is collectable “after 12 noon” on the stated day and anything “not collected within 10 days of the collection date will be cancelled”. And the 28-day return window does not simply lapse — “Items returned after 28 days will receive a refund in the form of an eVoucher”, which is store credit rather than money back. Next also reserves the right to “charge an investigation fee of no less than £35”, and on large homeware and furniture a collection costs £30, rising “to £60 if you return your item/s 15-30 days after the day of delivery”.

The account behind the basket

Next has sold clothes on its own credit since the Directory days, and the arrangement is unusual enough to be worth reading properly: the shop is the lender. The credit agreement is “made between Next Retail Limited” trading as Next Online, the same company number 4521150 that supplies the clothes, “authorised and regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority … under the Consumer Credit Act 1974”. Two products sit on one account. nextpay is a revolving account at “Representative 24.9% APR variable. Interest rate 24.9% p.a. variable” on an “Assumed credit limit £1,200”. pay in 3 splits an order across three statements and charges nothing at all provided, as Next puts it, “you pay each ‘pay in 3’ payment in full and on time”.

The mechanics reward anybody who clears the balance and punish anybody who drifts. Payment “is due 7 days prior to your next statement (which are one calendar month apart)”. The minimum is “the higher of £5 or 5% of your Account balance … rounded up to the nearest £1” on nextpay and to the nearest penny on pay in 3, and where the balance is under £5 the minimum becomes the whole of it. Interest on a purchase starts, for a parcel sent to a home, “from the day after we expect you to receive the items” rather than from the day you open the box. Miss two minimum payments and “you'll be asked to pay a late payment fee of £7.50 and the whole balance on your account will become chargeable” at the Standard Rate of 34.9% a year. There is a 14-day right to withdraw, and Next undertakes to give “at least 30 days' written notice of any increase in the interest rate”.

One clause deserves to be read twice, because it decides how much a late payer actually pays. Next's own explanation of its interest charge says it is calculated “on any opening balance, regardless of the value of any payments made before it is produced”. Pay £400 off a £500 statement balance the day after the statement is cut and the month's interest is still worked out on the £500.

To its credit, Next publishes the sum rather than leaving a shopper to do it. Its own table for a £100 order gives £100 in one month at no interest; £34.60 a month for three months, £103.80 in total and £3.80 of interest; £17.84 for six months, £107.00 and £7.00; and £9.43 for twelve months, £113.12 and £13.12. So a year's credit on a £100 order of school uniform costs 13.1% of the order. The monthly figures are rounded to the penny and the twelve of them add to £113.16 rather than the £113.12 printed beside them, a 4p artefact of that rounding and not a charge.

Pay in 3 went from 29.9% to 34.9%, and the shareholders got the arithmetic first

On 26 October 2026 the headline rate on pay in 3 rose. Next's own notice is clear about the size and the scope of it: “The APR will be increasing from the current rate of 29.9% to 34.9% p.a variable”, applied “automatically on your first statement after 26th October 2026”, and — the line that matters — “The new APR applies for all items and balance on the account at the point it takes effect.” A customer who wanted out had “until 25th October 2026 to let us know”. A rate rise that reaches money already spent is lawful and normal in revolving credit, and it is still the single most consequential thing on this page for anybody carrying a balance.

What makes it checkable rather than a matter of opinion is that the same change is costed in the half-year results of 17 September 2026, five weeks before it took effect. There Next told the market it planned “to increase the headline APR on ‘pay in 3’ credit accounts from 29.9% to 34.9% for existing customers”, and that the change “is expected to add c.£4m of interest income annually, of which c.£1m falls within the current financial year”. The trading statement of 5 November 2026 then forecast that “Growth in NEXT Finance interest income is expected to increase in Q4, to +3.0%, due to the increase in APR on ‘pay-in-3’ credit accounts”, against +0.9% in the first half and +1.2% in the third quarter. The customer notice gives the date; the investor documents give the value.

The two accounts of who it hits do not quite agree, and both are Next's own words. The body of the half-year statement says the increase is “for existing customers”, while its own footnote says “The new APR rate of 34.9% applied to new customers recruited from mid-July 2026.” Neither reading is unreasonable — a staged rollout would produce exactly that pair of sentences — but a shopper cannot tell from the documents which group they fall into, and the only safe assumption is the higher number. Note also that 34.9% is already the Standard Rate quoted on Next's credit page as the rate a nextpay balance falls to if the account goes wrong, so the two products have converged at the top.

A third of the customers, nearly two thirds of the money

The scale of this is not a side business. In the year to January 2026 Next's own annual report counts 3.3 million UK customers paying on a Next credit account out of 10.4 million UK customers altogether — 31.7% of the people — and those 3.3 million spent £1,787m of the £2,800m UK total, which is 63.8% of the money. Sales per credit customer were £547 against £269 across all UK customers, so a shopper with an account is worth almost exactly twice one without.

The lending itself closed the year at £1,340m of customer receivables, up 2.0%, on average balances of £1,284m, and produced £301m of interest income. That is a yield of about 23.4% on the average book, a little under the 24.9% nextpay headline, and Next explains the gap itself: interest income grew only 0.2% because pay in 3 balances “generate less interest than ‘nextpay’ accounts”. NEXT Finance made £175m before a £20m release of historical bad-debt provisions and £195m after it, against group profit before tax of £1,158m — about a sixth of what the whole company earned. The bad debt charge was £22m, “£6m lower than last year”, the default rate 2.2% against 2.6%, and debtor days fell from 194 to 187.

The first half of the current year, to July 2026, shows the same book growing and earning slightly less: receivables £1,309m, interest income £152m, bad debt £10m, no provision release at all against £10m a year earlier, and profit of £90m against £101m. Default rates were restated onto a gross basis at 2.1% for 2025/26 with 2.1% forecast for 2026/27, and the company carries a provision for future defaults of 6.9% of gross receivables. Full-year guidance in September was “around £180m” of NEXT Finance profit on a book of “around £1.36bn”. For a children's clothing shopper the useful reading of all that is simple: the terms on this account are not an afterthought to the retail business, they are a sixth of its profit, and they are priced accordingly.

Five children's recalls sit on the government's own register

The Office for Product Safety and Standards publishes every UK recall and alert at gov.uk/product-safety-alerts-reports-recalls, free, searchable and quite separate from anything a retailer chooses to say about itself. Searching it for Next returns 39 reports, of which five are children's items sold by the chain: Next Kids Patent T Bar Bow Shoes, reference 2305-0061, on 23 May 2023; Children's Wellington Boots sold by Next on 7 July 2023; Girls Parachute Trousers in stone and khaki, 2401-0076, on 9 February 2024; Next 3 Pack Footless Yellow/Blue Zip Sleep Suits, 2406-0059, on 18 June 2024, the only one of the five carrying a stated risk level, recorded as “Low”; and Miss summer dresses and outfit sets sold by Next and Matalan, 2507-0080 and 2507-0076, on 21 July 2025.

Read that in both directions, because one direction on its own is misleading. Five recalls in two and a half years across a range running from preterm to 17 years is not a safety record to be alarmed by, and a recall is evidence that a business can trace its own stock and reach the people who bought it — the shops that cannot do that never appear on the register at all. The honest caveat is the other half: four of the five listings record the risk level as “Not provided”, so the register tells a parent that something was withdrawn without telling them how serious it was, and the individual alert pages are the place to find out.

What it does give a parent is a free check that costs nothing and takes a minute, which is worth more on children's clothing than on most categories: cords, drawstrings, poppers and small fastenings are the recurring reasons childrenswear gets withdrawn, and the register is searchable by brand name before a hand-me-down goes back in the drawer.

Where Next's own pages disagree with each other

Several numbers on this page exist in more than one version on next.co.uk, and since every one of them is the retailer's own publication the fair thing is to print the pair and say which document is which. Furniture delivery is the clearest: the terms say two-person items “are charged at £30.00 per delivery”, the nextunlimited help article agrees at “Standard price £30”, the sale delivery article says “Furniture Delivery - £15.00” and an image caption inside the nextunlimited article says “Cost for 2 person delivery is £20.00.” Three prices, one service. The same sale article still advertises the subscription at “For only £22.50”, which the terms date as superseded: “the Nextunlimited annual subscription increased from £22.50 to £24.50 on the 14th May 2026”.

The credit page carries a quieter version of the same thing. Its body gives nextpay at “Representative 24.9% APR variable” while the page's own search-results description reads “Representative 29.9% APR variable. Credit subject to status.” A searcher comparing credit offers from a results page is reading a rate five points above the one the page itself quotes, which is the kind of stale tag that goes unnoticed for years.

One absence is worth naming as well. Next states on its terms page, its head office page, its credit page and its credit complaints leaflet that it is “authorised and regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority for consumer credit”, and its annual report repeats that “Both NEXT credit offers are authorised and regulated by the FCA”, but no firm reference number appears on any of them. The claim is almost certainly right; it is simply not checkable from the page a customer is reading, and a six-digit number would make it so. The complaints route, by contrast, is published properly and in full: a call to 0333 777 8000 option 5 or an email to complaints@next.co.uk, resolution by the contact centre “usually … within 3 business days”, escalation to a Complaint Resolution Management Team that acknowledges “within 5 business days”, a final response “in most cases … within 8 weeks”, and then the Financial Ombudsman Service at Exchange Tower, London E14 9SR.

The one thing this page cannot tell you is what a cardigan costs. Next's listing pages return “You need to enable JavaScript to run this app” to anything reading the page as text, so no price survives into the fetched document, and the shop's own robots.txt disallows the /shop/ paths and /shop/product/ paths where the prices live. Every figure above is taken from Next's terms, its help centre, its credit documents, its published accounts or the government's recall register — not one of them from a price list, because there was no price list to read.

Where it wins

  • Free next-day delivery to any of 458 Next stores with no minimum spend, and free returns at the same counter in every store except the airport ones
  • 28 days to return an online order rather than the 14 the Consumer Contracts Regulations require, counted from the day after delivery
  • One flat £4.95 home delivery charge whatever the size of the order, so a big back-to-school basket is carried on the same terms as a single vest
  • nextunlimited genuinely is cheaper than five home deliveries — £24.50 against £24.75 — and Next publishes the comparison itself rather than leaving it to be worked out
  • pay in 3 costs 0% if the three payments land in full and on time, and Next prints the cost of every other option: £3.80 over three months, £7.00 over six and £13.12 over twelve on a £100 order
  • The shop and the lender are one company, 4521150, so a wrong parcel and a wrong statement are the same phone call rather than two
  • A complaints procedure published in full with named stages, a 3-business-day first attempt, a 5-business-day acknowledgement, an 8-week final response and the Ombudsman named at the end
  • Which? ranked Next at 83% in its baby shops survey, above the 80% category average, on fieldwork in July and August 2025 with 2,317 respondents

Where it falls short

  • No order value makes home delivery free. The £4.95 applies to a £10 basket and a £300 one alike, the help centre confirms “there is no minimum spend”, and the only way to remove it is to buy a £24.50 annual subscription — which rose from £22.50 on 14 May 2026
  • A courier, post or Evri ParcelShop return is £2.50 every time, and nextunlimited does not cover it, so the common children's pattern of ordering two sizes and sending one back costs £7.45 of carriage unless you can get to a store
  • The pay in 3 rate rose from 29.9% to 34.9% a year from 26 October 2026 and, in Next's own words, “applies for all items and balance on the account at the point it takes effect” — a rate rise that reaches money already spent, with an opt-out window that closed on 25 October 2026
  • Interest is worked out “on any opening balance, regardless of the value of any payments made before it is produced”, so paying most of a statement off early in the month does not reduce that month's charge
  • Miss two minimum payments and a £7.50 default charge is added and the whole balance becomes chargeable at 34.9%; the credit agreement also allows an investigation charge “of no less than £35” and up to £35 of liability on a lost store card
  • Return an item on day 29 and the refund comes back as an eVoucher rather than money, and a store parcel left uncollected for more than 10 days is cancelled and sent back to the warehouse
  • Next's own pages contradict each other on prices a shopper would act on: furniture delivery is quoted at £15.00, £20.00 and £30.00 in three different places, the sale page still advertises the superseded £22.50 subscription, and the credit page's search description says “Representative 29.9% APR” while the page itself says 24.9%
  • Four FCA authorisation statements across the terms, head office page, credit page and complaints leaflet, and not one firm reference number among them, so a customer cannot look the permission up from what Next publishes
  • None of the childrenswear prices could be verified for this page: the listing pages serve no prices to anything reading them as text, and Next's robots.txt disallows the /shop/ and /shop/product/ paths where they sit

Common questions

Is delivery free at Next?

To a store, yes — free, next day on orders placed before 11pm, with no minimum spend, although Next reserves the right to charge 25p for next-day-to-store “at times of peak trade”. To a home address it is £4.95 per order however much you spend, and £3.50 to an Evri ParcelShop. There is no basket value that removes the charge; the only way to waive it is nextunlimited at £24.50 for twelve months, which Next describes as “equivalent to £2.04/month”.

How long do I have to send children's clothes back?

28 days from the day after the goods were delivered, which is longer than the 14-day statutory cancellation period. Store returns are free in all but the airport stores. A courier collection, a post return or an Evri ParcelShop drop-off costs £2.50 per return regardless of how many items are in it, and return the whole order and the £4.95 delivery charge is refunded. After 28 days the refund is issued as an eVoucher.

What interest does a Next account charge?

nextpay is advertised at a representative 24.9% APR variable, with an interest rate of 24.9% a year variable on an assumed credit limit of £1,200. pay in 3 charges nothing if each of the three payments is made in full and on time, and 34.9% a year variable otherwise. 34.9% is also the Standard Rate a nextpay balance moves to if the account falls into arrears. Clearing the statement balance in full each month avoids interest altogether.

Did the pay in 3 rate really change?

Yes. Next's notice says the APR increased “from the current rate of 29.9% to 34.9% p.a variable”, applied automatically on the first statement after 26 October 2026, with an opt-out deadline of 25 October 2026, and that it “applies for all items and balance on the account at the point it takes effect”. Next told the market on 17 September 2026 that the change would add about £4m of interest income a year. One qualification: the body of that statement says the rise is for existing customers while its own footnote says the 34.9% “applied to new customers recruited from mid-July 2026”, and the two cannot both describe everybody.

Who do I complain to if something goes wrong with the account?

Next's credit complaints leaflet gives 0333 777 8000 option 5, complaints@next.co.uk, or Next Retail Ltd, Gedding Road, Leicester LE5 5DW. Complaints handled by the contact centre are “usually resolved within 3 business days”; anything escalated goes to a Complaint Resolution Management Team which acknowledges it “within 5 business days”, with a final response “in most cases … within 8 weeks”. After that, or after eight weeks without one, the Financial Ombudsman Service at Exchange Tower, London E14 9SR, 0800 023 4567, can take it on.

Our verdict

For the way children's clothes are actually bought — small orders, often, with half of each one going back — Next is set up better than most. Free next-day delivery to 458 stores with no minimum spend, free returns at the same counter, 28 days rather than 14, one company behind both the goods and the money, and a flat £4.95 if a store is not convenient. The subscription maths is honest and it pays for itself on the fifth order of the year. What deserves a cold look is the account, because this is a retailer that earned £301m of interest income and about a sixth of its £1,158m pre-tax profit from lending to its own shoppers. nextpay at 24.9% and pay in 3 at 34.9% from 26 October 2026 are priced like credit cards, the rise reached balances already run up, and interest is charged on the opening balance whatever you pay before the statement prints. Buy the uniform here and collect it in a shop; think twice before spreading it over twelve months.
