Santander Credit Cards Review: Five Cards, 34 Months at 0% and the Fee Arithmetic

Rates and terms checked 12 September 2026 · Credit Cards · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Santander's longest balance transfer runs 34 months at 0% for a 3.15% fee, and its five personal credit cards carry representative APRs from 24.9% to 49.8% variable. Every figure on this page was taken from Santander's own product pages, summary boxes and key facts documents on 12 September 2026, and the document references are given where they matter.

The lender is Santander UK plc, on the Financial Services Register under firm reference number 106054. Its range is unusually easy to read against itself, because it publishes a full summary box for each card with the same headings — the introductory periods, the fees, the default charges and the minimum payment rule all sit in the same place on every document. Three of the five cards charge nothing for spending abroad in local currency, which is rare on a mainstream range rather than a specialist travel card.

Entry is also set low. The stated requirement is an income of £10,500 a year, being 18 or over, permanent UK residence, a clean recent credit history and no bankruptcy, county court judgment or individual voluntary arrangement in the last six years. That is a modest income bar for a range that includes a 34-month transfer card, and it is published rather than left to the application to reveal.

Five cards, and the three jobs they are built for

Two of the five are transfer cards. The Everyday Long Term Balance Transfer card gives 0% on transfers for 34 months from the day the account opens, for a fee of 3.15% with a £5 minimum, and 0% on purchases for three months. The Everyday No Balance Transfer Fee card gives 0% on transfers for 12 months with no fee at all, and the same three months on purchases. Both quote 24.9% APR representative on an assumed £1,200 limit with no monthly charge.

Two are spending cards. The Rewards card pays 3% cashback in the first twelve months on a defined list of everyday categories and 0.25% on everything else, at 24.9% APR representative and no monthly fee. The All in One card costs £3 a month and gives 0% on both purchases and balance transfers for 15 months with no transfer fee, plus 0.5% cashback capped at £10 a month; its purchase rate is 23.9% and the monthly fee lifts the representative APR to 29.8%.

The fifth is a premium card. The World Elite Mastercard costs £15 a month, has the lowest purchase rate in the range at 18.9%, the highest representative APR at 49.8%, and is closed to anyone who is not a Santander Select or Private Banking customer. Across all five, a balance transfer will only be processed if the card is activated within 45 days of arriving.

Thirty-four months at 0%, and what the 3.15% costs

Take a £2,000 debt to the Everyday Long Term card and the fee is £63.00, added to the balance at the point of transfer. Clearing £2,063 over the 34 interest-free months means paying about £60.68 a month, and nothing else is owed provided the payments land on time and the balance is gone before the promotion ends.

Set that against the no-fee card. Twelve months at 0% and no charge for the transfer means £2,000 clears for exactly £2,000 — but only if you can find £166.67 a month to do it. Fall short and the remainder starts attracting 24.9%. So the choice between Santander's two transfer cards is not really about the fee; it is about which monthly payment you can actually sustain. Above roughly £167 a month on a £2,000 balance, the free card wins outright. Below it, the £63 buys you nearly three years of protection and is cheap at the price.

Two details in the summary box are worth having before you commit. The 0% on purchases lasts three months on both cards, not the length of the transfer deal, so anything bought after that sits at 24.9%. And once the introductory period is over, a new balance transfer on the Long Term card attracts a 3% fee with no 0% attached to it. The document those come from is Santander's own summary box, reference ACS1 3417 OCT 25.

The £3-a-month card that undercuts the fee card

The most useful thing on this page is a comparison Santander's own marketing does not draw, because the All in One is sold as a cashback card rather than a transfer card. It gives 0% on balance transfers for 15 months with no transfer fee. The cost of holding it is £3 a month, so fifteen months of it comes to £45.

On that same £2,000 debt, £45 is less than the £63 the Long Term card charges, and it buys three more months than the free card gives. Clearing £2,000 across 15 months needs £133.34 a month. So the range actually offers three thresholds rather than two: £166.67 a month costs nothing, £133.34 a month costs £45, and £60.68 a month costs £63. Anyone who can manage the middle figure is paying less than the headline transfer card asks and finishing more than a year sooner.

The catch is that the £3 does not stop. It is a monthly account fee, not a transfer fee, so it keeps running for as long as the card is open — £36 a year, indefinitely, and 34 months of it would come to £102. The card only undercuts the fee card if you close it or clear it roughly on schedule. Against that, it is the only card in the range with 0% on purchases and transfers at the same time, for the same 15 months, and it pays 0.5% back on spending up to £10 a month.

Where 24.9% actually lands: £151.09 on £1,200

Santander publishes a worked representative example rather than leaving the reader to model it, and it is the clearest number on the whole range. Borrow the assumed £1,200 limit as a single purchase on the Rewards card and repay it in twelve equal monthly instalments at the standard rate, and the total payable is £1,351.09. The interest is £151.09 — about 12.6% of what was borrowed, on a card quoting 24.9%.

The gap is not a discrepancy. An APR describes the rate on money still outstanding, and a balance being repaid in equal instalments is only fully outstanding in the first month. It is worth stating plainly because a reader comparing a 24.9% card against a personal loan quoted at a similar rate is comparing two things that behave the same way, while a reader comparing it against a balance left to sit is not.

For scale, Bank Rate was 3.75% on 12 September 2026, held at the Monetary Policy Committee meeting of 30 July with the next decision set for 17 September. A 24.9% purchase rate is about six and a half times the cost of money, and 49.8% is over thirteen times it. The minimum payment rule is published too: the highest of £5, or the total of interest, monthly fee and default fees plus 1% of the remaining balance plus any arrears. Paying only that leaves most of the debt in place.

Cashback that runs for twelve months, and the list it applies to

The Rewards card pays 3% cashback during the first twelve months from account opening, and Santander publishes the qualifying merchant categories by code rather than by adjective — petrol, diesel and LPG stations, electric vehicle charging, trains, Transport for London and trams, buses, taxis, restaurants, cafés, coffee shops, takeaways and food delivery. Everything else earns 0.25%, and after twelve months everything earns 0.25%. The cashback terms carry no monthly or annual cap, which is unusual and worth checking against your own statement rather than assumed.

What does not earn cashback is set out in the same document: balance transfers, cash transactions, travellers' cheques, foreign currency, interest, account charges, fraudulent transactions, anything cancelled or refunded, and purchases made over the credit limit. Cashback is totted up each month and added on the following month's statement date. The reference is ACSMC1133, dated June 2026.

The other two cashback rates are capped and easy to size. All in One pays 0.5% up to £10 a month, so the cap bites at £2,000 of monthly spending and the £3 fee is covered by £600 a month. World Elite pays 0.5% up to £15 a month, so the cap bites at £3,000 a month.

£15 a month, and the spending it takes to earn it back

The World Elite Mastercard costs £180 a year. Its cashback is 0.5% capped at £15 a month, which is a maximum of £180 a year — so the cashback covers the fee only if you put £3,000 a month, £36,000 a year, through the card, and at any lower spend the card runs at a loss on cashback alone. The rest of the value has to come from the benefits, and those are real: LoungeKey access to over 1,400 airport lounges for the cardholder and additional cardholders, Mastercard Airport Security Fast Track, 3GB of free data roaming a year through Flexiroam across more than 150 countries with 15% off further plans, 40% off Santander travel insurance, and no foreign transaction fees on local-currency purchases.

Whether that beats £180 depends entirely on how often you fly. Guests and children are £24 a visit at the lounges, so a family is paying per head for everyone but the named cardholders.

The representative APR deserves a second look. At 49.8% it is the highest number on this page, and it is mostly the fee: the purchase rate is 18.9%, the lowest in the range. The £15 a month is folded into the representative calculation on an assumed £1,200 limit, which is what pushes 18.9% to 49.8%. A cardholder who clears the balance monthly pays the £180 and no interest; one who carries a balance pays 18.9% on it plus the £180. The card is also closed to anyone outside Santander Select or Private Banking.

The fees that are not in the headline

Default charges are £12 for missing a minimum payment and £12 for being over the credit limit on the statement date, on each card. Cash withdrawals cost 3% of the amount with a £3 minimum, and interest runs from the day of the withdrawal rather than from the statement — on the All in One that cash rate is 29.9% a year, above its own 23.9% purchase rate.

Spending abroad is where the range contradicts itself. The Rewards card, the All in One and the World Elite charge nothing for purchases made in the local currency. The two Everyday cards charge 2.95% of the transaction. Two cards sold under the same brand, from the same lender, on the same application journey, and the traveller who happens to have picked the transfer card pays £29.50 on every £1,000 spent abroad while the cashback customer pays nothing. Nothing on the product pages flags the difference.

One more piece of timing to watch: a balance transfer is only processed if the card is activated within 45 days of receiving it. Miss that and the transfer does not happen, while the introductory clock on the Everyday Long Term card runs from the day the account opened rather than from the day the transfer lands.

57,274 complaints, and a £107.7m penalty

Santander publishes its own complaints return twice a year, which is a better source than any summary of it. For the six months from 1 January to 30 June 2026 it opened 57,274 banking and credit card complaints, a rate of 2.4 per 1,000 accounts. It closed 54% within three days and 46% between three days and eight weeks, and upheld 47%. The largest single cause was recorded as other general administration and customer service.

Nearly half of complaints being upheld is a figure worth sitting with. It means that when a Santander customer takes the trouble to complain, the bank agrees with them about as often as not — which cuts both ways, since a bank that upholds nothing is not necessarily doing better. The 54% closed inside three days is the softer number: it is below what the strongest performers in retail banking report on the same measure.

The regulatory record behind the licence is heavier. On 9 December 2022 the FCA fined Santander UK plc £107,793,300 for serious and persistent anti-money-laundering control failures between 31 December 2012 and 18 October 2017, affecting more than 560,000 business customers, with around £298 million passing through accounts the bank had failed to manage properly before they were closed. The penalty was reduced by 30% for early settlement, from £153,990,400. It concerns business banking rather than credit cards, and it is nearly four years old — but it is the largest published finding against the entity that would be lending you the money, and it belongs on the page.

Who can apply, and where a complaint goes

The published criteria are the same across the range: 18 or over, a permanent UK resident, an annual income of £10,500 or more, a good credit history, no bankruptcy, county court judgment or individual voluntary arrangement in the past six years, and not already holding the card applied for. The World Elite adds the Select or Private Banking requirement on top; Santander states plainly that it cannot accept applications for it from anyone else.

The lender is Santander UK plc under firm reference number 106054, and that reference is what a complaint attaches to. Santander's own complaints process runs first; an unresolved complaint can be taken to the Financial Ombudsman Service free of charge, normally after eight weeks or once a final response has been issued. Purchases between £100 and £30,000 made on any of these cards also carry the statutory joint liability that comes with paying by credit card, which is a protection the card gives and the debit card in the same wallet does not.

The 0% on purchases on the two Everyday cards can be extended to 56 days if the full statement balance is paid on time, which is the standard interest-free window rather than a promotion, and it is the cheapest way to use any card in this range: clear the statement in full and the only card here that costs anything to hold is the one with a monthly fee.

Where it wins

  • 34 months at 0% on balance transfers, the longest in the range, for a 3.15% fee with a £5 minimum
  • A 12-month 0% transfer with no fee at all on the Everyday No Balance Transfer Fee card
  • All in One gives 0% on purchases and transfers together for 15 months with no transfer fee, for £3 a month
  • 3% cashback for the first twelve months on petrol, EV charging, public transport, taxis and eating out, with no cap stated in the cashback terms
  • No foreign transaction fee on local-currency purchases with the Rewards, All in One and World Elite cards
  • A published income requirement of £10,500 a year, which is low for a range including a 34-month transfer card
  • A full worked representative example: £1,200 repaid over 12 months totals £1,351.09
  • A summary box per card with the same headings, so the five can be compared directly against each other

Where it falls short

  • The World Elite costs £180 a year and caps cashback at £15 a month, so £36,000 of annual card spending is needed before the cashback covers the fee
  • The two Everyday cards charge 2.95% on non-sterling transactions while the other three charge nothing, and the product pages do not flag the difference
  • The All in One's £3 monthly fee continues indefinitely after its 15-month 0% period, reaching £102 over 34 months
  • 0% on purchases lasts only three months on both Everyday cards, against 34 and 12 months on transfers
  • A missed minimum payment costs £12 and going over the limit costs £12, on every card
  • Cash withdrawals cost 3% with a £3 minimum and attract interest from the day of withdrawal — 29.9% a year on the All in One, above its own purchase rate
  • The Rewards card's 3% cashback drops to 0.25% after twelve months, on the same spending
  • Santander upheld 47% of the 57,274 banking and credit card complaints it opened in the first half of 2026, and closed only 54% within three days
  • Santander UK plc was fined £107,793,300 by the FCA on 9 December 2022 for anti-money-laundering failures between December 2012 and October 2017
  • A balance transfer is lost if the card is not activated within 45 days of arriving, while the 0% clock runs from account opening

Common questions

Which Santander card gives the longest 0% on a balance transfer?

The Everyday Long Term Balance Transfer card, at 34 months from the day the account opens, for a fee of 3.15% of the amount transferred with a £5 minimum. On £2,000 that fee is £63.00. It gives only three months at 0% on purchases, and after the introductory period new transfers attract a 3% fee with no 0% attached.

Is the fee-free transfer card actually cheaper?

Only if you can clear the balance inside twelve months. The Everyday No Balance Transfer Fee card gives 0% for 12 months and charges nothing, so £2,000 costs £2,000 — but that needs £166.67 a month. At £60.68 a month the same debt takes 34 months, which only the Long Term card covers, and its £63 fee is far cheaper than paying 24.9% on the remainder. The middle option is the All in One: 15 months at 0% with no transfer fee for £3 a month, £45 in total.

What is the representative APR on Santander credit cards?

24.9% variable on the Rewards, Everyday Long Term Balance Transfer and Everyday No Balance Transfer Fee cards; 29.8% variable on the All in One, whose purchase rate is 23.9% with a £3 monthly fee on top; and 49.8% variable on the World Elite Mastercard, whose purchase rate is 18.9% with a £15 monthly fee. All are quoted on an assumed credit limit of £1,200.

How much cashback does the Santander Rewards card pay?

3% for the first twelve months from account opening on a published list of categories — petrol, diesel and LPG, EV charging, trains, Transport for London and trams, buses, taxis, restaurants, cafés, coffee shops, takeaways and food delivery — and 0.25% on everything else. After twelve months everything earns 0.25%. Balance transfers, cash, foreign currency, interest, charges and refunded purchases earn nothing.

Do Santander credit cards charge for spending abroad?

It depends which one. The Rewards, All in One and World Elite cards charge no foreign transaction fee on purchases made in the local currency. The Everyday Long Term Balance Transfer and Everyday No Balance Transfer Fee cards charge 2.95% of the transaction. Cash withdrawals cost 3% with a £3 minimum on every card, with interest running from the day of withdrawal.

What do I need to earn to be accepted?

Santander publishes a minimum income of £10,500 a year, along with being 18 or over, a permanent UK resident, having a good credit history and no bankruptcy, county court judgment or individual voluntary arrangement in the past six years. The World Elite Mastercard additionally requires you to be a Santander Select or Private Banking customer, and Santander states it cannot accept applications from anyone else.

Our verdict

Santander's range rewards reading the summary boxes side by side, because the best transfer deal is not the card sold as one. On a £2,000 balance the 34-month card costs £63 and the All in One costs £45 for 15 months with no transfer fee, so the answer turns on whether you can find £133 a month or only £61. The Rewards card is a decent first-year cashback card on a published category list, and the World Elite needs £36,000 a year of spending before its cashback covers its £180 fee, which makes it a lounge card rather than a value one. Check the 2.95% foreign transaction fee on the two Everyday cards before taking either abroad.
