Santander Personal Loan

Rates and terms checked 23 October 2026 · Loans · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Santander prices its personal loan by how much you borrow and publishes the whole ladder before you apply: 10.6% APR on £1,000 to £2,999, 8.1% on £3,000 to £4,999, 7.5% on £5,000 to £7,499, 6.7% APR representative on £7,500 to £25,000 and 6.9% on £25,001 to £35,000. Four of Santander's own loan pages carried that table on 23 October 2026, each stamped “Information and rates correct as at 06/10/2026”, alongside a stated ceiling of 29.9%.

That is more than most large banks put on the shelf. A searcher can work out the likely rate on a £6,000 loan without handing over a single detail, and the table is headed “New and existing Santander customers” – there is no requirement to bank here first. The eligibility checker runs a soft search that “won't leave a record on your credit file”, an online decision usually arrives within five minutes, and approved money reaches a nominated account within two working days. The rate is fixed, and the written terms are unusually tight about it: Santander will change the agreement only “to comply with a legal or regulatory requirement” and promises it will “never increase the interest rate or the amount of your monthly repayment”.

Three rules decide whether any of it is available. Santander lends on personal loans from age 21, not 18. Its Key Facts Document wants a regular yearly income of at least £10,500 for loans up to £19,999, rising to £20,000 of income for £20,000 to £25,000. And there must have been no bankruptcy, County Court judgment or individual voluntary arrangement in the previous six years, with repayment by Direct Debit.

The ladder, priced in pounds

Percentages hide the size of the decision, so here are the rungs as monthly payments and total cost, worked from Santander's published APRs on a loan with no arrangement fee.

Borrow £3,000 over five years at 8.1% APR and the repayment is about £60.56 a month, £3,633.75 all told, of which £633.75 is interest. Take £10,000 over four years at 6.7% and it is roughly £237.16 a month and £11,383.76 in total; stretch the same £10,000 to five years and the monthly figure drops to about £195.67 while the total rises to £11,740.43. At £25,000 over five years the cost is in the region of £489.18 a month and £29,351.09 overall, and at the new top of the range £35,000 over five years runs to about £687.95 a month and £41,277, with £6,277 of interest.

Two things have moved since this page was last checked on 5 October 2026, and both are the lender's doing rather than the Bank of England's – Bank Rate has stood at 3.75% since 17 September 2026. The three lower rungs each went up by a tenth of a point, from 10.5%, 8.0% and 7.5% to 10.6%, 8.1% and 7.5%, and the representative rung went up by three tenths, from 6.4% to 6.7%. On £10,000 over five years that costs £79.39 more than the old rate; on £25,000 it costs £198.47 more. The smaller rises are trivial in cash – £8.08 on a £2,999 loan, £8 on a £3,000 one – but the direction is the point, because a lender announces a cut and says nothing about an increase.

The ceiling matters as much as the ladder. Each of the four pages states that “the maximum APR we could offer on any loan amount is 29.9%”, so the advertised rung is where pricing starts rather than where it lands. A rate between 6.7% and 29.9% is a spread of 23.2 points, and which end of it you see depends on the credit and affordability assessment behind the application.

The band edges now cut both ways

A price list arranged in bands has edges, and Santander's sit at £3,000, £5,000, £7,500 and now £25,000. Cross one of the first three and the rate on the entire balance falls, which produces an outcome worth checking before you type an amount into the calculator: borrowing slightly more can cost less.

Take a five-year term. £2,999 at 10.6% comes to roughly £3,833.60 in total. £3,000 at 8.1% comes to about £3,633.75. One extra pound of borrowing, £199.85 less to repay, and £3.33 a month cheaper for five years. The same thing happens at £5,000: £4,999 at 8.1% totals around £6,055.04 against £5,000 at 7.5% at £5,976.36, a £78.67 difference. And at the third edge, £7,499 at 7.5% runs to about £8,963.35 at £149.39 a month, while £7,500 at 6.7% runs to £8,805.33 at £146.76 – one pound more borrowed, £158.02 less repaid.

The fourth edge is new and it runs the other way. Santander extended the range from £25,000 to £35,000 and priced the extension at 6.9%, two tenths of a point above the representative rung, so crossing £25,000 raises the rate on the whole balance. £25,000 at 6.7% over five years totals £29,351.09 at £489.18 a month; £25,001 at 6.9% totals £29,484.75 at £491.41. One extra pound of borrowing costs £133.66 more to repay, and there is no amount inside the new band that undoes it – the 6.9% total is above the £25,000 figure from the first pound onwards.

None of that is a trick in the pricing; it is the arithmetic of banded rates, and the bands are published so anyone can do the sum. The practical move is to price the amount just below £25,000 and just above each of the three lower edges as well as the amount you had in mind. It is the single most useful thing this page can tell a reader who has already decided to borrow from Santander.

Where 6.7% sits against the rest of the market

An APR means very little on its own, and there is a free monthly figure that gives it a scale. The Bank of England's Money and Credit release of 29 September 2026 puts the effective interest rate on new personal loans to individuals at 9.96% in August, up from 9.86% in July. Santander's representative rung is 3.26 points below that average; its £1,000 to £2,999 rung, at 10.6%, is 0.64 points above it.

So the shape of this product is a cheap large loan and an expensive small one, measured against what lenders as a whole were actually charging in August. The same release puts interest-charging credit cards at 21.55% and interest-charging overdrafts at 20.27%, which is the comparison that matters if the money is going to clear a card: even the 10.6% rung is less than half the card rate. Net consumer credit borrowing rose to £2.5bn in August from £2.1bn in July, with the annual growth rate at 9.6%.

Industry volumes are flatter than that growth rate suggests. The Finance and Leasing Association's latest consumer finance figures are unchanged since this page was last checked and still cover July 2026, when its members advanced £10.3bn of new credit, 1% below July 2025, with credit card and personal loan new business together 2% lower and retail store and online credit 3% lower, while the first seven months of 2026 ran 4% ahead of 2025.

A second loan that pays your card companies instead of you

Alongside the main product Santander runs a separate agreement called the Personal Loan to pay off your credit card(s), and its Key Facts Document, effective 4 November 2024, describes something built differently. The money does not arrive in a current account: it is “paid directly to your existing credit card account(s) shown in your credit agreement”, and it “cannot be used for any other purpose”.

Two of its terms are more generous than the main loan's and one is tighter. It starts at £500 rather than £1,000, and it opens at 18 rather than 21. Its eligibility list asks only that an applicant has not been declared bankrupt or had an individual voluntary arrangement in six years, where the main loan's list adds a County Court judgment. But the term runs 1 to 4 years rather than 1 to 5, so the same balance has to be cleared a year sooner, and the upper limit is £25,000 against the £35,000 the main loan pages now advertise. The rate is fixed for the life of the agreement, with the document noting that the rate offered “may differ from the advertised Representative APR”.

Because Santander settles the card balances itself, the borrower never has the cash in hand to spend elsewhere, which is the whole point of the design. It is a narrower product than a general-purpose loan and it is easy to miss on the main loans page.

The list of things the money cannot be spent on

The car loan page says only that “we are unable to lend for certain purposes” and sends the reader to the Key Facts Document. The document has the list, and it is longer and more specific than a reader would guess: a house deposit, bridging negative equity, property in the UK or abroad, land “for example for parking space”, investment or business, household bills, living expenses, CSA payments, personal tax bills, time share, stocks and shares, gambling, borrowing on behalf of a third party “unless given as a genuine gift”, equity transfer, capital raising and any business purpose.

Two entries stand out. The first is that household bills and living expenses are excluded, which rules out the reason a good many people go looking for a loan at all, and which sits awkwardly beside the ombudsman decision further down this page. The second is that the document bars crypto currency and then names one company: a loan cannot be used “to pay for crypto currency or to make payments to Binance Markets Ltd”. That is the only company named anywhere in the list, and the exclusion has been sitting in a document effective 4 November 2024 for almost two years.

Santander's own framing of the same page is that “a personal loan from Santander can be used for a wide range of purposes”, and the rate “doesn't vary according to what you're taking the loan out for”. Both are true. The list is worth reading anyway, because the application asks what the money is for.

Five minutes, two working days, a search that leaves no mark

The mechanics are quick and documented. Santander's wording on the checker is that “using our checker will give you an idea of whether we'll accept you for a loan” and that “it won't leave a record on your credit file”, with the hard search attached to the full application: “once you submit your application, we'll do a credit check on you”. An application made between 6.30am and 11.30pm usually produces an accept, decline or refer decision by email within five minutes; one made overnight gets an answer by 7.30am, and a referred case takes up to two working days from the point the extra information arrives.

After approval, “once released, your loan funds will be in your chosen bank account within 2 working days”. Terms run from 12 to 60 months in six-month steps, and the Terms and Conditions set out how the money is priced day to day: “we'll work out interest each day on the outstanding loan amount” and “we will add interest to your loan on the last day of each month”. A maximum of two people can be named on one application.

The statutory escape hatch is spelled out too. Withdrawal is available “within 14 days, starting the day after your loan has been opened”, and the document is explicit that “you won't need to pay any interest if you change your mind during this time”.

A £3.2bn loan book inside a £235.3bn bank

The lender named on the agreement is Santander UK plc, and its own half-year report for the six months to 30 June 2026 gives a profit before tax of £517m against £746m a year earlier, on customer loans of £235.3bn and customer deposits of £220.7bn, with a CET1 capital ratio of 14.2% against 15.8% at the end of 2025. The holding company above it, Santander UK Group Holdings plc, reports the same half on a slightly wider basis – £528m of profit before tax, £238.9bn of loans, £227.2bn of deposits and a banking net interest margin of 2.25% against 2.26% – and it is worth saying which entity a figure belongs to, because the two sets are close enough to be mistaken for each other and only one of them is the lender.

Inside the plc, the unsecured personal loan book was £3.2bn, up from £2.0bn at the end of 2025. Mortgages were £202.6bn, up from £167.3bn, and credit cards £3.7bn against £3.1bn – so Santander still has more money out on plastic than on loans, and both are rounding errors beside the mortgage book. A reader deciding whether to borrow here is dealing with a mortgage bank that also writes loans, which explains why the loan proposition is priced to compete rather than designed to dominate.

Those jumps are an acquisition rather than a lending surge. Santander completed its purchase of TSB on 30 April 2026, and its own results commentary says the bank was “pleased to welcome 4 million customers and 4,500 colleagues to Santander UK”, that “Santander UK and TSB have 480 branches” between them, and that it does “not intend to close any additional Santander or TSB branches before 2028 at the earliest”. TSB continues to trade under its own brand, and existing loan terms are fixed, so nothing about an in-force agreement changes on acquisition.

1,132 complaints, and a third fewer than six months earlier

Santander publishes its own complaints return and the latest covers 1 January to 30 June 2026. Across the bank it opened 62,497 complaints. The Credit Related line – where a personal loan grievance goes – accounted for 1,132 of them, at a rate of 2.9 per 1,000 loans, with 45% upheld and 1,143 closed.

The half before it is the useful comparison and it is on the same page. Between 1 July and 31 December 2025 the Credit Related line ran at 1,660 complaints, 3.9 per 1,000 loans and 53% upheld. So lending complaints fell 31.8% in volume, the rate per thousand loans fell by a full point, and the uphold rate fell eight points. That is a line moving in the right direction on all three measures at once, which is not something most of these returns show.

Set it against the bank's other products in the same return. Banking and credit cards drew 57,274 complaints at 2.4 per 1,000 accounts with 47% upheld; home finance 3,878 at 4.1 per 1,000 balances with 66% upheld; investments 86 at 0.4 per 1,000 client accounts with 47% upheld; insurance and pure protection 82 at 0.2 per 1,000 policies with 24% upheld. Read the speed columns carefully, because they are headed “Percentage closed within 3 days” and “Percentage closed after 3 days but within 8 weeks” and the two groups are separate: 54% and 46% on banking and credit cards means every complaint was closed inside eight weeks, not that 46% of them took longer. Both of those columns read N/A on the Credit Related line, so Santander publishes no speed figure for loan complaints at all.

One sum that cannot be done here is worth flagging, because it looks tempting. A rate of 2.9 per 1,000 loans against 1,132 complaints implies roughly 390,000 loan accounts, but the return's own heading covers “Santander & Cahoot” while the £3.2bn book is the consolidated figure that now includes the loans acquired with TSB, so dividing one by the other does not give an average balance. Independent ratings are thin too. Smart Money People shows the Santander loan at 3.20 out of 5 from 57 reviews, against 3.75 from 2,666 reviews for the brand, with only the business bank account and Options home insurance scoring lower among the 23 Santander products it rates. No Defaqto star rating or Fairer Finance score specific to the personal loan could be found, which needs confirming rather than reading as an absence of quality.

Fifty-eight days, and the twelve-month line that halves it

The sales pages lead with unlimited free overpayments. The Key Facts Document is where closing the agreement gets priced, and it carries a condition the headline number hides: to work out a settlement figure Santander will “assume that you'll repay your loan in full within 28 days”, and “if your original loan term was longer than 12 months, you'll also be charged an extra 30 days' interest”, which “means you'll be charged up to 58 days' interest in total”.

The twelve-month qualifier matters and it is easy to miss. A loan taken over 12 months settles on 28 days' interest, not 58, and the support page repeats the condition in the same terms. On £5,000 outstanding at 6.7% the whole 58 days is about £51.79, of which the extra 30 days is £26.86; at 10.6% the 58 days is about £80.69 with £41.90 of it the extra; and at the 29.9% ceiling on £10,000 it is about £424.45 with £221.74 of it the extra. Tens of pounds on a mid-range loan, a few hundred at the top of the rate range.

Credit where it is due: Santander does the addition for the reader and prints the total, which not every lender bothers to do, and the allowance itself comes from the Consumer Credit (Early Settlement) Regulations 2004 rather than from Santander. What the sales pages do not do is flag that free overpayments and up to 58 days' interest are answers to two different questions. Chipping away at the balance while the agreement runs is free; closing it is not. The exact figure comes from one phone call, on 0800 028 4055, and it stays “available for 28 days”.

The detail around a partial overpayment has a trap of its own. The default is that “your loan term will be reduced, but your monthly repayment will stay the same”. Keeping the original term and paying less each month is available, but it has to be asked for, and the reduction “may take up to 3 months to take full effect”. Any surplus is refunded within 30 days, and the Direct Debit should not be cancelled until the loan is repaid in full.

What the rulebook makes a lender do once the money has gone

Most of the attention on consumer credit rules goes on the decision to lend. The obligations that run for the next five years sit in CONC 6.7 of the FCA Handbook and are short enough to quote. CONC 6.7.2R, in force since 1 March 2018, requires that “a firm must monitor a customer's repayment record and take appropriate action where there are signs of actual or possible repayment difficulties”. That is a standing duty, not a response to a phone call.

The refinancing rules are the ones to read before consolidating anything. CONC 6.7.17R, from 25 November 2020, defines refinancing to cover replacing, varying or supplementing an agreement, exercising a contractual power, or “granting an indulgence or waiver to the customer”. CONC 6.7.18R, in force since 1 April 2014, is blunt: “a firm must not encourage a customer to refinance a regulated credit agreement if the result would be the customer's commitments are not sustainable”. CONC 6.7.19R, from the same date, bars refinancing other than by forbearance unless the firm acts “at the customer's request or with the customer's consent” and “reasonably believes that it is not against the customer's best interests to do so”.

Santander's debt consolidation page sits squarely in that territory and does not oversell itself. Its own caveat is that “we can't guarantee that this type of loan will reduce your overall cost of borrowing”. The same page sets out two routes to borrowing more and they are not equivalent: a top-up where “your new loan will pay off your existing loan”, which settles the original and therefore triggers the interest allowance described above, and a second loan alongside the first, which does not but leaves two repayments running.

The other end of the same chapter is less comfortable reading, and the published documents go further than this page previously recorded. If payments stop, Santander can demand the balance “straight away”, “use money you have in any other accounts held with us to pay off your outstanding balance” – a right of set off, named as such in section 12 of the Terms and Conditions – charge “our reasonable costs in trying to collect the loan amount from you, including legal costs”, “sell your loan to a different company who will continue to try and collect the loan from you”, take legal action, and report the account to credit reference agencies. Section 11 adds that Santander “can transfer all of our rights and duties under this agreement to someone else at any time”. The document promises only that “we'll usually let you know before we do any of these things”.

The support side has improved since this page was last checked, when the money worries page would not open at all. It now reads normally and lists three things a struggling borrower can ask for: “set up a repayment plan to get back on track”, “a temporary reduction in your monthly repayment” and “breathing space, 30 days to get free independent debt advice”, on a dedicated line, 0800 085 1152, which is not the complaints number. What it does not do is name a single free debt advice organisation, while the loan's own Key Facts Document names PayPlan and gives its number, 0800 280 2816.

One £1,000 loan, as an ombudsman read it

Published Financial Ombudsman Service decisions are the closest thing to watching a lender's affordability process tested from outside. Decision DRN-5688533, decided by ombudsman Robert Walker, concerns a £1,000 Santander personal loan taken in March 2019 at 24.89% APR, repayable in 60 monthly instalments of £27.87 – £672.20 of interest, fees and charges on top of the £1,000, and £1,672.20 to repay in all.

The bank statement Santander itself collected showed the customer already dependent on payday lending, and the ombudsman checked the credit file and found regular payday borrowing both before and after the loan, so the statement was not a one-off. The complaint was upheld. Santander was directed to “remove all interest, fees and charges applied” from the outset, treat payments already made as reducing the £1,000 balance, refund anything paid above it with interest “at 8% per year simple” from the date of each overpayment, and remove all adverse information about the loan from the credit file.

Three things in that for a reader today. The affordability assessment is a check Santander can get wrong, and the Ombudsman will unwind the cost of the credit when it does. A £1,000 loan written over five years at £27.87 a month looks affordable on the face of it, which is exactly what the decision says made it look affordable, and it is the shape the Key Facts Document's own ban on lending for “living expenses” is meant to prevent. And 24.89% on £1,000 is a reminder of how far the price can sit from the bottom rung: well inside the 29.9% ceiling, on an amount whose advertised rate today is 10.6%.

The figures Santander does not serve

For a product where the law is built around a worked example, the example is the hardest thing to read. On the main loans page and on the car, home improvement and debt consolidation pages, the representative example renders as labels without values – “Loan amount”, “Monthly repayment”, “Interest rate”, “Term”, “Total amount repayable” – because the calculator fills them in by script, with the note that “these figures will change every time you make changes to the calculator”. On two of the four pages the unrendered placeholders calc_rate and calc_rate_max are visible in the served text. A visitor using a browser normally almost certainly sees the numbers appear, so this is a rendering matter rather than a missing disclosure, and it is stated here as unestablished rather than absent. It still means the one passage designed for comparing lenders side by side cannot be read without running the page, and it has now been true on three separate checks over three weeks.

The same template carries a second oddity. All four pages state a maximum of 29.9% in their own text, twice, and all four also serve the sentence “maximum APR we could offer on a loan of £XX,XXX is 26.9%” with the amounts left as placeholders. A figure sitting beside £XX,XXX is template filler rather than a published rate, and it is read here as such; 29.9% is the number Santander states in prose.

The paperwork has not kept up with the product. The newest Key Facts Document that could be located is still effective 4 November 2024, and so are the Terms and Conditions, reference CONS 0055 NOV 24. Both describe loans “of between £1,000 and £25,000”, while every live loan page now advertises £1,000 to £35,000 and prices a 6.9% band above £25,000. The income requirement is published only in that document, which means there is no stated income floor at all for the £25,001 to £35,000 band, and the regulated pre-sale summary a borrower is pointed to describes a product narrower than the one on sale. Whether a 2025 or 2026 version exists behind the application journey could not be confirmed.

There is also no fee schedule anywhere in the open material. Beyond the 58-day settlement allowance, which is explained, and the undefined “reasonable costs” of collection, a prospective borrower cannot find out what anything else costs until the agreement is in front of them. The Terms and Conditions name the PRA and the FCA and make no mention of the Financial Services Compensation Scheme, which is correct rather than an omission – that scheme protects deposits and has nothing to pay out on a loan – and the complaints route they give is Santander on 0800 171 2171 and then the Financial Ombudsman Service, with no reference to the European online dispute resolution platform that several banks still signpost and that was discontinued in July 2025.

Common questions

What rate will Santander actually give me?

The advertised rate depends on the amount: 10.6% APR on £1,000 to £2,999, 8.1% on £3,000 to £4,999, 7.5% on £5,000 to £7,499, 6.7% APR representative on £7,500 to £25,000 and 6.9% on £25,001 to £35,000. Those are starting points. Santander states a maximum of 29.9%, and the rate offered follows its credit and affordability assessment. The eligibility checker uses a soft search, so it costs nothing on a credit file to find out.

Why did the rates change in October 2026?

Santander repriced the whole ladder with effect from 6 October 2026, the date stamped on each of its four loan pages. The three lower rungs each rose by a tenth of a point and the representative rung rose from 6.4% to 6.7%, while the range was extended from £25,000 to £35,000 with the new slice priced at 6.9%. Bank Rate has been 3.75% since 17 September 2026, so none of the increase follows a change there. On £10,000 over five years the new rate costs £79.39 more than the old one.

Do I need a Santander current account to get a loan?

No. The rate table is headed for new and existing Santander customers alike, and there is no requirement to hold a Santander account first. You do need to be able to repay by Direct Debit, be 21 or over, live permanently in the UK, be employed, self-employed or retired, and have had no bankruptcy, County Court judgment or individual voluntary arrangement in the last six years. The Key Facts Document adds a regular yearly income of at least £10,500 for loans up to £19,999 or £20,000 for loans of £20,000 to £25,000.

Is it really free to overpay?

Partial overpayments are, and interest is worked out daily, so the saving starts at once. The default is that the term shortens and the monthly payment stays the same; a lower payment over the original term has to be asked for and can take up to three months to take effect. Clearing the loan in full is different: the Key Facts Document assumes settlement within 28 days and adds an extra 30 days' interest where the original term was longer than 12 months, up to 58 days in total. On £5,000 outstanding at 6.7% that is about £51.79, of which £26.86 is the extra. Ask for a settlement figure on 0800 028 4055; it holds for 28 days.

What can a Santander loan not be used for?

The Key Facts Document rules out a house deposit, bridging negative equity, property in the UK or abroad, land, investment or business, household bills, living expenses, CSA payments, personal tax bills, time share, stocks and shares, gambling, borrowing for a third party unless it is a genuine gift, equity transfer, capital raising and any business purpose. It also bars crypto currency and payments to one named company, Binance Markets Ltd. The rate itself does not vary with the purpose.

What happens if I miss a payment?

Santander can ask for the whole balance back straight away, take money from any other account you hold with it under the right of set off in section 12 of the Terms and Conditions, charge its reasonable collection costs including legal costs, sell the loan to another company, take legal action and report the account to credit reference agencies, usually after telling you first. Before it gets that far the money worries line is 0800 085 1152, and the options listed there are a repayment plan, a temporary reduction in the monthly payment, or 30 days of breathing space to get free independent debt advice.

Who regulates Santander, and where does a complaint go?

Santander UK plc is on the Financial Services Register under firm reference number 106054, checkable at fca.org.uk/register. Complaints go to the bank first, on 0800 171 2171, and can then be referred to the Financial Ombudsman Service. Between 1 January and 30 June 2026 Santander opened 1,132 credit-related complaints, 2.9 per 1,000 loans, and upheld 45% of them, against 1,660 at 3.9 per 1,000 and 53% upheld in the previous six months.

Our verdict

Santander is one of the few large UK lenders that tells you the price before it tells you about itself: 10.6%, 8.1%, 7.5%, 6.7% and 6.9% APR, set by how much you borrow, published openly and open to people who bank elsewhere. The representative rung sits more than three points under the 9.96% the market charged on new personal loans in August, the written terms promise never to raise the rate or the payment, and the banded structure throws up a genuinely useful quirk at £3,000, £5,000 and £7,500, where borrowing a pound more drops the rate on the whole balance and cuts the total cost. The caveats have grown since the range was extended. Every rung rose on 6 October while Bank Rate stood still; the new £35,000 ceiling is priced above the representative rate, so crossing £25,000 costs more rather than less; and the Key Facts Document a borrower is pointed at still describes a £25,000 product and is the only place the income requirement appears. Worth a soft search if you are borrowing £3,000 or more, and worth pricing the amount just above each lower band edge – and just below £25,000 – before you commit.
