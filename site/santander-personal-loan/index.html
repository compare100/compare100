Santander Personal Loan

Rates and terms checked 5 October 2026 · Loans · Compare100 editorial team

Affiliate disclosure: we may earn a commission if you take out a product through links on this page. It costs you nothing extra and does not influence how providers are listed.

Santander sets its personal loan price by how much you borrow, and it publishes the whole ladder before you apply: 10.5% APR on £1,000 to £2,999, 8.0% on £3,000 to £4,999, 7.5% on £5,000 to £7,499 and 6.4% APR representative on £7,500 to £25,000. Those four rates were read off Santander's own loan pages on 5 October 2026, alongside a stated ceiling of 29.9%.

That is more than most of the big banks put on the shelf. A searcher can work out the likely rate on a £6,000 loan without handing over a single detail, and the table applies to new and existing Santander customers alike – there is no requirement to bank with Santander first. The eligibility checker runs a soft search that leaves no mark on a credit file, an online decision usually arrives within five minutes, and approved money reaches a nominated account within two working days.

Two rules decide whether any of it is available. Santander lends on personal loans from age 21, not 18, and it wants a regular yearly income of at least £10,500 for loans up to £19,999, rising to £20,000 of income for anything between £20,000 and £25,000. No bankruptcy, County Court judgment or individual voluntary arrangement in the previous six years, and repayment has to be by Direct Debit.

The ladder, priced in pounds

Percentages hide the size of the decision, so here are the same four rungs as monthly payments and total cost, worked from Santander's published APRs on a loan with no arrangement fee.

Borrow £3,000 over five years at 8.0% APR and the repayment is about £60.43 a month, £3,625.75 all told, of which £625.75 is interest. Take £10,000 over four years at 6.4% and it is roughly £235.86 a month and £11,321.21 in total; stretch the same £10,000 to five years and the monthly figure drops to about £194.35 while the total rises to £11,661.05. At the top of the range, £25,000 over five years costs in the region of £485.88 a month and £29,152.62 overall, with £4,152.62 of interest.

The ceiling matters as much as the ladder. Santander states plainly that "the maximum APR we could offer is 29.9%", so the advertised rung is where pricing starts rather than where it lands. A rate between 6.4% and 29.9% is a spread of more than 23 points, and which end of it you see depends on the credit and affordability assessment behind the application.

Where borrowing one pound more makes the loan cheaper

A price list arranged in bands has edges, and Santander's edges sit at £3,000, £5,000 and £7,500. Cross one and the rate on the entire balance falls, which produces an outcome worth checking before you type an amount into the calculator: on the published rates, borrowing slightly more can cost less.

Take a five-year term. £2,999 at 10.5% comes to roughly £3,825.51 in total. £3,000 at 8.0% comes to about £3,625.75. One extra pound of borrowing, £199.76 less to repay. The same thing happens, more mildly, at £5,000: £4,999 at 8.0% totals around £6,041.71 against £5,000 at 7.5% at £5,976.36, a £65.34 difference. And at the top edge, £7,400 at 7.5% over five years runs to about £8,845.02 at £147.42 a month, while £7,500 at 6.4% runs to £8,745.79 at £145.76 a month – a hundred pounds more borrowed, £99.23 less repaid, and a smaller payment every month for five years.

None of that is a trick in the pricing; it is the arithmetic of banded rates, and the bands are published so anyone can do the sum. It does mean the sensible move is to price the amount just above each edge as well as the amount you had in mind, and it is the single most useful thing this page can tell a reader who has already decided to borrow from Santander.

A second loan that pays your card companies instead of you

Alongside the main product, Santander runs a separate agreement called the Personal Loan to pay off your credit card(s), and its Key Facts Document, effective 4 November 2024, describes something built differently. The money does not arrive in a current account: it is "paid directly to your existing credit card account(s) shown in your credit agreement", and it "cannot be used for any other purpose".

Two of its terms are more generous than the main loan's. It starts at £500 rather than £1,000, and it opens at 18 rather than 21. The eligibility list also asks only that an applicant has not been declared bankrupt or had an individual voluntary arrangement in six years, where the main loan's list adds a County Court judgment. The upper limit is the same £25,000 and the rate is fixed for the life of the agreement, with the document noting that the rate offered "may differ from the advertised Representative APR".

Because Santander settles the card balances itself, the borrower never has the cash in hand to spend elsewhere, which is the whole point of the design. It is a narrower product than a general-purpose loan and it is easy to miss on the main loans page.

Five minutes, two working days, a search that leaves no mark

The mechanics are quick and documented. Santander's own wording on the checker is that "using our checker will give you an idea of whether we'll accept you for a loan. It won't leave a record on your credit file" – a soft search, with the hard one attached to the full application. Online applications made between 6.30am and 11.30pm typically get a decision within five minutes.

After approval, "once released, your loan funds will be in your chosen bank account within 2 working days". Applying in a branch can be faster still: Santander says an approved branch application on an account more than 60 days old can be funded instantly, and a phone application usually lands the next working day. Terms run from 12 to 60 months in six-month steps, and the rate is fixed – "the rate you pay is fixed and will remain so for the life of your loan".

The statutory escape hatch is spelled out too. Withdrawal is available "within 14 days, starting the day after your loan has been opened", and the document is explicit that "you won't need to pay any interest if you change your mind during this time". Up to two applicants can go on one agreement.

A £3.2bn loan book inside a £238.9bn bank

Santander UK plc reported a profit before tax of £528m for the six months to 30 June 2026, down from £764m a year earlier, on customer loans of £238.9bn and customer deposits of £227.2bn. Banking net interest margin was 2.25% against 2.26%, and the CET1 capital ratio stood at 14.2%.

Inside that, the unsecured personal loan book was £3.2bn. Mortgages were £204.7bn, and credit cards £3.7bn – so Santander has more money out on plastic than on loans, and both are rounding errors beside the mortgage book. A reader deciding whether to borrow here is dealing with a mortgage bank that also writes loans, which is worth knowing mainly because it explains why the loan proposition is priced to compete rather than designed to dominate.

The shape of the bank changed this year. Santander completed its purchase of TSB on 30 April 2026, bringing in around five million customers and roughly 174 branches to make a combined network of about 480, with a commitment that no further Santander or TSB branches will close before 2028 at the earliest. TSB continues to trade under its own brand for now, and existing TSB loan and mortgage terms are fixed, so nothing about an in-force agreement changes on acquisition.

For market context, the Finance and Leasing Association reported £10.3bn of new consumer credit advanced by its members in July 2026, 1% below July 2025, with credit card and personal loan new business together 2% lower than the same month a year before and the first seven months of 2026 running 4% ahead of 2025. Demand is flat rather than falling away.

1,132 complaints, and roughly 390,000 loans behind them

Santander publishes its own complaints return, and the figures for 1 January to 30 June 2026 are specific enough to be useful. Across the bank it opened 62,497 complaints. The Credit Related line – which is where a personal loan grievance goes – accounted for 1,132 of them, at a rate of 2.9 per 1,000 loans, with 45% upheld.

Set that against the bank's other product lines in the same return. Banking and credit cards drew 57,274 complaints at 2.4 per 1,000 accounts, 54% closed within three days and 47% upheld. Home finance drew 3,878 at 4.1 per 1,000 balances with 66% upheld. Insurance and pure protection managed 82 complaints at 0.2 per 1,000 policies and 24% upheld. The lending line is therefore running barely above the everyday banking line on frequency and slightly below it on uphold rate, which is not what a borrower braced for an argument about a declined decision might expect.

The rate also gives away a number Santander does not otherwise publish. If 1,132 complaints represent 2.9 per 1,000 loans, there are roughly 390,000 live loan accounts, which against a £3.2bn book implies an average balance somewhere near £8,200 – comfortably inside the 6.4% rung. That is derived arithmetic rather than a disclosed figure, so treat it as an estimate.

Independent ratings are thinner. Smart Money People shows the Santander loan at 3.20 out of 5 from 57 reviews, last reviewed 22 January 2026. No Defaqto star rating or Fairer Finance score specific to the personal loan could be found, which needs confirming rather than reading as an absence of quality.

Fifty-eight days, and Santander prints the number itself

The sales pages lead with "overpay at no extra cost" and "you can make unlimited overpayments at any time before your final repayment is due or you can repay your loan in full". The Key Facts Document, effective 4 November 2024, is where the second half of that sentence gets priced: on settling in full, Santander says it will "assume that you'll repay your loan in full within 28 days so it will include interest over that period" and that "you'll also be charged an extra 30 days' interest. That means you'll be charged up to 58 days' interest in total".

Credit where it is due: Santander does the addition for the reader and prints the total, which not every lender bothers to do. The charge itself is an allowance the Consumer Credit (Early Settlement) Regulations 2004 give every creditor, not something Santander invented, and taking the full allowance is normal practice.

What the sales pages do not do is flag that "no extra cost" and "up to 58 days' interest" are answers to two different questions. Chipping away at the balance while keeping the agreement running is free. Closing the agreement is not. On £5,000 still outstanding the extra works out at roughly £49.53 at 6.4% APR and about £79.96 at 10.5%, so it is tens of pounds rather than hundreds – but a reader who plans to clear the loan with a bonus should ask for a settlement figure, on 0800 028 4055, rather than assume the headline covers them. A partial overpayment shortens the term by default; keeping the original term and paying less each month is available on request.

What the rulebook makes a lender do once the money has gone

Most of the attention on consumer credit rules goes on the decision to lend. The obligations that run for the next five years sit in CONC 6.7 of the FCA Handbook, and they are short enough to quote. CONC 6.7.2R, in force since 1 March 2018, requires that "a firm must monitor a customer's repayment record and take appropriate action where there are signs of actual or possible repayment difficulties". That is a standing duty, not a response to a phone call.

The refinancing rules are the ones worth reading before consolidating anything. CONC 6.7.17R, from 25 November 2020, defines refinancing to include extending a repayment period by agreement, by contractual power or by waiver. CONC 6.7.18R, in force since 1 April 2014, is blunt: "a firm must not encourage a customer to refinance a regulated credit agreement if the result would be the customer's commitments are not sustainable." CONC 6.7.19R bars refinancing without the customer's request or consent unless the firm is exercising forbearance, and then only where it reasonably believes it is in the customer's interests.

Santander's debt consolidation page sits squarely in that territory and does not oversell itself. Its own caveat is that "we can't guarantee that this type of loan will reduce your overall cost of borrowing", with a prompt to compare existing rates against what is offered. A longer term at a lower rate can still cost more in total, and the bank says so.

The other end of the same chapter is less comfortable reading. If payments stop, the Key Facts Document says Santander can "ask you to repay the remaining loan amount back straight away", "charge you our reasonable costs in trying to collect the loan amount from you, including legal costs and costs finding you", and "give information about you and how you've managed your account to licensed credit reference agencies". The collection costs are described as reasonable rather than listed, which is a real gap: there is no published schedule putting a number on them.

One £1,000 loan, as an ombudsman read it

Published Financial Ombudsman Service decisions are the closest thing to seeing a lender's affordability process tested by somebody outside it. Decision DRN-5688533, decided by ombudsman Robert Walker, concerns a £1,000 Santander personal loan taken in March 2019 at 24.89% APR.

The bank statements Santander itself collected showed the customer paying nearly £800 a month to eight loan providers, four of them payday lenders. The ombudsman found that "the bank statement shows that Mr G was likely dependent on payday lending" and that new borrowing was going on living costs, and concluded that Santander had lent anyway despite holding evidence that contradicted its credit check. The complaint was upheld. Santander was told to remove £672.20 of interest, fees and charges, refund anything paid above the original £1,000 with 8% simple annual interest from the date of payment, and delete the adverse entries from the customer's credit file.

Two things in that for a reader today. The affordability assessment is a check Santander can get wrong, and the Ombudsman will unwind the cost of the credit when it does. And 24.89% on a £1,000 loan is a reminder of how far the price can sit from the bottom rung: that rate is well inside the 29.9% ceiling, on an amount whose advertised rate today is 10.5%.

The figures Santander does not serve

For a product where the law is built around a worked example, the example is the hardest thing to read. On the main loans page and on the car, home improvement and debt consolidation pages, the representative example renders as labels without values – "APR representative", "Monthly repayment", "Total amount repayable" – because the calculator fills them in by script, with the note that "these figures will change every time you make changes to the calculator". A visitor using a browser normally almost certainly sees the numbers appear, so this is a rendering matter rather than a missing disclosure, and it is stated here as unestablished rather than absent. It still means the one passage designed for comparing lenders side by side cannot be read without running the page.

The newest Key Facts Document that could be located is effective 4 November 2024, which was almost two years old at the date of checking. Whether a 2025 or 2026 version exists behind the application journey could not be confirmed, so every quotation above is attributed to that document and that date. Santander's money worries page would not open on the day of checking, so the forbearance options it describes and the free debt advice organisations it names could not be verified first hand – worth a call to 0800 171 2171 if arrears are the reason for reading.

There is also no fee schedule anywhere in the open material. Beyond the 58-day settlement allowance and the undefined "reasonable costs" of collection, a prospective borrower cannot find out what anything else costs until the agreement is in front of them.

Where it wins

  • Four rates published by loan size before you apply, rather than a single representative figure with nothing behind it
  • No requirement to bank with Santander – the same ladder applies to new and existing customers
  • Soft-search eligibility checker that leaves no mark on a credit file, with an online decision usually inside five minutes
  • Unlimited partial overpayments at no charge, and the choice of a shorter term or a smaller monthly payment
  • The Key Facts Document prints the full 58-day settlement figure instead of leaving the reader to add it up
  • A separate credit-card payoff loan from £500, open from age 18, paid straight to the card providers
  • Funds within two working days, and potentially the same day on a branch application
  • Lending complaints run at 2.9 per 1,000 loans with 45% upheld, barely above the bank's everyday banking line

Where it falls short

  • The 6.4% headline covers only £7,500 to £25,000; the ceiling is 29.9%, a spread of more than 23 points with no published table of who gets what
  • "Overpay at no extra cost" sits on the sales pages while the Key Facts Document charges up to 58 days' interest to settle in full, and neither page cross-refers to the other
  • The representative example renders as empty labels on all four loan pages, so the one legally mandated comparison passage is unreadable without running the page's script
  • Minimum age 21 and an income floor of £10,500, rising to £20,000 of income above £20,000 borrowed, rules out a slice of borrowers other lenders will quote
  • No published fee or charge schedule: collection costs are described only as "reasonable", with no figures attached
  • The newest Key Facts Document findable is effective 4 November 2024, and no later version could be confirmed
  • An ombudsman found in DRN-5688533 that Santander lent £1,000 at 24.89% to a customer its own bank statements showed to be dependent on payday lending
  • No Defaqto or Fairer Finance rating specific to the loan could be found, and Smart Money People has only 57 reviews behind a 3.20 out of 5

Common questions

What rate will Santander actually give me?

The advertised rate depends on the amount: 10.5% APR on £1,000 to £2,999, 8.0% on £3,000 to £4,999, 7.5% on £5,000 to £7,499 and 6.4% APR representative on £7,500 to £25,000. Those are starting points. Santander states a maximum of 29.9%, and the rate offered follows its credit and affordability assessment. The eligibility checker uses a soft search, so it costs nothing on a credit file to find out.

Do I need a Santander current account to get a loan?

No. The published rate table applies to new and existing Santander customers alike, and there is no requirement to hold a Santander account first. You do need to be able to repay by Direct Debit, be 21 or over, live permanently in the UK, and have a regular yearly income of at least £10,500 for loans up to £19,999 or £20,000 for loans of £20,000 to £25,000.

Is it really free to overpay?

Partial overpayments are. Santander says you can make unlimited overpayments at any time at no extra cost, and the term shortens unless you ask to keep it and pay less each month. Clearing the loan in full is different: the Key Facts Document effective 4 November 2024 assumes settlement within 28 days and adds an extra 30 days' interest, up to 58 days in total. On £5,000 outstanding that is roughly £49.53 at 6.4% APR. Ask for a settlement figure on 0800 028 4055 before you pay it off.

How quickly does the money arrive?

An online application made between 6.30am and 11.30pm typically gets a decision within five minutes, and approved funds reach your chosen account within two working days. A branch application on an account more than 60 days old can be funded instantly, and a phone application usually lands the next working day. You then have 14 days from the day after the loan opens to withdraw, with no interest to pay.

Who regulates Santander, and where does a complaint go?

Santander UK plc is on the Financial Services Register under firm reference number 106054, checkable at fca.org.uk/register. Complaints go to the bank first, on 0800 171 2171, and can then be referred to the Financial Ombudsman Service if you are unhappy with the answer. Between 1 January and 30 June 2026 Santander opened 1,132 credit-related complaints, 2.9 per 1,000 loans, and upheld 45% of them.

Is a Santander consolidation loan cheaper than my credit cards?

Not necessarily, and Santander does not claim it is: its own wording is that it cannot guarantee this type of loan will reduce your overall cost of borrowing. A lower rate over a longer term can still cost more in total interest. CONC 6.7.18R forbids a lender from encouraging refinancing where the result would be unsustainable commitments, but the sum itself is still yours to do. There is also a separate Santander loan from £500 that pays your card balances directly and cannot be spent on anything else.

Our verdict

Santander is one of the few large UK lenders that tells you the price before it tells you about itself: 10.5%, 8.0%, 7.5% and 6.4% APR, set by how much you borrow, published openly and open to people who bank elsewhere. That makes it unusually easy to check, and the banded structure throws up a genuinely useful quirk at £3,000, £5,000 and £7,500, where borrowing a pound or a hundred more drops the rate on the whole balance and cuts the total cost. The caveats are the ones that come with any bank loan priced on a ladder: the advertised rung is a starting point against a 29.9% ceiling, settling early costs up to 58 days' interest even though the sales page says overpayments are free, and the detail thins out fast once you go looking for a fee schedule or a current Key Facts Document. Worth a soft search if you are borrowing £3,000 or more, and worth pricing the amount just above the nearest band edge before you commit.
