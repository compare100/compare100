Trip.com – Global Hotel Deals

Rates and terms checked 8 October 2026 · Hotels · Compare100 editorial team

Our interest: we earn a commission if you book through the link on this page. It costs you nothing. The jurisdiction clause and the search problem we found below are reported as they are, whether they help the sale or not.

Start with the thing that genuinely counts in Trip.com’s favour, because plenty of large booking sites cannot match it. Its UK terms state: “We provide full financial protection for our ATOL protected flight only sales”, citing Air Travel Organiser’s Licence number 11572.

That is a real, checkable licence number rather than a badge, and the UK party named alongside it is Trip Air Ticketing (UK) Limited, which holds an ICO data-protection registration, ZB360291, at Floor 10, 70 St Mary Axe, London EC3A 8BE, registered on 1 August 2022. You can verify the ATOL number yourself on the Civil Aviation Authority’s register before you pay, which is exactly how the scheme is meant to work.

Hotels are a different arrangement, and that is where this page spends its time. Your hotel contract runs to Trip.com Travel Singapore Pte. Ltd. at 30 Raffles Place, #29-01, Singapore 048622, under Singapore law. And when we searched its UK site for hotels in London, a good part of what came back was in London, Kentucky.

The search result that should worry you

We opened the page headed “Hotels in London” on the UK site on 8 October 2026 and read what it offered. These were the properties and prices:

Fairfield Inn, Hampton Inn, Microtel, Baymont, Motel 6, Comfort Suites, Quality Inn, Budget Host. Those are American roadside chains, and two of them carry “KY” in the name. This is London, Kentucky — a town of a few thousand people off Interstate 75 — served up under a heading that says Hotels in London on a uk.trip.com address.

A £53-a-night Microtel looks like a bargain next to central London prices. It is not a bargain; it is a different continent. Nobody is being deliberately deceived here — it is a place-name collision in a database — but the consequence is the same if you book in a hurry.

What to do about it: before you book anything on this site, check the city, region and country on the property page, not the name in the list. Look at the map. If a price looks too good for the city you have in mind, that is usually because it is not that city. The same trap exists for Birmingham, Boston, Richmond, Hamilton and a dozen others.

The £81 against £66 inconsistency on the same hotel on the same page is a smaller problem of the same kind: the figure in the listing and the figure in the page’s own FAQ did not match. Treat any “from” price as an advertisement and the basket as the price.

Who you are contracting with, and under whose law

The terms name two companies and they do different jobs.

Trip.com Travel Singapore Pte. Ltd. is described as “a limited liability company with its address at 30 Raffles Place, #29-01 Singapore 048622”. Trip Air Ticketing (UK) Limited is the UK party, and the one that carries the ATOL licence — though the terms give no company number or registered address for it, which we had to find elsewhere.

The jurisdiction clause is the part to understand. The terms are “governed by and construed in accordance with the law of Singapore”, and “the courts of Singapore will have jurisdiction over any dispute or claim”.

Two saving clauses soften that, and they are worth knowing because they are genuinely in your favour:

  • Your mandatory consumer rights as a UK resident are preserved — a choice-of-law clause cannot strip them out.
  • You keep the right to sue in the courts of your own country of residence instead of Singapore.

So the position is better than a bare “Singapore law” clause sounds. But the default remains a Singapore contract interpreted under Singapore law, and that is a different footing from booking with a British company.

On its role, Trip.com calls itself “a global online travel agency” and says the travel products are “provided by third party suppliers”. The terms avoid the words agent and principal, but the practical effect is clear enough: on a hotel booking, the hotel’s own terms govern what you can change. The relevant line is “please check the relevant Supplier’s terms for information on your rights to change or cancel your booking”.

There is no ABTA membership and no named dispute resolution scheme in the terms.

Cancellations: the terms point you somewhere else

This is the honest shape of a marketplace booking, and it is worth being clear-eyed about rather than disappointed by later.

For hotels and other supplier-provided products, Trip.com does not set the cancellation rules — the supplier does, and the terms tell you to go and read them. On flights, the wording is: “if you are unsure whether your flight ticket permits changes and/or refunds for cancellation, please contact us”. On refunds generally: “any refunds will be paid back to the original payment method, unless we agree otherwise”, which is the right answer and worth having in writing.

What that means in practice:

  • The cancellation policy you are agreeing to is on the room, not on the website. Two rooms in the same hotel on the same page can have completely different terms, and the cheaper one is usually the non-refundable one.
  • Read the policy on the room you are about to pay for, on the payment page, not the one you read on the search results.
  • Screenshot it. If you later need to argue, the policy as displayed at the moment of payment is the thing you want evidence of.

One reassurance on price: for prepaid bookings the terms say “you will pay the exact amount shown on the payment page”. So the payment page figure is binding even if the search result was not. What the terms do not say anywhere we could find is whether displayed prices include taxes and fees — and the London hotel list we read made no statement about taxes or fees either. On a marketplace that often means the room rate is shown and local taxes or resort fees appear later, so check the total on the payment page against the per-night figure that attracted you.

Where the site is genuinely strong

We have spent most of this page on the cautions, so let us be fair about what this site is good at, because it is not nothing.

The ATOL licence is the headline, and it is real. Licence 11572 for flight-only sales puts Trip.com in a different category from the many international booking sites that sell UK customers flights with no UK financial protection at all. If you book a flight through it, that protection is a material benefit and you can check the number before you pay.

The refund route is the right one. Money goes back to the card you paid with, which is what you want.

The scale is the point of it. This is a genuinely global inventory, and it is strongest exactly where British agencies are weakest — domestic hotels across Asia, regional Chinese and Japanese cities, airlines that do not sell through European channels. For a trip across several Asian cities it will often show you rooms and routes that a UK-focused site does not have.

Member-only rates exist, unlocked by signing in, though we could not tie one to a specific hotel or quantify the discount, so treat it as worth a look rather than a reason to join.

Our overall read: use it for the inventory and the ATOL-covered flights, and apply the discipline above to the hotels. The problems we found are not dishonesty — they are the normal failure modes of a very large marketplace translated across dozens of countries. They are also entirely avoidable if you check the city, read the room’s own cancellation terms and compare the payment page against the headline.

Common questions

Is Trip.com ATOL protected?

For flight-only sales, yes. Its UK terms state “we provide full financial protection for our ATOL protected flight only sales” under Air Travel Organiser’s Licence number 11572, with Trip Air Ticketing (UK) Limited as the UK party. You can check that number on the Civil Aviation Authority’s register before booking. A hotel-only booking is not ATOL protected — the licence attaches to flight sales.

Which company am I booking with, and under what law?

The terms name Trip.com Travel Singapore Pte. Ltd., at 30 Raffles Place, #29-01, Singapore 048622, and state that they are “governed by and construed in accordance with the law of Singapore” with the Singapore courts having jurisdiction. Two things soften that: your mandatory consumer rights as a UK resident are preserved, and you keep the right to bring a claim in the courts of your own country instead.

Why are the London hotel prices so cheap?

Because some of them are not in London, England. On 8 October 2026 the page headed “Hotels in London” on the UK site offered us Motel 6 London KY from £51, Baymont by Wyndham London KY from £56, a Microtel at £53 and several other American roadside chains — that is London, Kentucky. Always check the city, region and country on the property page and look at the map before booking.

Who sets the cancellation policy?

The hotel or supplier, not Trip.com. The terms say to “check the relevant Supplier’s terms for information on your rights to change or cancel your booking”. That means two rooms in the same hotel can carry completely different terms, and the cheapest rate is usually the non-refundable one. Read the policy on the exact room on the payment page, and take a screenshot of it.

Do the prices include taxes and fees?

The terms do not say, and the hotel list we read made no statement about taxes or fees either. What the terms do promise for prepaid bookings is that “you will pay the exact amount shown on the payment page”, so the payment page is the figure that binds. Compare that total against the per-night price that attracted you before you confirm.

What is it actually best for?

Flights, because of the ATOL cover, and Asia, because the inventory is deepest there — domestic hotels and airlines across China, Japan and south-east Asia that UK-focused sites often do not sell at all. For a trip around several Asian cities it will show you options others do not have. For a hotel in a European city, you are not gaining much over a British booking site and you are taking on a Singapore contract to get it.

Our verdict

Trip.com earns a real mark in its favour that most global booking sites cannot claim: ATOL licence 11572 for flight-only sales, held through a named UK company, with a number you can check on the CAA register before you pay. For flights, and for Asian hotels and airlines that British agencies simply do not carry, it is a sensible place to look. The hotel side needs discipline. Your contract runs to a Singapore company under Singapore law, the cancellation terms belong to the hotel rather than the website, the terms never say whether taxes and fees are in the price, and when we searched its UK site for hotels in London it offered us London, Kentucky at £51 a night with one property priced two different ways on the same page. None of that is dishonest, and all of it is avoidable. Check the city, the region and the map on the property page; read the cancellation policy on the exact room on the payment page and screenshot it; compare the payment total against the per-night figure that caught your eye; and pay by credit card.
